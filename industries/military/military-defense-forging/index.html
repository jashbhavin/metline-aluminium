Aluminium Forgings for Military and Defence Programmes

Overview

Why defence parts are forged

A forging is shaped by compressive deformation of a hot billet. The process closes internal porosity, breaks up the cast structure and lines up the grain flow with the outline of the part. The result is metal with higher fatigue strength, better fracture toughness and more consistent properties in all directions than a part machined from plate or bar.

Those properties matter most where a failure would be catastrophic and loads are cyclic or impulsive: landing impacts, gun recoil, blast, vibration from tracks and propellers, and the acceleration of a missile at launch. Defence programmes therefore specify forgings for primary load paths, and they usually demand full traceability, ultrasonic inspection and properties verified in several grain directions.

Aluminium forgings are chosen where weight is critical and service temperatures are moderate. High-strength 7xxx alloys dominate airframe and vehicle structures; 2xxx alloys cover parts that run warm or must be welded; 6061 is used for medium-strength, corrosion-resistant fittings in ships, shelters and ground equipment.

Advantages of a forged part over machined plate

  • Grain flow follows the contour, improving fatigue life at fillets and lugs
  • Higher short-transverse properties and better resistance to stress-corrosion cracking
  • Near-net shape saves expensive alloy and machining time on large parts
  • Sound internal structure that passes demanding ultrasonic classes
Forging routes

Die, hand and rolled-ring forgings

Closed-die forgings

Formed between shaped dies to near-net shape. Suit repeat parts such as brackets, hubs, flanges, links and fittings. Tooling costs more, but machining and material are reduced and properties are repeatable from part to part.

Hand (open-die) forgings

Worked between flat or simple dies into blocks, bars, discs and stepped shafts. Ideal for prototypes, small batches and very large parts, and for blocks that will be machined into complex housings.

Rolled rings

A pierced preform is rolled into a seamless ring with grain flow running round the circumference. Used for turret races, missile body rings, flanges, bearing housings and shaft rings.

Forged bar and shafts

Solid, hollow or flanged shafts for drive lines, axles and actuator rods, with better fatigue properties than extruded bar.

Forged discs and blocks

Discs for wheels, covers and seeker housings; blocks for gearbox casings, gyro platforms and electronics enclosures.

Precision forgings

Close-tolerance forgings with thin webs and minimal draft, reducing machining on high-volume parts.

Where the old 7075-T6 temper is shown on legacy drawings, defence users increasingly specify T73 or T7352 for new parts because of stress-corrosion cracking in short-transverse loading. 7050 and 7085 extend high strength into thicker sections, where 7075 cannot be quenched fast enough to reach full properties.

Quality

Inspection, traceability and confidentiality

  • Traceability: heat, lot and serial records linking each forging to the cast, the forging operation, heat treatment and testing
  • Mechanical testing: tensile tests from prolongations or sacrificial parts in the directions the specification requires (L, LT, ST)
  • Ultrasonic inspection: to AMS-STD-2154 or the class on the drawing, usually Class A or AA for rotating and primary-structure parts
  • Other NDT: penetrant inspection of finished surfaces, conductivity checks on 7xxx tempers, and hardness surveys
  • Dimensional inspection: first-article inspection against the drawing, then sampling per the agreed plan
  • Certification: EN 10204 3.1 mill certificates; third-party or customer inspection before dispatch on request

Drawings and programme details shared with Metline are used only to prepare the quotation and fulfil the order, and we review non-disclosure agreements before drawings are exchanged. Exports of defence-related parts may need end-user statements or licences under the rules of the shipping and receiving countries; we plan that documentation with you at quotation stage.

Design & fabrication

Design and fabrication advice for forged parts

Choose the route early

Batch size, size and complexity decide between open-die blocks machined to shape and closed-die forgings. Small batches and prototypes usually start as hand forgings; the die forging follows once the design is frozen.

Mind grain direction

Mark the principal load directions on the drawing so the forging can be oriented to put the best properties where stresses are highest, and avoid short-transverse tension on 7xxx parts.

Quench and section size

Thick sections cool slowly and lose strength. Keep sections as uniform as practical, rough machine before heat treatment where allowed, and pick 7050 or 7085 for very thick parts.

Residual stress

T652, T7352 and T7452 tempers include compression stress relief, which reduces distortion when the part is machined. Specify them for heavily machined parts.

Surface protection

Anodising (sulphuric or chromic replacement processes), conversion coating and primer are standard for 2xxx and 7xxx defence parts. Specify the system on the drawing.

Machining condition

State whether you want as-forged, rough-machined or finish-machined parts, and mark critical datums and tolerances for first-article inspection.

Examples

Typical defence forging examples

Vehicle wheel hub. A typical wheeled armoured vehicle hub is closed-die forged in 6061-T6 or 7075-T73, ultrasonically inspected, then machined for bearings and studs. Forging cuts unsprung mass compared with a steel hub and gives better fatigue life than a part machined from plate.

Missile body ring. A body joint ring is usually a seamless rolled ring in 2219-T6 or 7075-T73, rough machined before heat treatment so that the thin final wall reaches full properties, then finish machined with threads or bolt circles.

Turret race ring. Large-diameter rings in 7xxx alloys are ring-rolled to a near-net profile, heat treated, stress-relieved and machined. Roundness and flatness tolerances drive the choice of stress-relieved temper.

Radar pedestal base. A thick hand-forged 6061 or 2219 block is machined into a stiff, stable base that must hold antenna alignment through vibration and temperature cycles.

FAQ

Military aluminium (aluminum) forgings: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium alloys are used most for military forgings?

7050 and 7075 for highly loaded airframe, vehicle and weapon parts; 2014 and 2219 for parts that need forgeability, weldability or warm strength; 2618 for elevated temperatures; and 6061 or 5083 for corrosion-resistant naval and ground-equipment fittings.

Should I specify 7075-T6 or 7075-T73 for a defence forging?

T6 is about 10 – 15 % stronger, but T73 has far better resistance to stress-corrosion cracking. For parts loaded in the short-transverse direction or used in humid and marine environments, T73 or T7352 is normally preferred.

Can you supply forgings to AMS, MIL, ASTM or GJB standards?

Yes. Forgings are supplied to the specification and revision named on your drawing or order, with EN 10204 3.1 certificates. Tell us any ultrasonic class or test direction so the documentation can be agreed before production.

Do you supply prototypes and small batches?

Yes. Prototype and small batches are usually made as hand forgings machined to shape, which avoids die costs; closed-die tooling can follow when quantities justify it.

Can forgings be supplied machined?

Forgings can be supplied as-forged, rough machined (common before heat treatment on thick parts) or finish machined to drawing, with surface treatment if required.

How do you handle confidential drawings?

Drawings are used only for quotation and production, and we review non-disclosure agreements on request before you share them. Export documentation is planned with you at quotation stage.
