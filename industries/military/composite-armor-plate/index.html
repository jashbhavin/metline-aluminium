Composite Aluminium Armour Plate: From Single Alloy to Multi-Ply

Aluminium armour has evolved through three generations of monolithic plate (weldable 5xxx, Al-Zn-Mg 7xxx and high-copper 2xxx) and now into roll-bonded laminates that stack hard and ductile 7xxx layers. This page explains how each type works, which alloys and specifications apply, and how armour plate is qualified and accepted, so you can match the material to your threat level and weight budget. Overview

Why aluminium is used for armour

Aluminium armour plate is not chosen because it stops more per millimetre than steel; it does not. It is chosen because, at equal weight, an aluminium plate is roughly three times thicker than rolled homogeneous steel armour, and that extra thickness makes the hull far stiffer, easier to weld into a self-supporting structure, and more effective against fragments and small-arms threats per kilogram.

That logic, first proven on tracked personnel carriers in the 1960s, still drives the use of aluminium in light armoured vehicles, amphibious craft, naval superstructures and add-on (appliqué) armour kits. A thick aluminium hull doubles as the vehicle structure, so fewer frames and stiffeners are needed and the empty weight falls. It also helps with air transportability and with mobility on soft ground and over bridges with weight limits.

Against these benefits sit three engineering limits. Aluminium loses strength in the weld heat-affected zone, the high-strength grades can be sensitive to stress-corrosion cracking, and a single homogeneous plate tends to fail by plugging or spalling once a projectile has enough energy. Each generation of armour alloy, and finally the multi-ply composite plate, was developed to push one of those limits back.

What drives the choice of armour plate

  • Threat: fragment simulating projectiles, ball or armour-piercing small-arms rounds, blast
  • Areal density budget (kg/m²) for the hull, floor, roof or appliqué panel
  • Whether the plate is part of a welded structure or bolted on as appliqué
  • Service environment: seawater, humidity and temperature affect stress-corrosion risk
  • Specification and acceptance testing demanded by the programme

5083 and 5456

The original weldable armour grades. Strength comes from magnesium in solution and cold work (H131), so welding does not destroy a heat treatment. Still the default for hulls that must be welded easily and survive salt water.

5059

A higher-magnesium alloy with zinc and zirconium. It is stronger than 5083 in comparable tempers, giving better ballistic protection at the same thickness while keeping 5xxx weldability.

7039

An Al-Zn-Mg alloy developed for better protection against armour-piercing rounds. It is weldable but must be designed and fabricated with stress-corrosion cracking in mind.

7A52 and 7B52

Weldable Al-Zn-Mg-Cu armour alloys widely used in light armoured vehicle hulls. 7A52 is relatively ductile; 7B52 is supplied as a laminated plate for floors and side armour.

2519-T87

Developed for the US Army as a weldable replacement for 5083 and 7039 on new vehicles. It gives higher ballistic resistance than 5083 and better stress-corrosion resistance than 7039.

2139 and 2195

Third-generation Al-Cu-Mg-Ag and Al-Cu-Li alloys with high strength and toughness. 2195 is lighter than conventional alloys because of its lithium content.

Multi-ply plate

How composite (multi-ply) aluminium armour works

A composite armour plate is a laminate of several aluminium alloys bonded into one plate, usually by hot roll bonding and less often by explosive welding. A typical build alternates a high-strength, hard 7xxx alloy (for example 7A62) with a more ductile 7xxx alloy (7A52), with a thin, soft bonding layer of a low-zinc cladding alloy such as 7072 or 7A01 at every interface.

The hard layer erodes and blunts the projectile. The ductile layers then stretch and bend over a wide area instead of shearing out a plug. Each interface can delaminate locally, which absorbs energy and deflects the crack path, so fewer fragments leave the rear face. Published light-gas-gun and simulation studies on 7A52/7A62 laminates report clearly higher energy absorption than a monolithic plate of the same mass, and show that the thickness ratio and stacking order of hard and ductile layers are the main design variables.

Related constructions

Foam-filled and honeycomb-backed aluminium armour

Aluminium foam layers

Closed-cell aluminium foam behind a hard face spreads blast and impact loads over time, cutting peak acceleration transmitted to the crew. It also adds acoustic damping. Foam is used under floors and in seats rather than as a stand-alone ballistic layer.

Honeycomb-backed panels

A dense armour face bonded to an aluminium honeycomb core gives a stiff, light panel for roofs, hatches and ship superstructures. The honeycomb crushes progressively under blast.

Ceramic-faced hybrids

For armour-piercing threats, ceramic tiles are often bonded to an aluminium backing plate. The aluminium catches the fractured core and ceramic debris; 5083, 6061 and 7xxx plate are used as backings.

These constructions are designed as systems, so the aluminium part is specified by the armour designer. Metline supplies the plate, honeycomb and foam materials to that specification; we do not design or certify the armour system itself.

Quality

How armour plate is qualified and accepted

Military armour plate specifications use two levels of testing. A first-article inspection qualifies the alloy, temper and producer for each thickness; lot conformance inspection then checks every production lot.

  • Full heat and lot traceability from casting through rolling and heat treatment
  • Third-party or customer inspection before dispatch on request
Design & fabrication

Cutting, welding and designing with armour plate

Cutting

Waterjet and abrasive cutting avoid heat damage at edges. Plasma and laser cutting are used on 5xxx plate, but the heat-affected edge on 7xxx and 2xxx should be machined back where the drawing specifies.

Welding 5xxx

MIG with ER5356 or ER5556 filler. Strength loss in the weld zone is small for H131 plate, which is why 5083 hulls are easy to build.

Welding 7xxx

ER5356 or ER5183 filler. Control heat input, avoid weld toes in high-stress areas and follow the programme rules on post-weld treatment to reduce stress-corrosion risk.

Laminates

Cut and bevel so interfaces are not exposed to welding heat at critical areas; seal exposed edges against moisture. Check bond integrity ultrasonically after forming.

Forming

5xxx armour bends readily; high-strength 7xxx and 2xxx need larger radii and are usually formed before final ageing where the specification allows.

Corrosion protection

Use chemical conversion coating, CARC or equivalent paint systems, and isolate steel fasteners to prevent galvanic attack.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) armour plate: frequently asked questions

Is composite aluminium armour better than a single-alloy plate?

At the same areal density, a well-designed laminate of hard and ductile 7xxx layers absorbs more energy and produces fewer rear-face fragments than a monolithic plate, because the interfaces delaminate and the ductile layers stretch. It costs more and is harder to weld, so monolithic 5083 or 7039 remains common for welded hull structure.

Which aluminium armour alloys can be welded?

5083, 5456 and 5059 weld easily. 7039, 7020, 7017 and 7A52 are weldable with care. 2519 is weldable to itself but not to 5xxx or 7xxx by conventional methods. 7075, 7085, 7056 and 2024 are treated as unweldable and are bolted on as appliqué.

What is a V50 ballistic test?

It is the velocity at which a given projectile has a 50 % chance of fully penetrating the plate, determined by firing a series of shots under MIL-STD-662. Armour specifications list minimum V50 values for each plate thickness and threat.

Why is 5083 still used if 7039 and 2519 give better protection?

5083 is cheap, widely available, resists seawater and welds with little strength loss. For amphibious and naval structures, and for hulls where thickness also provides stiffness, it remains the practical choice.

What thicknesses are composite armour plates made in?

Roll-bonded laminates are typically made from about 18 mm (five layers) up to 35 – 40 mm (nine layers). The number of layers and the hard-to-ductile ratio are tuned to the threat.

Yes. We source armour plate to the specification and revision you name and supply it with EN 10204 3.1 mill certificates. Ballistic and corrosion test reports are provided where the contract requires them; tell us the tests at enquiry stage.
