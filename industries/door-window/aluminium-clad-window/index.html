Timber-aluminium window systems

Aluminium Clad Wood Windows

An aluminium clad wood window has a solid or engineered timber frame on the room side and an extruded aluminium shell on the weather side, so you get the warmth and insulation of wood indoors and a coated metal skin that does not need repainting outdoors. It suits villas, hotels, heritage-style apartments and coastal homes where a plain aluminium frame feels too cold and an all-timber frame needs too much upkeep. Overview

What is an aluminium clad wood window?

A clad window is a timber window with a protective metal jacket. The timber does the structural and thermal work and gives the interior its look; the aluminium shell keeps rain, ultraviolet light and wind-driven grit away from the wood. Because the two materials each do the job they are best at, a well-made clad window can outlast both a painted timber window and a basic aluminium window in the same climate.

The idea came from northern Europe and North America, where timber windows were traditional but repainting the outside every five to eight years was expensive. Fixing a metal cap to the exterior faces removed most of that maintenance while keeping the timber inside. Modern systems use precision extrusions clipped to the timber with a ventilated gap behind them, so the wood can breathe and any moisture that gets behind the cladding drains away rather than soaking the frame.

Clad windows are sometimes confused with two related products. A wood-clad aluminium window is the reverse: an aluminium (often thermally broken) structural frame with timber trim fixed to the room side. A composite or hybrid window may combine timber, aluminium and a polymer or foam insulating core. All three aim to show wood indoors and metal outdoors, but they behave differently under load and heat, so it pays to know which one a quotation actually describes.

Why architects specify aluminium clad timber

  • Natural timber interior that can be stained, oiled or painted to match joinery and floors
  • Coated aluminium exterior that resists UV, salt air and rain without regular repainting
  • Timber conducts heat about a thousand times less than aluminium, so the frame stays warm to the touch
  • Exterior colour can be chosen independently of the interior finish
  • Narrow, crisp exterior sightlines that suit contemporary and heritage elevations alike
Construction

How an aluminium clad window is constructed

Every clad system has the same four layers, read from the room outwards. Understanding them helps you judge quality, because most failures in clad windows start where one layer meets the next.

LayerTypical materialWhat it doesWhat to check
Interior timber frame and sashFinger-jointed, laminated (glulam) pine, spruce, larch, oak, meranti or modified timberCarries the glass and hardware loads, provides insulation and the visible interior finishLaminated, kiln-dried stock at 10 – 14 % moisture; factory-applied finish on all faces
Glazing and sealsDouble or triple insulated glass units with warm-edge spacers; EPDM or TPE gasketsControls heat loss, solar gain, noise and air leakageGasket continuity at corners, drained glazing rebate, spacer type
Ventilation and drainage gapAir space of a few millimetres between timber and cladding, with drainage slotsLets any moisture behind the cladding drain and evaporate instead of being trapped against the woodThe gap must be open at the bottom and never sealed solid with silicone
Aluminium claddingExtruded 6063 or 6060 alloy, typically 1.2 – 2.0 mm wall, coatedWeather shell: sheds water, blocks UV, resists impact and gives the exterior colourExtruded rather than roll-formed sheet, mitred and corner-cleated or welded corners, coating standard

The cladding is fixed with plastic or nylon clips that slide into a groove in the extrusion and screw into the timber. Clips let the aluminium expand and contract independently of the wood: aluminium moves about 2.3 mm per metre for every 100 °C change in temperature, while timber hardly moves with heat but swells and shrinks with humidity. Rigid fixing of one to the other would force one material to crack or buckle over a few seasons.

Corners are where cheap cladding gives itself away. Quality systems use mitred extrusions joined with internal corner cleats, crimped or bonded, or factory-welded corners on sashes. Butt-jointed, roll-formed sheet with exposed sealant joints may look the same on delivery but tends to open up, admit water and stain the timber within a few years.

Roll-formed cladding is shaped from thin coated sheet, often around 0.6 – 0.8 mm. Extruded cladding is pushed through a die and is usually two or more times thicker, holds sharper profiles and resists denting far better. For long-life projects, specify extruded cladding.

Aluminium specification

Cladding alloys, tempers and surface finishes

Window cladding needs an alloy that extrudes into thin, detailed shapes with a smooth surface for coating. That is why almost every system uses the 6xxx series, and in particular 6063 and its European near-twin 6060. Strength requirements are modest because the timber carries the loads; what matters is surface quality, dimensional accuracy and coating adhesion.

Coating choice decides how the window looks after fifteen or twenty years in the sun, so match it to the exposure:

PVDF (fluorocarbon) coating

Resins with at least 70 % PVDF give the best colour and gloss retention. Tested to AAMA 2605 in North America and to the top class of Qualicoat or GSB in Europe. The right choice for coastal, high-altitude and tropical sites.

Polyester powder coating

The most common finish worldwide. Standard-durability powders meet Qualicoat class 1 or AAMA 2603/2604; super-durable powders (Qualicoat class 2) hold colour much longer in strong sunlight.

Anodising

An oxide layer grown from the metal itself in clear, bronze or black. Specify at least 20 µm (Qualanod class 20) for exterior use, and 25 µm near the sea.

Wood-grain finishes

Sublimation printing over powder coat reproduces oak or walnut grain on the exterior for heritage or rustic projects.

Textured and metallic powders

Fine textures hide handling marks and minor dents; metallic effects need clear topcoats outdoors.

Two-tone units

Because cladding and timber are finished separately, any RAL or custom colour outside can be paired with any stain or paint inside.

Interior timber

Wood species and interior finishes

The timber species affects cost, stability, colour and how well the frame takes stain. Engineered (laminated, finger-jointed) sections are now standard in good clad windows because they twist and check far less than solid boards.

Ask for timber from legally harvested, certified sources (FSC or PEFC) where your project or national rules require it, especially for public buildings and projects targeting green ratings. Interior finishes are normally water-based lacquers or hard-wax oils applied in the factory before glazing, so that every face of every component is sealed. Site-finished timber tends to leave end grain and rebates unprotected.

Interior colour options typically include clear lacquer, light and dark stains, white or RAL-matched opaque paint, and brushed or wire-textured surfaces for a more rustic look. Request a finished sample of both the timber and the cladding colour before approving production.

Opening styles

Aluminium clad window types

Almost every opening style can be built as a clad window. The table on the left of most system catalogues starts with these:

Casement

Side-hinged sash opening outward (common in the UK, Asia and North America) or inward (common in continental Europe). Compression seals give excellent air and water tightness.

Tilt-and-turn

Inward-opening sash that tilts at the top for secure ventilation or swings fully open for cleaning. The standard European clad window and ideal for high-rise residential.

Awning and top-hung

Hinged at the top, opening outward so the sash sheds rain while ventilating. Often combined with fixed lights below.

Double-hung and sash

Vertically sliding sashes for traditional elevations; clad versions replace old timber sash windows with far less maintenance.

Sliding and lift-and-slide

Horizontal sashes on tracks for wide openings; lift-and-slide hardware raises the sash off its seals to glide, then lowers it to seal tightly.

Fixed, picture and shaped

Non-opening lights in large sizes, plus arches, trapezoids and circles that are difficult in plain aluminium but natural in timber.

Bay and bow

Projecting assemblies of casement and fixed units joined by structural mullions, popular in residential renovation.

Clad wood doors

Entrance, French, folding and lift-and-slide doors built with the same timber-and-cladding principle, so doors and windows match.

Combination units

Transoms, sidelights and stacked fixed-over-opening assemblies coupled into one frame for a consistent sightline.

Thermal performance is where clad windows earn their price. Softwood has a thermal conductivity of roughly 0.13 W/m·K and 6063 aluminium roughly 200 W/m·K, so a timber frame core without a thermal break already insulates well. Most of the remaining heat loss is through the glass and its edge, which is why triple glazing with warm-edge spacers and argon fill takes a good clad window down towards 0.8 W/m²·K, a level required for passive-house projects in cold climates.

In short, choose a plain thermally broken aluminium window when you want the slimmest frames, the largest glass and a modern metal interior, or when the budget is tight. Choose aluminium clad wood when the interior design calls for real timber, when thermal comfort near the frame matters, or when the project replaces traditional timber windows and must keep their character. Maximum sash sizes are limited by glass weight and hardware rating rather than the timber. Triple glazing weighs about 30 kg/m² or more, so a large tilt-and-turn sash may need heavy-duty hinges or a change to a fixed light with a smaller opening vent. Design advice

Design, installation and when clad windows are not the right choice

Good practice

  • Specify extruded cladding with mitred, cleated corners and a drained, ventilated gap
  • Ask for factory-finished timber on every face, including rebates and end grain
  • Detail the window-to-wall joint with a vapour-tight membrane inside and a vapour-open, watertight membrane outside
  • Use a sill and sub-sill that throw water clear of the timber and the wall below
  • Choose stainless-steel or coated fixings; avoid bare carbon steel in contact with aluminium
  • Keep site silicone off the cladding drainage slots

Consider another frame when

  • The interior is permanently wet, such as pool halls and commercial kitchens
  • The design needs very slim frames with floor-to-ceiling glass on large spans
  • Fire rules require a fully non-combustible window assembly
  • The budget cannot carry the premium over aluminium or uPVC
  • Installation will happen before the building is weathertight and dry
  • There is no plan for occasional care of the interior timber finish

Timber reacts to its surroundings. Storing clad windows outdoors on site, or installing them into a building that is still drying out after wet plastering and screeding, can raise the moisture content of the frame and lift the interior finish. Keep units wrapped, raised off the ground and ventilated until the building is closed in.

In replacement work, measure the structural opening after removing old frames where possible. Clad windows can be supplied as full-frame replacements, with the opening stripped back to the masonry, or in some markets as insert units that sit inside a sound existing frame. Full-frame replacement allows the wall junction to be insulated and sealed properly and usually gives better long-term results.

Applications

Projects that suit aluminium clad wood windows

Villas and private residences

Wood interiors that match floors and joinery, with a durable coloured exterior on every elevation.

Apartments and renovation

Tilt-and-turn and casement units that bring comfort and acoustic performance to city flats, including replacement of old timber windows.

Hotels and resorts

Warm, natural interiors that support the guest experience, with low-maintenance exteriors near coasts and mountains.

Coastal and high-UV sites

PVDF or anodised cladding protects the timber from salt spray and intense sun.

Heritage and conservation areas

Timber profiles and glazing bars that respect traditional elevations while meeting modern energy rules.

Low-energy and passive houses

Triple-glazed clad windows reach very low Uw values with warm interior surfaces.

A typical villa package combines tilt-and-turn bedroom windows, a large fixed living-room light and a lift-and-slide door to the terrace, all in oak inside and a dark grey PVDF exterior. A typical apartment renovation uses pine-framed casements with white paint inside and a powder-coated exterior matched to the building colour, delivered floor by floor according to the window schedule.

Cost

What drives the cost of aluminium clad windows

  • Timber species and grade: laminated oak and modified timbers cost considerably more than pine or spruce
  • Glazing: triple glazing, laminated safety glass, acoustic interlayers and solar-control coatings add cost and weight
  • Opening type and hardware: tilt-and-turn and lift-and-slide hardware costs more than fixed lights or simple casements
  • Cladding specification: extruded profiles and PVDF coatings cost more than roll-formed sheet and standard powder but last longer
  • Size and shape: very large or shaped units need special glass and handling
  • Certification and testing: project-specific wind, water or security testing
  • Packing and freight: crated, corner-protected export packing and the shipping route to site

Compare quotations on a like-for-like basis: same Uw, same glass build-up, same cladding thickness and coating standard, same hardware brand class. Two windows that look identical in a brochure can differ widely in what sits behind the cladding.

FAQ

Aluminium clad wood windows: frequently asked questions

Are aluminium clad wood windows worth the extra cost?

They are worth it when you want a real timber interior with very little exterior maintenance, or when replacing traditional timber windows in a way that keeps their character. Over 25 – 30 years the avoided repainting and the better thermal comfort usually offset much of the price difference. If neither the wood interior nor the low upkeep matters to you, a thermally broken aluminium window is better value.

What is the difference between aluminum clad and aluminium clad windows?

None. "Aluminum clad" is the American spelling and "aluminium clad" the British and international spelling for the same product: a timber window with an aluminium exterior shell.

Does water get trapped between the wood and the aluminium cladding?

Not in a properly designed system. The cladding is held off the timber on clips with a ventilated, drained gap, so any water that gets behind it runs out through slots at the bottom. Problems arise when cladding is sealed tight to the wood or the drainage slots are blocked with sealant.

How long do aluminium clad windows last?

With extruded cladding, a durable coating and factory-finished timber, 40 years or more is a reasonable expectation. The interior timber finish may need light refreshing every 10 – 15 years in sunny rooms, and seals and hardware should be checked and adjusted periodically.

Which aluminium alloy is used for window cladding?

Usually 6063-T5 or 6060-T5/T6 extrusions, chosen for their smooth surface and excellent coating and anodising response. Heavier reinforcing sections in large doors may be 6063-T6 or 6005A-T6.

Can clad windows be used in hot, humid climates?

Yes, with the right choices: a stable laminated or modified timber, a fully sealed interior finish, a PVDF or anodised exterior, and installation only after the building interior is dry. In permanently wet interiors such as pool halls, an all-aluminium window is the safer option.
