Aluminium Window Profiles: A Complete Guide

Aluminium window profiles are extruded frame, sash, mullion, bead and sill sections, almost always in 6063 or 6060 alloy, that are cut and assembled into finished windows. The right profile combines adequate wall thickness and stiffness for the wind load, a thermal break sized for the energy target, and a durable anodised or powder-coated finish. Metline supplies window extrusions to drawing, with cutting, machining and finishing on request, to fabricators and system companies worldwide.

Overview

What an aluminium window profile has to do

A window frame is a small structure. It carries the weight of the glass, resists wind pressure and suction without deflecting enough to crack the glazing, keeps rain and air out at the seals, limits heat loss, and has to look the same after twenty or thirty years of sun and weather. Aluminium extrusions handle these demands well because the extrusion process can put material exactly where it is needed: chambers for stiffness, grooves for gaskets, screw ports for corner cleats, and slots for polyamide thermal-break strips, all in a single section.

Compared with timber or uPVC, an aluminium frame is far stiffer for its size. The elastic modulus of aluminium is about 69 GPa, roughly twenty times that of rigid PVC, so aluminium sections can be slimmer while carrying larger panes. That is why aluminium dominates large sliding doors, floor-to-ceiling glazing, commercial storefronts and curtain walls, and why it remains popular in residential windows where narrow sightlines are wanted.

The weakness of bare aluminium is heat flow. Its thermal conductivity is around 200 W/m·K, so a frame without a thermal break conducts heat freely and forms condensation in cold climates. Modern window systems solve this with an insulating barrier between the inner and outer aluminium shells, and much of the engineering in a window profile today goes into that barrier.

What to define before you order window profiles

  • The window type (casement, sliding, tilt-turn, fixed, awning, sash) and the system depth
  • Alloy and temper, usually 6063-T5 or T6, or 6060-T66 in Europe
  • Minimum wall thickness for the main load-bearing members
  • Thermal break type and the target frame or window U-value
  • Finish system, colour (RAL or sample), and the coating standard
  • Tolerance standard, lengths, packing and any machining or cutting
Profile types

Types of aluminium window profiles

A window system is a family of extrusions that fit together. Buyers usually meet the following groups, either as a full profile system or as individual sections made to a fabricator's own die drawings:

Non-insulated (standard) profiles

Single-shell sections without a thermal barrier. Lower cost and simpler to fabricate, they suit warm climates, interior partitions, garages, utility buildings and shading frames where heat loss is not a design driver.

Thermal-break profiles

Inner and outer aluminium shells joined by glass-fibre-reinforced polyamide strips or a poured polyurethane barrier. Standard for heated or air-conditioned buildings and required by most energy codes in cold and temperate regions.

Aluminium-clad timber profiles

A timber window with a thin aluminium cladding on the weather side, clipped on so the wood can move. The aluminium protects the timber and gives a low-maintenance exterior finish; see our aluminium-clad window page.

Frame (outer frame) profiles

The fixed perimeter section anchored to the wall opening. It carries the sash or fixed glass, holds the main weather seals and transfers wind load to the building.

Sash and vent profiles

The opening part of the window. Sash profiles are lighter than frames but must stay rigid enough to keep the seals compressed along their full length.

Mullions and transoms

Vertical and horizontal dividers that split large openings. They carry the highest bending loads in a window, so they are deeper, thicker or reinforced, sometimes in 6005A or 6061.

Sill profiles

External sills that shed water away from the wall, and internal or sub-sill sections that close the gap to the masonry and drain the frame.

Glazing beads

Snap-in sections that hold the glass unit in the frame. Bead geometry is matched to glass thickness, so a system offers several bead sizes for different glazing units.

Trims and adaptor profiles

Cover strips, extension profiles, coupling sections and flashing angles that finish the joint between window and wall, or join two windows together.

Wall thickness & stiffness

How thick should window profile walls be?

Wall thickness is the question buyers ask most, and the honest answer is that thickness alone does not make a good window. What matters is the stiffness of the whole section (its moment of inertia) against the wind load on the opening, plus enough metal in the right places for screws, cleats and hardware to hold.

ProfileTypical wall thicknessWhy
Residential window frames and sashes1.4 – 2.0 mmEnough for screw ports and hardware fixings at moderate wind loads
Commercial windows and large vents1.8 – 2.5 mmHigher wind pressures, heavier glass units and more frequent operation
Sliding door and lift-slide frames2.0 – 3.0 mmTrack loads from heavy sashes and wear at rollers
Mullions and transoms2.0 – 3.5 mm or reinforcedHighest bending moments; depth is often more effective than thickness
Glazing beads and trims1.0 – 1.4 mmNon-structural; must snap in without permanent deformation

Many national building codes set a minimum wall thickness for the main load-bearing members of windows and doors, and these minimums differ by country and change over time. Check the code that applies to your project and put the figure on the purchase order, together with the thickness tolerance (EN 12020-2 for precision 6060/6063 profiles, or ASTM B221 / ANSI H35.2 for US orders). A profile quoted as "2.0 mm" that arrives at the lower tolerance limit may not meet the code.

For tall or wide openings, deflection usually governs before stress. A common limit is span/175 or span/200 for mullions under design wind load, and some glass manufacturers set an absolute limit, such as 15 mm, to protect the edge seal of insulating glass. Increasing depth is the most efficient way to gain stiffness: doubling the depth of a box section raises its moment of inertia roughly eight times, while doubling the wall only doubles it. Where depth is fixed, steel or high-strength aluminium inserts are slid into the mullion chamber.

Energy performance

Thermal breaks, U-values and low-E glass

A thermal break interrupts the aluminium path between the inside and outside of the frame. Two technologies dominate:

Polyamide strip (crimped)

Glass-fibre-reinforced polyamide strips, typically PA66 with 25 % glass fibre (PA66 GF25), are rolled into knurled grooves in the two aluminium shells. The polyamide conducts around 0.3 W/m·K, several hundred times less than aluminium, and its thermal expansion is close to aluminium's. Strip depth ranges from about 14 mm to more than 40 mm; deeper strips and foam inserts give lower U-values. The composite is tested for shear and transverse strength under EN 14024.

Pour and debridge (PU)

A two-part polyurethane is poured into a channel in the profile, cures, and the aluminium bridge beneath it is then cut away. Common in North American systems. It is economical for high volumes but gives a narrower barrier than deep polyamide strips, so it is used more for moderate thermal targets.

PVC strips

Cheaper unreinforced PVC barriers exist, but PVC has lower heat resistance and a much higher expansion than aluminium. It softens at temperatures reached by powder curing and dark frames in sun, so glass-reinforced polyamide is the safer specification.

Thermal performance is expressed as a U-value in W/m²·K: the lower the number, the less heat passes through. The frame value (Uf) is calculated to EN ISO 10077-2; the whole-window value (Uw) combines frame, glass (Ug) and the linear loss at the glass edge (ψ) under EN ISO 10077-1. In North America the equivalent ratings are calculated and certified through NFRC procedures.

Frame constructionIndicative Uf (W/m²·K)Typical market
Non-insulated aluminium5.5 – 7.0Warm climates, interior and utility use

Indicative ranges only. The certified value depends on system depth, strip geometry, gaskets and glass rebate, and is published by the system designer for each section.

Glass covers most of the window area, so glazing choice matters as much as the frame. Low-emissivity (low-E) glass carries a microscopically thin metal-oxide coating that reflects long-wave heat back into the room while letting daylight through. A double-glazed unit with one low-E surface and argon fill reaches a Ug of about 1.0 – 1.1 W/m²·K, compared with about 2.8 for plain double glazing; triple glazing with two low-E coatings reaches 0.5 – 0.7. Solar-control low-E coatings also reduce cooling load in hot climates. Pair the glass with a "warm-edge" spacer to cut the edge loss that otherwise concentrates condensation at the bottom of the pane.

Dark colours absorb more solar heat, so in hot climates check that the thermal-break material and gaskets are rated for the frame temperatures reached.

Thermal-break profiles can be finished in two colours, for example anthracite outside and white inside, by coating the two shells separately before the polyamide strips are rolled in. Pretreatment matters as much as the topcoat: chromate-free conversion coatings are now normal, and for coastal sites a "Seaside" class pretreatment with deeper etching gives better resistance to filiform corrosion under the paint. Production & quality

How window profiles are produced and checked

  1. BilletHomogenised 6063 or 6060 billet is cast to EN 573-3 chemistry. Tight control of magnesium and silicon keeps strength and anodised colour consistent from batch to batch.
  2. ExtrusionThe billet is heated to roughly 450 – 500 °C and pushed through a steel die. Hollow window sections use porthole or bridge dies that split and re-weld the metal around a mandrel.
  3. Quench and stretchProfiles are cooled at the press exit (air or water mist for T5) and stretched slightly to straighten them and relieve stress.
  4. AgeingProfiles are artificially aged in an oven, typically several hours at about 175 – 200 °C, to reach T5, T6 or T66 strength.
  5. FinishingAnodising, powder or PVDF coating, or wood-grain transfer, followed by assembly of thermal-break strips where specified.
  6. InspectionDimensions against the drawing and tolerance standard, hardness or tensile tests, coating thickness, adhesion and gloss, and visual inspection of decorative faces.

Quality checks worth asking for on every order: a mill certificate (EN 10204 3.1) showing chemistry and mechanical results; dimensional reports for critical features such as gasket grooves, thermal-break slots and screw ports; coating thickness and adhesion results to the named standard; and, for thermal-break profiles, shear strength results to EN 14024. Webster hardness is a quick production check for 6063 temper; a reading in the low teens for T5 is a common acceptance level, but agree the figure with your supplier.

Secondary processing

Cutting, machining and assembly

Cutting to length

Profiles are supplied in mill lengths (often 5.8 or 6.0 m) or cut to size. Frames and sashes are mitre-cut at 45° on double-head saws; mullions are notched and end-milled to fit.

Drilling and milling

Drainage slots, hardware holes, lock pockets and handle bores are machined on copy routers or CNC profile centres. Machining before finishing avoids damaging the coating.

Punching

High-volume holes and slots are punched with dedicated tools, faster and cheaper than milling for repeated features.

Corner assembly

Most window corners are joined with aluminium corner cleats that are crimped or pinned into the chambers, with corner glue for a watertight joint. Welding is rare for coated profiles because heat damages the finish.

Bending

Arched and curved windows are roll-bent from straight profiles. Bending is easier in softer tempers and before coating, or uses profiles designed for bending.

Glazing and hardware

Gaskets, glazing beads, hinges, multipoint locks and handles are fitted last. Hardware from established suppliers is designed around specific profile grooves, so confirm compatibility early.

Metline can supply profiles cut to length and machined to drawing, with matching accessories, so the fabricator receives parts ready for assembly. Because tolerances on gasket and hardware grooves govern how well a window seals, send the hardware and gasket references with your enquiry.

Durability

Coastal projects and maintenance

Aluminium windows perform well near the sea if the finish and detailing are right. Salt deposits hold moisture on the surface, so the coating is the first line of defence and the aluminium alloy itself is the second.

  • Specify anodising of at least 25 µm, or a powder coating over a Seaside-class pretreatment, for sites within a few kilometres of the coast
  • Use stainless steel (A4 / 316) fixings and keep copper, brass and untreated carbon steel out of contact with the aluminium
  • Make sure drainage slots let water out of the frame chambers so salt water does not stand inside the profile
  • Seal cut ends and machined faces exposed after coating, or machine before finishing
  • Rinse frames with fresh water and a neutral detergent (pH about 5 – 8) every three to six months in coastal air, and at least once a year elsewhere
  • Avoid abrasive pads, solvents and strong acid or alkaline cleaners, which damage coatings and anodic films
  • Lubricate rollers, hinges and locking points once or twice a year and check gaskets for shrinkage

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) window profiles: frequently asked questions

Which aluminum alloy is best for window frames?

6063 is the standard choice worldwide, with 6060 widely used in Europe. Both extrude into thin, complex hollow sections with a fine surface that anodises and powder-coats well. 6005A is used for heavily loaded mullions and large sliding systems that need more strength.

What wall thickness should aluminium window profiles have?

Residential window frames and sashes are typically 1.4 – 2.0 mm, commercial windows 1.8 – 2.5 mm and sliding door frames 2.0 – 3.0 mm. The governing figure is the minimum set by your local building code and the stiffness needed for the design wind load, so state the minimum and the tolerance on the order.

What is the difference between 6063-T5 and 6063-T6 window profiles?

T5 profiles are cooled at the extrusion press and then aged; T6 profiles are fully solution heat-treated before ageing. T6 is about 20 – 25 % stronger by specified minimum (215 MPa versus 175 MPa tensile under EN 755-2). Most window sections are T5; T6 or T66 is used for larger spans.

What is a thermal break and do I need one?

A thermal break is an insulating barrier, usually glass-reinforced polyamide strips, that separates the inner and outer aluminium shells. It cuts heat flow through the frame by roughly half or more and prevents condensation on the inside. You need one for any heated or air-conditioned building in temperate or cold climates, and most energy codes require it.

Are aluminium windows suitable for coastal buildings?

Yes, provided the finish is specified for marine air: anodising of at least 25 µm or a powder coating over a Seaside-class pretreatment, stainless steel fixings, good drainage and regular rinsing with fresh water.

Can you match an existing window system or die drawing?

Yes. Send the die drawing or a sample section with the alloy, temper, finish and quantity. New dies are made to the drawing, and sample lengths are approved before production.

Do you ship window profiles internationally?

Yes. Metline supplies window extrusions worldwide from Mumbai, packed in export crates with interleaving or film protection and mill certificates, on EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP terms.
