Acoustic window systems

Aluminium Soundproof Window Systems

An aluminium soundproof window cuts outside noise by combining acoustic glass, a stiff thermally broken frame, compression seals and careful installation; thicker glass alone does very little. A good acoustic window reduces noise by about 38 – 45 dB (Rw), compared with roughly 30 dB for ordinary double glazing. Metline supplies acoustic window profiles, thermal-break sections and accessories to window fabricators for traffic, railway and airport noise projects worldwide.

Overview

How a soundproof window actually works

Sound reaches a room through a window in two ways: through the glass and frame as vibration, and through any air gap. The second route is the one most often overlooked. A gap of only a fraction of a percent of the window area can let through as much sound as the rest of the window combined, which is why an expensive acoustic glass in a poorly sealed sliding sash often disappoints.

A soundproof window therefore works as a system. The glass provides mass and damping. The frame and sash must be stiff enough to carry heavy glass without twisting, because a distorted sash no longer presses evenly on its seals. The seals and hardware close the air paths: multi-point locks pull the sash tight against two or three lines of EPDM gaskets. Finally, the installation joint between frame and wall must be filled and sealed, or noise simply goes around the window.

Aluminium is well suited to acoustic windows because its stiffness supports heavy laminated and triple glazing in slim, accurate sections, and modern thermally broken profiles with insulated chambers combine acoustic and thermal performance. The frame contributes little of the acoustic rating directly, but it decides whether the glass and seals can do their job.

Rules of thumb for acoustic windows

  • Airtightness first: any leak limits performance, whatever the glass
  • Use panes of different thickness so they do not resonate at the same frequency
  • Acoustic PVB laminated glass adds damping, mainly at mid and high frequencies
  • A wider cavity helps low frequencies; very wide gaps (secondary glazing) help most
  • Fixed and compression-sealed windows outperform sliders
Acoustic ratings

STC, OITC and Rw: how to specify acoustic performance

Acoustic performance is expressed as a single-number rating derived from laboratory measurements of sound transmission loss across a range of frequencies. Different regions use different ratings, and they are not interchangeable, so the specification must name the rating and the test standard.

RatingStandardFrequency rangeBest describesUsed in
RwISO 717-1 (tests to ISO 10140)100 – 3150 HzGeneral airborne sound, voices, mixed noiseEurope, UK, Australia, Asia, Middle East
Rw + CtrISO 717-1As Rw, weighted to low-frequency urban traffic spectrumRoad traffic, low-frequency rumbleFacades facing roads, rail and aircraft
Rw + CISO 717-1As Rw, weighted to pink noiseSpeech, TV, rail at speedEurope
STCASTM E413 (tests to ASTM E90)125 – 4000 HzSpeech and mid-frequency noiseUnited States, Canada
OITCASTM E133280 – 4000 HzOutdoor transport noise: traffic, rail, aircraftUS façade acoustics

Because Ctr is usually between −3 and −8 dB for glazing, a window rated Rw 40 might only achieve about 34 – 35 dB against traffic noise (Rw + Ctr). OITC values for the same window are typically several points below its STC. For street-facing bedrooms, specify Rw + Ctr or OITC rather than Rw or STC alone.

Decibels are logarithmic: a 10 dB reduction is perceived as roughly halving loudness, and a 3 dB change is just noticeable. Moving from ordinary double glazing (about Rw 30) to a good acoustic window (about Rw 40) makes traffic noise sound roughly half as loud.

Glazing

Glass build-ups and typical performance

Three mechanisms drive glass performance: mass (heavier glass blocks more, especially at low frequency), asymmetry (panes of different thickness have different coincidence frequencies, so one covers the other's weak spot) and damping (an acoustic PVB interlayer absorbs vibration energy). Cavity width helps too, but a normal 12 – 20 mm cavity has a mass-air-mass resonance in the low-frequency range that limits traffic-noise performance.

Glass build-upIndicative RwIndicative Rw + CtrComment
4 / 16 / 4 mm double glazing30 – 32 dB26 – 28 dBStandard thermal unit; poor acoustic choice
4 / 12 / 6 mm asymmetric33 – 34 dB29 – 30 dBCheap improvement from asymmetry
6 / 16 / 10 mm asymmetric36 – 38 dB32 – 34 dBGood value for moderate traffic noise
8 / 20 / 8.8 mm laminated37 – 39 dB33 – 35 dBHeavier; laminate adds security
10 / 20 / 8.8 mm acoustic laminated40 – 42 dB35 – 38 dBStrong performance for busy roads
Acoustic laminated both panes, e.g. 10.8 / 20 / 8.842 – 46 dB37 – 41 dBRail, airports, major roads
Primary IGU + secondary pane, 100 – 200 mm gap45 – 55 dB40 – 48 dBHighest performance; two separate frames

Glass-only indicative values from published manufacturer data ranges. A complete window usually rates 1 – 4 dB below its glass because of the frame and seals; always ask for a whole-window test report to ISO 10140 or ASTM E90.

Low-E coatings and argon fill do not improve acoustic performance on their own; they are added for energy efficiency and can be combined with any acoustic build-up. Triple glazing is not automatically quieter than a well-designed asymmetric laminated double unit, because its two narrow cavities each have their own resonance.

Typical indoor targets help set the window rating. The UK guidance in BS 8233, for example, suggests about 30 dB LAeq at night in bedrooms and 35 dB by day in living rooms. If outdoor night-time noise at the façade is 70 dB, the window must remove around 40 dB of traffic-weighted noise, which points to an acoustic laminated build-up with excellent sealing.

Window types

Which window type performs best acoustically?

Fixed windows

No opening joint, so the highest airtightness and acoustic performance. Ideal for façades where ventilation is provided mechanically. See fixed windows.

Casement windows

Compression closing onto two or three gasket lines with multi-point locks gives stable, high performance. The mainstream choice for acoustic residential and hotel projects. See casement windows.

Tilt-turn windows

Compression sealing like a casement, plus a secure tilt position for ventilation. Common in European apartments, schools and hospitals.

Awning windows

Top-hinged and compression sealed; good acoustic results in smaller openings and as vents above fixed lights. See awning windows.

Sliding windows

Brush and fin seals cannot compress, so performance is limited, usually to around 30 – 35 dB even with good glass. Use lift-and-slide or compression sliders where a sliding action is essential. See sliding windows.

Secondary glazing

A second window fitted inside the first with a wide air gap. The most effective upgrade for existing or heritage buildings and airport zones.

Most acoustic shortfalls trace back not to the glass but to the details: an untested sliding sash, an open trickle vent, foam left unsealed at the reveal or a roller-shutter box with a thin cover. A small leak can halve the benefit of the acoustic glass, so these details deserve as much attention as the glass build-up.

Avoiding problems

Common mistakes in acoustic window projects

Specifying Rw for traffic

A window chosen on Rw alone can fall well short against road noise, because the Ctr correction for low frequencies is often 5 dB or more. Use Rw + Ctr or OITC on traffic-facing façades.

Thicker symmetric glass

Two equal panes resonate together. Changing 6/16/6 to 8/16/8 adds weight but little benefit; 6/16/10 or a laminated pane usually gains more.

Ignoring ventilation

If occupants must open the window to get fresh air, the acoustic rating is irrelevant at night. Plan acoustic trickle vents or mechanical ventilation from the start.

Testing the glass, not the window

Glass data sheets describe a pane in a laboratory frame. Ask for whole-window tests, at a size close to what will be installed.

Weak installation joints

Unsealed foam, gaps behind trims and hollow shutter boxes create flanking paths that bypass the window altogether.

Oversized opening sashes

Very large sashes with heavy glass deflect and lose seal pressure over time. Split large openings into fixed lights with smaller opening vents.

Typical projects

Where acoustic aluminium windows are used

  • Hotels and serviced apartments: casement or fixed windows with acoustic laminated asymmetric units on road- or airport-facing elevations, and standard units on quiet sides to control cost.
  • Roadside apartments: bedrooms on the street side with tilt-turn windows and acoustic trickle vents, living rooms with fixed lights and casement vents.
  • Schools and libraries: classroom windows rated to keep internal noise low enough for teaching, often with mechanical ventilation.
  • Hospitals and care homes: patient rooms where quiet sleep matters, using compression-sealed windows and laminated glass for safety.
  • Offices and meeting rooms: fixed curtain wall or window wall glazing with acoustic laminate on noisy frontages.
  • Homes near rail lines or airports: secondary glazing or double-frame systems in noise-insulation schemes.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) soundproof windows: FAQ

How much noise do soundproof windows reduce?

A good acoustic window with laminated asymmetric glass and compression seals typically achieves Rw 38 – 45 dB, against about 30 dB for standard double glazing. Secondary glazing systems with a wide air gap can reach 45 – 55 dB.

Is triple glazing better for soundproofing than double glazing?

Not necessarily. Triple glazing improves thermal performance, but its narrow cavities limit acoustic gains. An asymmetric double unit with acoustic laminated glass and a wider cavity often performs as well or better for traffic noise.

Can sliding windows be soundproof?

Only to a degree. Standard sliders rely on brush seals that cannot be compressed, so they usually reach around 30 – 35 dB. Where high acoustic performance is needed, casement, tilt-turn or fixed windows are recommended.

What rating should I specify for traffic noise?

Specify Rw + Ctr (or OITC in the US), because these weight the low-frequency rumble of traffic. A busy urban road often needs Rw + Ctr of 35 dB or more for bedrooms.

Why do windows with similar glass test so differently?

Because the frame, seals, hardware and installation decide how much sound leaks around the glass. A stiff, airtight frame with continuous gaskets and multi-point locks preserves the glass rating; a weak or leaky one can lose several decibels.

Can you quote without a defined acoustic target?

Yes. Describe the noise source, the building use and approximate sizes, and we will suggest glass build-ups and profile series, which can then be confirmed against an acoustic consultant's report.
