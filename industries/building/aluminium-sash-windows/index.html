Aluminium Sash Windows

An aluminium sash window has one or two glazed sashes that slide up and down within the frame, balanced by springs so they stay where they are left. Built from thermally broken 6063 extrusions, it keeps the proportions of a traditional sash window while cutting maintenance and improving insulation. Metline supplies single hung, double hung and vertical sliding sash window profiles, thermal-break sections and balances to window fabricators for renovation and new-build projects worldwide.

Overview

What is an aluminium sash window?

The sash window is one of the oldest opening-window designs: two framed panes (sashes) in a box frame, at least one of which slides vertically. Traditional timber versions used cords, pulleys and cast-iron weights hidden in the frame. Modern aluminium versions replace the weights with compact spring balances in the jamb, which allows a slimmer frame.

Because a sash slides within its own plane, it never swings into a room or out over a walkway. That makes sash windows a good fit for narrow rooms, for windows facing balconies or pavements, and for tall, narrow openings where a casement sash would be heavy and awkward. Opening the top and bottom sashes together also sets up natural stack ventilation: warm air leaves at the top while cooler air enters at the bottom.

Aluminium suits the format well. Extrusions can be shaped with deep, slim meeting rails, integral horns and glazing beads that echo timber mouldings, and a durable powder coat or wood-grain finish removes the need for the repainting and repair that timber sashes demand.

Why specify aluminium sash windows

  • Traditional proportions with far less maintenance than timber
  • Slimmer frames than uPVC for the same sash size, so more glass and daylight
  • Thermal-break profiles and Low-E glazing for modern energy targets
  • Stable colour from powder coat, PVDF or anodised finishes
  • Consistent dimensions and finish across large window schedules

Single hung windows have fewer moving parts and a fixed top sash that seals well, so they are cheaper and slightly more airtight. Double hung windows give flexible ventilation and, with tilt-in sashes, let occupants clean both sides of the glass from inside, which is the deciding factor for apartments and upper floors. The thermal break is the single biggest factor in the frame's insulation. Aluminium conducts heat several hundred times better than polyamide, so separating the inner and outer extrusions with polyamide strips stops the frame acting as a cold bridge. Wider strips and foam-filled cavities lower the frame U-value further. In cold climates, unbroken aluminium frames are prone to condensation on the inner face and rarely meet energy codes.

The 6063 alloy is used because it extrudes cleanly into thin, complex hollow sections with a fine surface for coating, and its T5 or T6 temper gives enough stiffness for sash frames. See our 6063 extrusions page for chemistry and properties.

Appearance

Keeping a traditional sash appearance

Sash horns

Small projections below the top sash stiles, originally added to strengthen joints in timber sashes. Aluminium systems offer clip-on or integral horns for period façades.

Georgian bars

Glazing bars that divide each sash into smaller panes. Bars bonded to both faces of the glass with a spacer bar between give the look of true divided panes with full insulated glazing.

Slim meeting rail

The horizontal rail where the two sashes meet. A slim, deep rail is the key detail that makes an aluminium sash look like timber.

Heritage colours

Off-whites, greys, greens and black in matt or satin finishes match traditional painted joinery.

Wood-effect finish

Wood-grain transfer gives a timber appearance inside or out without timber maintenance.

Proportions

Frame and sash widths are adjusted to the original opening proportions, which matters in conservation areas.

In conservation areas and on listed buildings, replacing timber sashes with any other material may need consent. Many planning authorities will consider aluminium where the sightlines, glazing bars and meeting rail match the original closely, so detailed drawings and a sample are worth preparing before applying.

Glass and hardware

Glazing and balance systems

Glazing optionWhat it doesTypical use
Double Low-E IGU, argon filled, warm-edge spacerLowers heat loss; standard for energy-efficient windowsMost housing
Triple IGUFurther reduces heat loss; heavier sashesCold climates, passive designs
Toughened (tempered) glassSafety glazing that breaks into small piecesLow-level panes and areas near doors
Laminated glassHolds together when broken; improves security and sound insulationGround floors, schools, upper floors
Acoustic laminated glassSpecial interlayer damps soundRoadside, rail and airport-noise sites

Glass weight drives the balance choice. A double-glazed sash of 1 m × 0.8 m with two 4 mm panes weighs about 16 kg for the glass alone; triple or laminated glazing can make it half as heavy again. The balance must be rated for the full sash weight or the sash will drift down or be hard to lift.

Spiral balances

A twisted rod inside a tube in each jamb. Compact and economical for light to medium sashes.

Block-and-tackle balances

A spring and pulley set in a channel; carries heavier sashes and allows tilt-in operation.

Constant-force coil springs

Flat coiled springs give the same support at any height; good for heavy or tall sashes.

Locks and restrictors

Cam or sash locks on the meeting rail, plus ventilation stops or restrictors on upper floors and in schools.

Applications

Where aluminium sash windows are used

  • Renovation and period façades: replacing tired timber sashes while keeping horns, bars and proportions.
  • Apartments and villas: tilt-in double hung windows that occupants can clean from inside.
  • Upper floors and high-rise housing: no outward swing over walkways, with restrictors for child safety.
  • Hotels, schools and public buildings: controlled ventilation openings and laminated or acoustic glazing.
  • Coastal and exposed sites: marine-grade pre-treatment, PVDF or anodised finishes and stainless hardware.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) sash windows: FAQ

What is the difference between single hung and double hung windows?

In a single hung window only the bottom sash moves; in a double hung window both sashes slide, and they often tilt inwards for cleaning. Single hung is simpler and cheaper; double hung gives better ventilation control and easier cleaning on upper floors.

How do sash windows differ from horizontal sliding windows?

Sash windows slide vertically and suit tall openings and traditional proportions. Horizontal sliders move left and right and suit wide, low openings.

Can aluminium sash windows use Low-E or acoustic glass?

Yes. Double or triple Low-E units, toughened, laminated and acoustic laminated glass can all be used. The heavier the glass, the stronger the balance system must be, so glass weight is checked against the balance rating.

Are aluminium sash windows energy efficient?

With a polyamide thermal break, warm-edge spacers and Low-E glazing, typical whole-window U-values are about 1.4 – 1.8 W/m²·K, enough to meet many building codes for replacement windows. Non-thermal frames are only suitable for mild climates.

Can aluminium sash windows replace timber sashes in a conservation area?

Often, if they match the original sightlines, meeting rail, horns and glazing bars closely. Consent rules vary, so prepare drawings and a sample for the planning authority.

What balance system do aluminium sash windows use?

Spiral balances for light sashes, block-and-tackle balances for heavier and tilt-in sashes, and constant-force coil springs for very heavy or tall sashes.
