Aluminium Casement Window Systems

An aluminium casement window is a hinged sash that closes against compressed gaskets, which is why it outperforms sliding windows on air tightness, water tightness and sound reduction. Performance depends on the system depth, the thermal break, the glazing and the hardware working together. Metline supplies casement window profiles in 6063 and 6060, thermal break sections, sheet and accessories, to window fabricators, system companies and project buyers worldwide.

Overview

What makes a casement window different

A casement sash swings on hinges at its side (or, for a tilt-turn, on a combined hinge and stay). When the handle is turned, locking cams pull the sash tight against one or more continuous gaskets. That compression seal is the reason casements are the default choice where a building must meet strict energy, acoustic or weather targets.

A sliding window relies on brush seals that must let the sash move, so some air always passes. A casement has no moving seal in the closed position: the gasket is squeezed, not wiped. In laboratory tests to EN 12207, well-built aluminium casements routinely reach air permeability class 4, the highest class, while most sliders sit in classes 2 or 3. The same principle helps watertightness and acoustic insulation, because sound and wind-driven rain both follow air paths.

Aluminium suits the casement format because extruded profiles are stiff, dimensionally stable and can carry large insulated glass units on narrow sightlines. With a polyamide thermal break between the inner and outer shells, a modern aluminium casement can reach whole-window U-values that meet current European, UK and Australian energy rules, while needing far less maintenance than timber and staying rigid in sizes where uPVC needs steel reinforcement.

Where aluminium casements earn their place

  • Projects with air-tightness, U-value or acoustic targets written into the specification
  • High-rise and coastal buildings that face strong wind and driving rain
  • Facades that need slim, consistent sightlines and durable colour
  • Refurbishments replacing timber or steel casements with a similar look
Configurations

Casement window types and when to use them

Outward-opening casement

The traditional format in the UK, Australia, India and much of Asia. Wind pressure pushes the sash onto its seals, which helps weather performance, and the sash never intrudes into the room. Friction stays or restrictors control the opening.

Inward-opening casement

Common in continental Europe and high-rise housing, because the glass can be cleaned from inside and the sash is not exposed to wind when open. Needs a well-drained sill, since rain on the closed sash is driven towards the inner seal.

Tilt-turn

One handle gives two actions: tilt for secure, draught-free ventilation, turn for full opening and cleaning. Standard in Germany and widely specified for apartments, schools and hotels where safe night ventilation matters.

Flush casement

The sash sits in the same plane as the frame rather than lipping over it. Popular for UK refurbishment and contemporary villas because it echoes traditional timber casements.

Slim-frame casement

Narrow face widths that maximise glass area. Strength comes from deeper sections and thicker walls instead of wider ones, so check glass weight and sash size limits.

Fixed + casement combinations

A fixed light with one or more opening sashes, joined by mullions and transoms. Gives daylight and views with a controlled opening ratio, common on offices and apartment facades.

System selection

Choosing a system depth by performance target

Once the opening type is fixed, the frame depth decides how much insulation, how heavy a glass unit and what wind load the window can take. Deeper systems allow wider thermal break strips, triple glazing and stiffer profiles. The ranges below are typical of the market, not a single manufacturer; every system has its own tested limits.

Profile wall thickness in casement systems is typically 1.4 – 2.0 mm. Many specifications ask for at least 1.4 mm on main frame walls; heavier sashes and high wind loads may call for 1.8 – 2.0 mm. The governing values are those in the system test report.

Material

Which aluminium alloys are used in casement windows

Almost all window profiles are extruded from 6xxx-series alloys, which flow well through complex dies, harden by ageing and take an even anodised or powder-coated finish. The choice between them is mostly about surface quality versus strength.

Engineering

How a casement window achieves its performance

Thermal break

Glass-fibre-reinforced polyamide strips (typically PA66 GF25) are rolled into the profile to join the inner and outer aluminium shells. The strip interrupts heat flow; foam or extra cavities inside it reduce radiation and convection. Its shear strength is tested to EN 14024 so the composite profile still acts structurally.

Compression gaskets

EPDM gaskets in the frame and sash form two or three sealing lines. The centre (or "middle") gasket divides a drained outer chamber from a dry inner chamber, the core of the rain-screen principle used in good casements.

Multi-point locking

Espagnolette or gear-driven locks engage several keeps around the sash. More points give even gasket compression, better resistance to wind suction and better security. Larger sashes need more points.

Drainage

Water that passes the outer seal collects in the frame chamber and leaves through slotted drainage holes, often with baffled covers to stop wind blowing it back. Pressure-equalised chambers make drainage work under storm conditions.

Corner joints

Mitred corners are joined with crimped or pinned aluminium cleats and structural adhesive, then sealed. Joint quality decides long-term squareness and whether the gasket line stays continuous at the corners.

Glazing

Glass sits on setting blocks, held by snap-in beads with inner and outer gaskets. Correct toe-and-heel packing transfers glass weight to the hinges so the sash does not drop over time.

Application

Matching the casement to the project

  • Residential and villas: outward or flush casements with double Low-E glazing, insect screens and a standard thermal break are usually enough; focus on hardware durability and a stable colour.
  • Apartments and hotels: tilt-turn or inward casements with restrictors, acoustic laminated glass towards roads and consistent batch colour across many floors.
  • High-rise towers: high-performance systems with tested wind classes, deeper mullions in 6063-T6 or 6005A-T6 and careful drainage design.
  • Coastal sites: marine-grade finishes, stainless-steel or coated hardware, sealed cleat joints and laminated glass for impact resistance.
  • Refurbishment: flush or slim casements that copy the sightlines of the windows being replaced, with frames sized to the existing openings.
  • Schools and public buildings: robust hinges, restrictors, safety glazing and tilt ventilation for occupied rooms.

A typical apartment block specification might call for 70 mm thermally broken tilt-turn sashes, 6-16-6 Low-E argon glazing, air permeability class 4, watertightness class 7A or better and wind class C4 or C5, powder coated in a super-durable grey. Each of those numbers then has to be shown in a test report for the chosen system.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) casement windows: frequently asked questions

Should I choose a casement or a sliding window?

Choose a casement when air tightness, water tightness, acoustic insulation or a low U-value is a priority, because the sash closes onto compressed gaskets. Choose a slider where the opening must not project, for very wide openings, or where cost is the main driver and performance targets are modest.

What decides the U-value of an aluminium casement window?

The glass unit has the largest effect, followed by the thermal break width and design, the frame face width and the spacer bar. The whole-window value is calculated to EN ISO 10077 or NFRC 100 for a stated size, so compare values for the same reference size.

Are aluminium casements suitable for coastal or high-wind sites?

Yes, if the system has a tested wind class to suit the design pressure, the profiles and mullions are sized for the load, and the finish and hardware are specified for marine exposure: super-durable powder, PVDF or thick anodising with stainless or coated fittings.

Which aluminium alloy are casement window profiles made from?

Usually 6063-T5 or T6 (aluminum 6063 in US terms) or the European 6060-T66. Heavily loaded mullions may use 6063-T6 or 6005A-T6. These alloys extrude into complex sections and give a smooth surface for coating.

Where are tilt-turn windows the best choice?

Apartments, schools, hotels and high-rise housing: the tilt position gives secure ventilation without a large opening, and the turn position lets occupants clean the outside glass from inside.

What do you need to quote casement window profiles?

The window schedule or drawings with opening sizes and quantities, opening type, performance targets (U-value, wind load, air and water classes, acoustics), glass and hardware preferences, colour and finish, and the delivery destination.

Do you supply complete casement windows?

No. Metline supplies casement profiles in mill finish, anodised or powder coated, in stock lengths or cut to size, with accessories; your fabricator assembles and glazes the windows. Export packing and mill certificates are included.

Planning casement windows for a project?

Send the window schedule, performance targets, finish and destination. Our team will propose a profile series and quote profiles and accessories.
