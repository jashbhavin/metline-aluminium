Thermal Break Aluminium Windows

A thermal break aluminium window has its frame split into an inner and an outer aluminium shell, joined by a low-conductivity insulating barrier, usually glass-fibre-reinforced polyamide. Because aluminium conducts heat roughly 700 times faster than polyamide, that barrier cuts heat flow through the frame several-fold and keeps the inside face warm enough to resist condensation. Metline supplies thermally broken window profiles in 6063 and 6060, with gaskets and accessories, to window fabricators for buildings worldwide.

Overview

What is a thermal break aluminium window?

Aluminium is an excellent structural material for windows: stiff, light, durable and easy to extrude into precise sections. Its weakness is thermal conductivity. A 6063 extrusion conducts heat at around 200 W/m·K, so a solid aluminium frame acts as a direct bridge between a cold outside and a warm room. A thermal break interrupts that bridge.

In a thermally broken profile, the outer shell faces the weather and the inner shell faces the room. They are held together only by the insulating barrier, which is designed to carry structural loads (wind pressure on the glass, sash weight, hardware forces) while conducting very little heat. Glass-fibre-reinforced polyamide 66 conducts about 0.3 W/m·K; polyurethane barriers are lower still.

The effect is large. A non-insulated aluminium frame typically has a frame U-value (Uf) somewhere above 5 W/m²·K. A frame with a standard 20 mm polyamide break typically falls to about 2.5 – 3.0 W/m²·K, and a deep high-insulation frame with a 30 – 40 mm shaped strip and foam inserts can reach 1.0 – 1.6 W/m²·K. Combined with modern insulating glass, that puts aluminium windows within current energy limits in Europe, the UK, North America, Australia and New Zealand.

What a thermal break does, and does not do

  • It reduces heat loss through the frame in cold weather and heat gain in hot weather
  • It raises the inside surface temperature of the frame, which lowers condensation risk
  • It does not insulate the glass: the glazing unit still sets most of the window's performance
  • It cannot prevent condensation in every condition; high indoor humidity and poor ventilation still cause it
Construction

How the thermal break is built into the profile

Two manufacturing methods are used worldwide. Both produce a composite profile whose structural performance is tested before it goes into a window.

AspectPolyamide strip (insert and roll)Pour and debridge
ProcessThe two aluminium shells are extruded separately. Liquid two-part polyurethane is poured into the channel and cured, then the aluminium bridge under it is cut away

Strip-type barriers also allow the inner and outer shells to be finished in different colours before assembly, for example anthracite outside and white inside. Because the strips are crimped mechanically, the quality of knurling and rolling is critical: a poorly rolled profile can slip under load or temperature cycling. That is why EN 14024 defines shear strength, transverse tensile strength and elasticity values for the composite, tested at low, room and high temperatures and after ageing.

Strip shape

Simple I-shaped strips are cheapest. Shaped strips with extra chambers or flags block convection and radiation inside the frame cavity, and give a lower U-value at the same depth.

Foam inserts

Closed-cell foam strips or co-extruded foam fill the cavity between the shells and reduce convection. They are a common route from standard to high-insulation performance.

Dark colours

Dark frames in sun can exceed 70 °C on the outer shell. Use strips rated for the temperature and allow for differential expansion of inner and outer shells on long frames.

Frame U-values are calculated to EN ISO 10077-2 or simulated in THERM for NFRC; always compare whole-window values (Uw) at the same reference size.

Performance

Why the thermal break matters: energy, condensation and comfort

Energy

Frames make up 15 – 30 % of a typical window area. Without a break, that area can lose more heat than the glass beside it. Energy codes now set whole-window U-value limits that a non-insulated aluminium frame cannot meet with ordinary glass.

Condensation

Water condenses on any surface colder than the room's dew point. With 20 °C and 50 % relative humidity indoors, the dew point is about 9 °C. A non-insulated frame in freezing weather can fall well below that; a broken frame stays several degrees warmer.

Comfort

Cold inner surfaces cause draughts and radiant discomfort near windows. A warmer frame and glass edge make seating next to the window comfortable in winter.

Hot climates

In the Gulf, India, Southeast Asia and northern Australia, the break limits heat flow into air-conditioned rooms and reduces cooling load, especially with solar-control Low-E glass.

Acoustics

The break itself adds little sound reduction, but thermally broken systems normally come with multiple gaskets and accept thicker acoustic glass, which together improve sound insulation.

Durability

Less condensation means less staining, mould and damage to reveals, sills and finishes around the window.

Condensation performance is often specified separately. In Europe it is expressed through the temperature factor fRsi of the inner surface; in North America through the AAMA 1503 condensation resistance factor (CRF) or the NFRC 500 condensation resistance rating. Ask for these values if the building has humid rooms, swimming pools or a cold-climate location.

Choose a non-insulated frame only when the climate is mild year-round, the building is unconditioned, or the window is internal. For most occupied buildings, the extra cost of a thermal break is recovered through energy savings, lower condensation damage and compliance with building regulations.

Window types

Thermal break window types

Casement

Inward or outward hinged sashes closing onto compressed gaskets. The strongest air, water and acoustic performance; available from standard to very high insulation depths.

Tilt-turn

Inward-opening sash with tilt ventilation and turn-to-clean. Common in European-style housing, schools and hotels, usually in 70 mm and deeper systems.

Sliding and lift-slide

Space-saving for balconies and wide openings. Thermally broken interlocks and lift-slide gaskets are essential for good insulation in sliders.

Fixed

Picture windows and fixed lights with the smallest frame area per square metre of glass, so the best U-value for their size.

Awning

Top-hung sashes for kitchens, bathrooms and corridors; shed rain when open.

Large custom windows

Big fixed or combined units with reinforced mullions, sized to wind load, glass weight and transport limits.

Glazing

Glass options and how they combine with the frame

The glazing unit usually covers 70 – 85 % of the window, so it sets most of the thermal result. The table gives typical centre-of-glass values (Ug) for common builds; exact figures depend on coating, gas and cavity width.

Glass build-upTypical Ug (W/m²·K)Where it fits
Single 6 mmabout 5.7Non-insulated or internal windows only
Double 4-16-4, air, uncoatedabout 2.7Not usually enough for energy codes
Double Low-E, argon, 16 mm cavityabout 1.0 – 1.1Standard energy-efficient specification
Double solar-control Low-Eabout 1.0 – 1.6, low g-valueHot climates and glazed offices
Triple Low-E, argon (two cavities)about 0.5 – 0.7Cold climates, passive-house direction
Laminated or acoustic laminated IGUsimilar to the base IGUSafety, security and noise control

Two details decide whether the frame and glass work well together. First, warm-edge spacers (stainless steel or polymer instead of aluminium) reduce heat loss along the glass edge, which is where condensation usually starts. Second, the glazing rebate must accept the full unit thickness with the specified gaskets; triple glazing needs deeper systems and heavier hinges. Typical thermally broken systems accept glass from about 24 mm in standard depths to 50 mm or more in deep systems.

Selection

How to choose a thermal break window for your project

  1. Set the climate targetIdentify the U-value and, in hot regions, the solar heat gain (g-value or SHGC) required by the local code or energy model. Cold climates push towards deep frames and triple glazing; hot climates towards solar-control Low-E glass with a standard break.
  2. Choose opening typesCasements and tilt-turns for the best sealing; lift-slides for wide openings; fixed lights wherever opening is not needed, because they insulate best.
  3. Pick the system depthMatch frame depth and strip width to the U-value target and the glass thickness. Do not buy a deeper system than the target needs; do not try to reach a passive-house target with a 55 mm frame.
  4. Check structureLarge windows, high-rise floors and windy or coastal sites need stiffer sections, reinforced mullions and tested wind classes.
  5. Detail sealing and drainageSpecify gasket lines, pressure-equalised drainage and sill details; poor drainage is a more common cause of failure than poor insulation.
  6. Specify finish and hardwareChoose a coating class for the exposure and hardware rated for the sash weight and environment.
  7. Confirm documentsList the test reports, certificates and calculations the authority or consultant will require before ordering.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Comparing U-values calculated at different window sizes or glass specifications
  • Specifying a deep thermal break and then fitting a basic aluminium-spacer double glazed unit
  • Ignoring the installation joint: a gap without insulation or membranes undoes a good frame
  • Substituting gaskets or hardware after testing, which invalidates the test report

Quality

Quality control from extrusion to finished profile

  • Profile dimensions: sections checked against drawings and the tolerance standard (EN 12020-2 for precision architectural profiles, EN 755-9 otherwise).
  • Composite strength: rolled profiles sampled for shear strength to the system's EN 14024 or AAMA requirements, so strips cannot slip in service.
  • Finish: film thickness, adhesion, gloss and colour checked against the approved sample and coating standard.
  • Processing: where profiles are cut and machined to order, cutting length, mitre accuracy and hole positions are checked against the drawing.
  • Accessories: gaskets, cleats and hardware checked for fit against the profile sections before packing, ready for your fabricator.
  • Documents and packing: mill test certificates for the alloy, finish records, labels matching the window schedule, and edge-protected export packing.

Metline is ISO 9001 certified, and every profile shipment carries a mill test certificate. Third-party inspection before shipment can be arranged when the project requires it.

Installation

Installation details that protect thermal performance

A thermally broken frame only performs as tested if the joint between frame and wall is treated with the same care. Heat and moisture both look for the weakest path, and in many buildings that path is the installation gap rather than the window itself.

  • Position in the wall: align the thermal break with the insulation layer of the wall so the insulating line is continuous; a window set fully in the outer masonry leaf creates a cold reveal.
  • Seal inside and outside: use a vapour-tight membrane or sealant on the warm side and a vapour-open, weather-tight membrane on the cold side, with insulating foam or mineral wool between them.
  • Fixings: use thermally isolated brackets or packers where possible; steel fixings bridging the break reduce its benefit.
  • Sills and drainage: provide a sloped external sill and keep drainage slots clear so water never reaches the interior seal.

FAQ

Thermal break aluminium (aluminum) windows: frequently asked questions

How much does a thermal break improve an aluminium window?

Typically it reduces frame heat loss by half to three-quarters. A non-insulated frame is usually above 5 W/m²·K; a standard 20 mm polyamide break brings it to about 2.5 – 3.0, and a deep high-insulation frame to about 1.0 – 1.6 W/m²·K.

Do thermal break windows stop condensation?

They greatly reduce condensation on the frame because the inner shell stays warmer, but no window stops it in every condition. High indoor humidity, poor ventilation and basic aluminium spacers in the glass can still cause condensation.

Polyamide strip or pour and debridge: which is better?

Polyamide strips allow wider barriers, better insulation and dual colours, and dominate in Europe, Asia and Australia. Pour-and-debridge is a proven, economical method widely used in North American commercial windows. Both are reliable when tested to EN 14024 or AAMA 505.

Are thermal break windows worth it in hot climates?

Yes. In air-conditioned buildings they reduce heat flow inwards and lower cooling demand, especially when combined with solar-control Low-E glass. They also stop condensation on the outside face of cold, air-conditioned frames in humid climates.

Can the inside and outside be different colours?

Yes. Because the two aluminium shells are separate until the strips are rolled in, each can be powder coated, anodised or wood-grain finished in a different colour.

What do you need to quote thermal break windows?

Project location, building type, window schedule or drawings with sizes and quantities, opening types, target U-value and other performance requirements, glass build-up, finish and colour, hardware preferences, required test documents and the delivery destination.

Can you supply thermally broken profiles only?

Yes, profiles are what we supply. Metline provides thermally broken profiles in mill finish, anodised or powder coated, as lengths or cut to size for your own fabrication, with mill test certificates and export packing; we do not supply complete windows.

Need thermal break windows or profiles?

Send the window schedule, U-value target, glass, finish and destination. Our team will recommend a system depth and quote profiles and accessories.
