Unitized Curtain Wall

A unitized curtain wall is a facade built from large, factory-assembled panels, each usually one floor high and one module wide, that are glazed and sealed before they reach site and then lifted onto anchors at the slab edge. Neighbouring units interlock through split mullions and stack joints, so the wall closes floor by floor with little site sealing. Metline supplies the aluminium profiles, machined components and spandrel sheet for unitized facades to facade contractors, to project drawings, worldwide.

Overview

What is a unitized curtain wall?

A curtain wall is a non-loadbearing facade hung from the building structure: it carries its own weight and the wind load on it, and passes both back to the floor slabs, but supports nothing else. In a unitized curtain wall, that facade is divided into prefabricated units. Each unit is a complete frame of aluminium mullions and transoms with glass or opaque panels, gaskets and seals installed in a factory.

On site, units are lifted by crane or monorail hoist and hooked onto anchors fixed to the slab edge. The vertical edges of each unit carry half a mullion (a "male" and a "female" profile), so two adjacent units slot together to form one complete split mullion. The top and bottom edges form a stack joint with the units above and below. Gaskets in these interlocks make the wall weather-tight as soon as each unit is installed, and the joints absorb building movement.

Because glazing, sealing and inspection happen indoors, quality is far easier to control than on a scaffold. Site work is reduced to anchoring, hanging, aligning and a limited amount of joint sealing, which is why unitized systems dominate tall towers, repetitive facades and projects with tight programmes or little space on site.

When a unitized curtain wall is the right choice

  • High-rise buildings where scaffolding or mast climbers would be slow, costly or unsafe
  • Facades with many repeated modules, so the factory set-up pays for itself
  • Tight programmes: units can be made while the frame is still going up
  • Sites with high wind, rain or seismic movement, where factory sealing and movement joints matter

A useful rule of thumb: the more floors, repetition and programme pressure a project has, the stronger the case for unitized. Small facades with many one-off shapes are often cheaper as stick systems. Many towers combine the two, with a unitized tower above a stick-built podium. Material

Aluminium alloys and profile design

Unitized frames are extruded from 6xxx alloys. Mullions are often deeper and thicker-walled than stick systems because the split mullion halves are each only partly effective in bending until they act together. The governing values in design are the specified minimum properties of the extrusion standard, not typical values.

Alloy and temperSpecified min. tensile / yield (MPa)StandardTypical role
6063-T6205 / 170 (ASTM B221); 215 / 170 for thin walls (EN 755-2)ASTM B221, EN 755-2Most mullions, transoms and covers; excellent surface for anodising and coating
6063-T5about 150 – 175 / 110 – 130 (by standard and wall)ASTM B221, EN 755-2Covers, trims and lightly loaded sections
6005A-T6270 / 225 for walls up to 5 mm (EN 755-2)EN 755-2Highly loaded mullions where a stronger, still well-finishing alloy is needed
6061-T6260 / 240 (ASTM B221)ASTM B221, EN 755-2Heavy brackets, stiffeners and large-span mullions
6082-T6290 / 250 for walls up to 5 mm (EN 755-2)EN 755-2Anchors, brackets and fin reinforcements in European projects

Typical profile wall thicknesses are 2.0 – 4.0 mm, with thicker walls at screw ports and anchor zones. Sightlines (the visible face width of the joined mullion) are commonly 50 – 80 mm. For design, aluminium's modulus of about 70 GPa means stiffness, not strength, usually governs: deflection limits such as span/175 or 19 mm under design wind load (common in North American practice) or the deflection criteria of EN 13830 decide the mullion depth. Glass weighs about 2.5 kg per m² for every millimetre of thickness, so a 1.5 × 3.6 m unit with an 8-16-8 IGU carries about 216 kg of glass alone. Glass weight drives transom, setting block and anchor design, and determines how units are crated and lifted. Factory structural glazing allows controlled cleaning, priming, curing and adhesion testing of the silicone, one of the main quality advantages of the unitized method.

Thermal performance

Thermal breaks and energy performance

Most unitized systems are thermally broken with polyamide strips (typically PA66 GF25), single or double, in widths of roughly 15 – 35 mm, or with insulating isolators between the outer pressure plate and the inner frame. The whole-facade U-value (Ucw) is calculated to EN ISO 12631 or NFRC 100 for the actual module, combining glass, frame and the linear heat loss at the glass edge.

Frame-to-glass ratio

Wider sightlines and more transoms mean more frame area. Since even a broken frame insulates less than good glass, slim grids help the facade U-value.

Glass edge

Warm-edge spacers and deep glass bites reduce the linear thermal transmittance (ψ) at the frame, a significant part of the total heat loss.

Spandrels

Opaque zones can insulate far better than vision glass, but only if the insulation is continuous and air gaps behind spandrel glass are vented or sealed correctly.

Condensation

Internal surface temperature at mullions and stack joints is checked by thermal simulation, especially for humid interiors and cold climates.

Solar control

In hot climates the g-value or SHGC of the glass often matters more than the U-value; fritted and solar-control coatings are common.

Typical results

With Low-E double glazing, well-detailed unitized facades commonly reach Ucw in the region of 1.4 – 2.0 W/m²·K; triple glazing and narrow grids go lower.

Engineering

Water management and movement accommodation

Unitized walls use the pressure-equalised rainscreen principle. The outer gasket line deflects most rain; behind it, a chamber vented to the outside allows air pressure to equalise, so wind does not drive water inward; the inner air seal is kept dry. Any water entering the chamber drains down the mullions to the stack joint and is weeped out through baffled openings.

  • Stack joint: the lower transom of the upper unit sits within the upper transom of the unit below, overlapping gaskets and an internal gutter. It must drain water outwards while allowing the units to move vertically.
  • Split mullion: male and female halves slide relative to each other, accommodating thermal movement and storey drift horizontally without stressing the glass.
  • Thermal movement: aluminium expands about 23 µm per metre per °C. A 4 m mullion going through an 80 °C range moves roughly 7 mm, which the joints must take up repeatedly.
  • Structural movement: slab edge deflection, column shortening, creep and interstorey drift under wind or earthquake are agreed with the structural engineer and designed into the joints.
  • Unit-to-unit seals: interlock gaskets and, at crossing points, moulded or site-applied seals keep the air line continuous where four units meet.
Interfaces

Anchors, spandrels and fire stopping

Anchors connect each unit to the building. They carry the unit's dead load (normally at one level, so the unit hangs) and wind load (at top and bottom), and provide adjustment to absorb the building's construction tolerances.

Typical anchor arrangement

Type
Top-hung hook-on brackets on slab-edge or cast-in channels; side or face-fixed variants
Material
Aluminium, stainless steel or hot-dip galvanised steel, with isolation between dissimilar metals
Adjustment
Three-way (in-out, left-right, up-down), commonly ±20 – 30 mm in each direction
Loads
Dead load, positive and negative wind pressure, movement and, where relevant, seismic forces
Corrosion
Stainless or galvanised fixings, separated from aluminium with isolators where moisture can collect

Spandrel zones hide slab edges, beams and services. They can be glass with an insulated back pan, solid aluminium sheet or cassette panels in grades such as 5005 or 3003, or aluminium honeycomb panels where large, flat and light panels are needed. Behind them, a perimeter fire barrier of mineral wool and smoke sealant closes the gap between slab and facade. That assembly should match a tested system (for example to ASTM E2307 in North America or the EN 1364-4 approach in Europe) and comply with national rules on combustible materials in external walls.

Testing

Standards and performance testing

Unitized curtain walls are almost always proven by a full-scale performance mock-up test at an independent laboratory before production. The mock-up includes typical units, a stack joint, split mullions and any vents, and is tested in a defined sequence.

Design

Key design points for unitized curtain walls

Grid and module

Fix the unit width early. It drives the number of units, crate size, glass size and the economics of custom dies.

Interlock geometry

Male and female mullions must engage easily on site yet keep gaskets compressed through the full movement range.

Stack joint drainage

Model the water path through the joint and at the four-way intersections; most leaks in unitized walls start here.

Corners and specials

Inside and outside corners, curved units and terminations need their own units and tested details.

Structure interface

Agree slab edge position, tolerances, cast-in channel layout and deflection limits with the structural engineer.

Maintenance

Plan glass replacement from inside or outside, building maintenance unit restraints and access to vents and actuators.

Process

From drawings to installed facade

  1. EngineeringFacade drawings, structural calculations, thermal simulations and shop drawings are developed and approved.
  2. Dies and extrusionCustom mullion and transom dies are made where needed; profiles are extruded, aged and checked against drawing tolerances.
  3. FinishingPowder coating, PVDF or anodising to the approved samples and coating standard.
  4. FabricationCNC cutting, drilling, milling and punching; thermal break assembly; preparation for anchors, gaskets and hardware.
  5. Unit assemblyIn the facade contractor workshop, frames are assembled and squared, gaskets fitted, glass and panels installed, structural silicone applied and cured where specified.
  6. Factory inspectionDimensions, diagonals, sealant adhesion, gasket continuity and finish checked; units labelled by floor and grid line.
  7. Packing and deliveryUnits packed in steel stillages or timber crates in installation sequence, with protective film and edge guards.
  8. InstallationAnchors set out and fixed, units lifted and engaged, aligned and secured; joints sealed and site water tests carried out.
Budget

What drives the cost of a unitized curtain wall

  • Unit size and count: larger units need stronger profiles, thicker glass and heavier lifting; more repetition lowers cost per unit.
  • Profile design: mullion depth, wall thickness, thermal breaks and custom dies.
  • Glass: Low-E, laminated, triple, acoustic and spandrel glass vary widely in price.
  • Appearance: captured systems are the most economical; two- and four-sided structural glazing require more factory work and quality control.
  • Finish: PVDF and high-performance powders cost more than standard polyester but last longer on exposed facades.
  • Performance and testing: high wind, seismic, acoustic or blast requirements and the full-scale mock-up test.
  • Scope: profiles in lengths, or cut and machined, versus the work the facade contractor does in its own factory.
  • Logistics: stillages, crating, container loading and distance to site.

FAQ

Unitized curtain wall: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a unitized and a stick curtain wall?

A unitized wall arrives as complete, factory-glazed units that interlock on site; a stick wall is assembled member by member and glazed on site. Unitized is faster to install, better controlled and more movement-tolerant; stick is simpler and usually cheaper for small or highly varied facades.

How big is a typical unitized curtain wall unit?

Most units are 1.2 – 1.8 m wide and one storey high, about 3.0 – 4.5 m. Larger units are possible but increase glass weight, crate size and lifting requirements.

Which aluminium alloy is used for unitized curtain wall mullions?

Mostly 6063-T6 (aluminum 6063 in US terms), with 6005A-T6 or 6061-T6 for highly loaded mullions and 6082-T6 or 6061-T6 for brackets. Mullion depth is usually set by deflection limits rather than strength.

How does a unitized curtain wall handle building movement?

Through its joints. Split mullions slide horizontally for thermal movement and storey drift, and stack joints allow vertical movement from slab deflection and thermal expansion, without stressing the glass. Performance is verified by drift testing such as AAMA 501.4.

What testing is required?

Typically a full-scale mock-up test for air (ASTM E283), water (ASTM E331, E547, AAMA 501.1), structure (ASTM E330) and drift (AAMA 501.4), or the EN 13830 / CWCT sequences in Europe and the UK, plus site water tests on installed joints.

Does Metline supply assembled unitized units?

No. We supply split mullions, transoms, pressure plates, covers and other curtain wall profiles in mill finish or finished, cut and machined to approved shop drawings, with mill test certificates and export packing; the facade contractor assembles and glazes the units.

What do you need to quote a unitized curtain wall?

Project location and height, facade drawings and typical sections, unit sizes and quantities, glass and glazing method, performance requirements, finish, supply scope and delivery destination.
