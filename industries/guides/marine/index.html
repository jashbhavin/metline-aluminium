Marine and Shipbuilding Aluminium: Material Requirements

A plate is not "marine grade" because a catalogue says so. For a hull or offshore structure it must be an approved alloy in a qualified temper, made by a works approved by the classification society, tested for exfoliation and intergranular corrosion, and delivered with traceable certificates. This guide sets out what to specify, which alloys suit which parts of a vessel, and the mistakes that cause plate to be rejected at the yard.

Context

What makes aluminium acceptable for marine structures

Aluminium hulls, superstructures and offshore modules are designed on the assumption that the metal will keep its strength and corrosion resistance for decades in seawater. That assumption only holds when the alloy, temper and production route are controlled, which is why classification societies regulate marine aluminium far more tightly than general engineering grades.

The non-heat-treatable aluminium-magnesium (5xxx) alloys dominate hull construction. Magnesium in solid solution gives strength without heat treatment, and the alloys keep most of their strength in the welded condition, unlike heat-treatable 6xxx and 7xxx alloys that lose a large share of theirs in the heat-affected zone. They also resist general seawater corrosion very well.

The weak point of the higher-magnesium alloys is sensitisation. When an alloy with more than about 3 % magnesium is held for long periods between roughly 65 °C and 200 °C, or is processed incorrectly at the mill, magnesium-rich beta phase (Al3Mg2) forms along grain boundaries. That continuous film corrodes preferentially, causing intergranular attack and exfoliation that can peel a plate apart in layers. The special marine tempers H116 and H321, and the corrosion tests that accompany them, exist to control exactly this risk.

The four things a marine specification must fix

  • An alloy and temper listed in the class rules for the intended product form
  • Material from a manufacturer approved by the classification society for that product
  • Corrosion testing (ASTM G66 / G67 or an approved metallographic method) for hull tempers
  • Certificates that tie each plate or bar to its cast, batch and test results
Alloy selection

Which alloys go where on a vessel

IACS Unified Requirement W25, which the member societies (ABS, BV, ClassNK, DNV, KR, LR, RINA and others) apply in their own rules, lists the rolled alloys 5083, 5086, 5383, 5059, 5754 and 5456 for hull construction, and the extruded alloys 5083, 5383, 5059 and 5086, plus 6005A, 6061 and 6082 in T5 or T6. The table below shows how they are typically used.

IACS adds an important note: 6005A and 6061 should not be used in direct contact with seawater unless protected by anodes or a paint system. 5052, widely sold as "marine" sheet, is not in the UR W25 list at all; it is excellent for small craft, tanks, fittings and interiors built to recreational-craft rules, but it is not a class-approved hull alloy for ships.

For welded design, rules take the strength of the heat-affected zone as roughly the annealed (O/H111) value, whatever temper the plate was delivered in. That is why 5059, with a minimum O-temper yield of 160 MPa against 125 MPa for 5083, allows thinner welded plating.

Corrosion acceptance

Exfoliation and intergranular corrosion tests

Rolled 5083, 5383, 5059, 5086 and 5456 in the H111, H112, H116 and H321 tempers intended for hulls, or for parts in frequent contact with seawater, must be corrosion tested. During approval the mill establishes a link between microstructure and corrosion resistance using reference micrographs at 500×. Each production batch of H116 or H321 plate is then accepted either by metallographic comparison or by direct testing.

TestWhat it revealsAcceptance under UR W25
ASTM G66 (ASSET)Susceptibility to exfoliation (layer corrosion)No evidence of exfoliation; pitting rating PB or better
ASTM G67 (NAMLT)Intergranular attack, via nitric-acid mass lossMass loss not more than 15 mg/cm²
MetallographyContinuous beta-phase films at grain boundariesMatches the approved reference micrograph

ASTM B928/B928M applies the same idea to US and many international marine plate orders: it covers high-magnesium 5xxx sheet, plate and extruded profiles for marine service and requires corrosion testing of production lots. When an enquiry says "ASTM B209 5083-H116", the buyer is not getting this lot-by-lot test; specify B928 for hull plate.

Documentation

Class approval, certificates and traceability

  1. Works approvalThe producing mill holds an approval from the classification society for the alloy, temper, product form and thickness range. A distributor cannot create this approval; it travels with the material.
  2. Batch testingEach batch (same cast, product form, thickness and temper) is tensile tested and, for hull tempers, corrosion tested, often witnessed by the society surveyor.
  3. BrandingPlates carry the manufacturer's mark, alloy and temper, batch number and the society stamp, so cut pieces can still be identified in the yard.
  4. CertificateA class certificate or an EN 10204 3.2 inspection certificate countersigned by the surveyor states chemistry, mechanical results and corrosion results.
  5. Yard receiptThe shipyard checks branding against certificates before the plate enters production, and keeps the record for the vessel's file.

A mill test certificate to EN 10204 3.1 is enough for many marine fittings, small craft and offshore non-structural parts. For anything the society will inspect as hull or primary structure, ask for the class certificate at enquiry stage; retrospective certification is rarely possible.

Design & fabrication

Welding, galvanic isolation and other design points

Filler metals

ER5183 is the standard filler for 5083, 5383 and 5059 hulls; ER5356 suits 5086 and 5052. Do not use 4043 or 4047 on alloys above about 3 % Mg, because Mg2Si forms in the weld and makes it brittle.

Weld quality

Porosity comes mainly from hydrogen: moisture, oil and hydrated oxide. Clean and brush joints, store wire dry, and protect welding from draughts that disturb the gas shield.

Galvanic contact

Aluminium is anodic to steel, stainless steel and especially copper alloys. Isolate fasteners with sleeves and washers, keep joints dry, and never let bronze fittings sit in the same bilge water as an aluminium hull.

Steel-aluminium joints

Explosion-bonded transition strip lets a steel hull carry an aluminium superstructure with welded joints on both sides and no exposed bimetallic crevice.

Temperature

Keep high-Mg alloys away from sustained service above about 65 °C: engine-room uptakes, exhaust trunks, hot tanks. Use 5454 or 5754 where heat is unavoidable.

Coatings and anodes

Below the waterline aluminium hulls normally carry aluminium-compatible antifouling and zinc or aluminium anodes. Copper-based antifouling is not used on aluminium.

UR W25 also sets minimum underthickness tolerances for rolled marine plate, from 0.10 mm on 3 – 4 mm plate up to 0.65 mm on 20 – 50 mm plate over 2 m wide. Tighter tolerances can be ordered; looser ones are not acceptable for class.

Practical advice

Common mistakes when buying marine aluminium

  • Ordering "5083" without a temper. 5083-H32 or -O may be perfectly good aluminium, but neither is a tested hull temper.
  • Specifying ASTM B209 instead of B928 for hull plate, then discovering the lot has no corrosion test results.
  • Assuming any certificate is a class certificate. A 3.1 mill certificate is not surveyor-witnessed.
  • Substituting 6061 for 5083 on immersed parts because it machines more easily. It will corrode faster and lose strength when welded.
  • Using 4043 filler on 5083 to stop cracking. It weakens the joint; fix the joint design or heat input instead.
  • Mixing H116 and H321 freely without checking the drawing. They meet the same minimums but are produced differently and must not be dual-certified.

FAQ

Marine aluminium (aluminum) requirements: FAQ

What is the best aluminium alloy for a boat hull?

5083 in H116 or H321 temper is the standard choice for welded hulls: strong, class-approved and resistant to seawater. 5383 and 5059 give higher welded strength for fast craft, and 5086-H116 is a slightly lower-strength alternative that forms more easily.

Is 5052 marine-grade aluminum?

5052 has very good resistance to marine atmospheres and seawater and is widely used for small boats, tanks and fittings. It is not listed in IACS UR W25, so it is not a class-approved hull alloy for ships; use 5083, 5086 or 5754 where class approval is needed.

What is the difference between H116 and H321?

Both are strain-hardened tempers controlled so that the plate passes exfoliation and intergranular corrosion tests, and both meet the same minimum strengths in UR W25. H321 includes a final thermal stabilisation; H116 is achieved through controlled rolling. They are certified separately and should be ordered as the drawing specifies.

Why can 6061 not be used under water on a ship?

It can, but only when protected. IACS notes that 6005A and 6061 should not be in direct seawater contact unless protected by anodes or paint, because their copper and silicon content makes them less resistant than 5xxx alloys. 6082 and 6005A extrusions are common above the waterline.

What certificate do I need for hull plate?

A class certificate, or an EN 10204 3.2 certificate endorsed by the classification society's surveyor, from a mill approved for that alloy and temper, including corrosion test results for H116 and H321 plate.
