Aluminium Packaging: Material Requirements

Packaging aluminium is bought for what it does on a converting line and on a shelf: how it draws, irons, seals, prints and protects the product. The alloy is only the starting point. A complete specification fixes the temper, gauge and tolerance, surface condition, coating or laminate, and the food-contact or pharmaceutical evidence for the finished material system.

Context

What packaging buyers actually need to specify

Aluminium is used in packaging because it is a complete barrier to light, oxygen and moisture at foil gauges, it forms into deep shapes at high speed, and it is infinitely recyclable. Each of those benefits depends on choosing the right alloy-temper-gauge combination for one specific process.

A beverage can body is drawn and wall-ironed from about 0.25 mm sheet at more than a thousand cans a minute; any inclusion, earing imbalance or gauge variation shows up as tear-offs on the bodymaker. A blister lid is pushed through by a patient's thumb, so it must be hard and brittle enough to rupture cleanly. A chocolate wrap needs dead-fold, staying where it is folded without springing back. These are different products even when two of them share an alloy number.

For food and drug contact, the base metal is rarely the part that touches the product. Lacquers, heat-seal coatings, adhesives and polymer films sit between the aluminium and the contents, and regulatory acceptance must be shown for that complete construction.

Packaging standards revise regularly, and EN 602 in particular has been under revision, so state the edition you require. For pharmaceutical foil, the drug-master file or pharmacopoeia requirements of your market sit on top of the material standard.

Acceptance criteria

Properties to put on the purchase specification

Gauge and tolerance

Packaging lines are set to the micron. Specify nominal gauge, tolerance band and how it is measured: micrometer for sheet, weighing (g/m²) for foil.

Mechanical properties

Tensile strength, proof strength and elongation for the temper; for can stock, earing percentage measured on a drawn cup.

Surface condition

Bright one side or both, degreased, wettability class for printing or lamination (often tested with water or brush methods).

Pinholes

Very thin foil contains microscopic pinholes; specify a maximum count per m² for the gauge, or rely on lamination for the barrier.

Coatings

Lacquer type, weight (g/m²) and cure; heat-seal lacquer compatible with the container material (PVC, PP, PET).

Coil geometry

Inner core diameter, outer diameter or coil weight, splice limits and winding tension to suit the slitter and converter.

Step by step

How to qualify a packaging material

  1. Describe the packageState the construction (e.g. PET / aluminium foil / PE pouch), the filling and sealing method, and any retort, pasteurisation or freezing steps.
  2. Define the forming routeDrawing, wall ironing, cold forming, embossing or folding each favour a different temper and alloy.
  3. Set the surface systemLacquers, primers and adhesives with their own compliance declarations; the aluminium supplier provides the metal composition data.
  4. Run a line trialTrial coils on the actual line reveal earing, wrinkling, splits or seal failures that a certificate never will.
  5. Freeze the specificationWrite the agreed limits into the purchase specification and keep the trial results as the reference for future lots.
Practical advice

Common mistakes in packaging aluminium purchasing

  • Treating the alloy as food-contact proof. Compliance is shown for the coated or laminated material, not for bare 8011.
  • Specifying only "8011 foil". Without temper, gauge and wettability, a hard temper meant for blister lids could arrive for a laminating line.
  • Ignoring pinholes on thin foil. Foil of a few microns relies on the laminate for its barrier; check the specification reflects that.
  • Changing supplier without a line trial. Two coils meeting the same standard can run very differently on a high-speed bodymaker.
  • Forgetting the core and coil size. Coils that do not fit the unwind stand cause delays and rewinding costs.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) packaging materials: FAQ

Which aluminium alloy is used for beverage cans?

Can bodies are made from 3104 sheet in a hard temper, and the ends and tabs from 5182, which is stronger and allows thinner gauges. Both are supplied as coil to the can maker.

What is the difference between 8011 and 8079 foil?

Both are aluminium-iron-silicon foil alloys. 8079 typically gives higher elongation and fewer pinholes at very thin gauges, which suits flexible laminates; 8011 is the general-purpose alloy for household, container, lidding and closure stock.

Is aluminium foil safe for food contact?

Aluminium foil and containers are widely accepted for food contact. Compliance is demonstrated for the finished material, including any lacquer or laminate, under rules such as Regulation (EC) 1935/2004; the metal composition is controlled by standards such as EN 602.

What thickness is pharmaceutical blister foil?

Push-through lidding foil is usually about 20 – 30 µm in hard temper with a heat-seal lacquer; cold-form base foil is thicker, around 45 – 60 µm in soft temper, laminated between polymer films.

Why does very thin foil have pinholes?

At a few microns thick, tiny particles and rolling defects can pass right through the foil. The number falls sharply as gauge increases, and laminating with polymer films closes the barrier in flexible packaging.

Does Metline supply packaging aluminium in coil?

Yes. Sheet, strip and foil are supplied in coil, slit to width, with core size and coil weight to your line, and with mill certificates for each lot.
