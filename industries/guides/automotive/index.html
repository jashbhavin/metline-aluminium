Automotive & EV Aluminium: Material Requirements Guide

Vehicle aluminium is chosen around the manufacturing route and the component's job: an outer panel must stamp cleanly and look perfect after painting, a crash rail must fold predictably, and a battery enclosure must stay sealed and protect cells in a collision. No single alloy does all of that. This guide maps the main automotive alloys to their components and explains what to define before sourcing.

Body sheet

Body sheet: the paint-bake effect and surface quality

6xxx body sheet is delivered in a stabilised T4 condition, soft enough for deep stamping and hemming. During the paint-bake cycle, typically around 170 – 185 °C for about 20 minutes, Mg-Si precipitates form and yield strength rises substantially, which improves dent resistance in the finished panel. Specify the bake response, not just the as-delivered strength, and confirm shelf-life limits because natural ageing slowly reduces formability.

5xxx alloys such as 5182 and 5754 do not heat-treat and keep their strength through painting, but they can show stretcher-strain (Lüders) marks after forming, so they are used for inner panels and structures rather than visible outer skins. Surface texture (mill finish or EDT), pretreatment and pre-lubrication should all be stated in the order, along with the governing material standard such as EN 485 or VDA 239-200.

Extrusions

Extrusions for crash structures and battery enclosures

  • Crash behaviour: the alloy, temper and wall thickness determine whether a profile folds progressively or cracks. Over-aged (T7) tempers are often used to improve ductility in crash members.
  • Microstructure: recrystallised coarse-grain layers at the profile surface can reduce crash performance and anodising quality; specify limits where they matter.
  • Dimensional control: multi-hollow battery-tray profiles need tight tolerances on wall thickness and flatness for welding and sealing.
  • Joining: friction stir welding, MIG, self-piercing rivets, flow-drill screws and structural adhesives each have alloy and temper limits; qualify the joint, not only the material.
  • Thermal management: integrated cooling channels or brazed cold plates often form part of the battery structure.
Qualification

Qualification and supplier requirements

Automotive parts are released through a formal qualification process rather than by grade alone. A general recommendation such as "use 6082" does not qualify a component.

Quality system

Automotive production suppliers usually work to IATF 16949, with production part approval (PPAP) packages that freeze material, process and inspection.

Material standards

EN 573 and EN 485 / EN 755 for composition and properties; OEM material standards and VDA 239-200 for body sheet.

Testing

Tensile and bake response, formability (e.g. bending, hemming), crash tests on profiles, corrosion tests (e.g. filiform, intergranular) and joint tests.

Traceability

Cast, coil or extrusion lot numbers tracked through to the part, with certificates for every delivery.

Durability

Corrosion and mixed-material design

Modern bodies mix aluminium with steel, magnesium and carbon-fibre composite. Where aluminium touches steel or carbon fibre in the presence of electrolyte, galvanic corrosion attacks the aluminium. Designers isolate joints with adhesives and sealers, use coated fasteners, and rely on e-coat coverage. Copper-containing 6xxx alloys can be more susceptible to intergranular and filiform corrosion; check corrosion test results for the actual alloy and paint system.

Pitfalls

Common mistakes in automotive aluminium sourcing

  • Specifying strength in the as-delivered T4 condition and forgetting the paint-bake response
  • Using a 5xxx alloy on a visible outer panel and getting stretcher-strain marks
  • Choosing the highest-strength alloy for a crash member, which can crack instead of folding
  • Releasing a part without qualifying the joint method on the real alloy and temper
  • Ignoring natural ageing and shelf life of T4 sheet between delivery and stamping

FAQ

Automotive and EV aluminium (aluminum) requirements: FAQs

Why are outer panels made from 6xxx rather than 5xxx alloys?

6xxx sheet forms well in T4 and gains strength in the paint-bake oven, giving dent resistance, and it does not show stretcher-strain marks. 5xxx alloys are used for inner panels where appearance is less critical.

Which aluminium is used for EV battery trays?

Typically 6xxx extrusions (6063, 6061, 6082 or 6005A) for frames and crash rails, combined with 5754 or 6xxx sheet or cast parts for floors and covers, joined by friction stir welding, MIG and adhesives.

What is the paint-bake response?

The increase in yield strength that 6xxx body sheet gains during the paint curing cycle, typically around 170 – 185 °C for about 20 minutes. It is a specified property for outer-panel sheet.

Are 7xxx alloys used in cars?

Yes, mainly as extruded bumper beams and some reinforcements (for example 7003 and 7108), and increasingly as high-strength sheet for safety-cage parts formed by hot or warm stamping.

Can a supplier certificate replace part qualification?

No. The certificate proves the material meets its specification; component qualification (PPAP or OEM release) proves the part, process and joints meet the vehicle requirements.

Sourcing aluminium for a vehicle programme?

Send the component, alloy and temper, specification and annual volumes. We will confirm options and documentation.
