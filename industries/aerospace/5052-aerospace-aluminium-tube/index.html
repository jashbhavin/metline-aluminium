5052 Aerospace Aluminium Tube

5052 tube is drawn, seamless aluminium-magnesium tubing used in aircraft for low and medium-pressure fuel, oil, vent, pneumatic and instrument lines, and for welded ducting and frames. It bends and flares easily in the annealed O temper, resists corrosion without coating and welds well. Overview

Why 5052 is the standard aircraft line tube

5052 is a medium-strength aluminium alloy with about 2.5 % magnesium and 0.25 % chromium. It cannot be heat treated, so its strength comes from cold drawing; in exchange it is ductile, weldable and very resistant to corrosion, including in marine air.

Aircraft fluid systems need tube that can be bent to tight radii, flared or beaded for fittings, and left in service for decades without corroding inside or out. Annealed 5052-O does this better than almost any other aluminium alloy. It is stronger than 3003, which it largely replaced for fuel and oil lines, yet far more forgiving to form than heat-treated 6061-T6 or 2024-T3. Where pressure is high, as in main hydraulic systems, designers move to 6061-T6, 2024-T3 or titanium and stainless steel; for fuel, oil, vent, drain, pneumatic, instrument and low-pressure hydraulic return lines, 5052-O remains the default.

The same alloy drawn in harder tempers, H32 to H38, is used for light frames, handrails, antenna masts, ducting and fixtures where some strength is needed and the tube is welded or mechanically joined. Compared with high-manganese alloys, 5052 also has better resistance to stress-corrosion cracking, which matters in tubes that are bent and then held under clamp loads.

The page below covers composition, properties by temper, the AMS and ASTM specifications buyers most often quote, sizes and weights, tolerances, fabrication advice, testing, finishes and how 5052 compares with the alternatives.

What 5052 tube offers

  • High formability in O temper for bending, flaring, beading and swaging
  • Good fatigue strength under vibration for a non-heat-treatable alloy
  • Corrosion resistance in fuel, oil, hydraulic fluid, humid and salt-laden air
  • Easy TIG and MIG welding with little strength loss in the soft tempers

Tempers

Which 5052 tube temper should you order?

5052-O

Annealed, fully soft. The only temper normally used for aircraft fluid lines that are bent and flared, because it tolerates tight radii and 37° flares without cracking.

5052-H32

Quarter-hard, strain-hardened and stabilised. Balanced strength and workability for welded frames, housings and enclosures.

5052-H34

Half-hard. More strength with moderate formability; used for structural reinforcement tube and medium-duty straight runs.

5052-H36

Three-quarter-hard. Chosen where the tube must resist denting or vibration loads and will be bent only gently, if at all.

5052-H38

Full-hard. Highest strength of the standard tempers; for straight, mechanically fastened tubes and fixed assemblies.

5052-H112

As-extruded. Used for larger extruded tube and pipe that will be machined or welded rather than drawn to close tolerance.

  • Bending, flaring or beading? Order O. Stabilised H3x tempers crack at the small radii typical of aircraft plumbing.
  • Welded frame or enclosure? H32 or H34; remember the weld zone will soften towards O properties.
  • Vibration or handling loads on straight tube? H36 gives more stiffness against denting.
  • Maximum strength, no forming? H38, or consider 6061-T6 if the part needs still more.

Design

Pressure rating and design notes for 5052 lines

A first estimate of burst pressure for a thin-walled tube comes from Barlow's formula, P = 2 × S × t ÷ OD, where S is the tensile strength, t the wall and OD the outside diameter. For a 3/8 in × 0.035 in 5052-O line using the 25 ksi minimum tensile strength, estimated burst is about 4,700 psi (32 MPa). Aircraft practice applies a substantial factor of safety, often four, giving a working pressure in the region of 1,100 – 1,200 psi for that size.

That calculation explains why 5052-O is used for fuel, oil, pneumatic and return lines, while 3,000 psi and 5,000 psi hydraulic pressure lines use 6061-T6, 2024-T3, titanium or corrosion-resistant steel. The figures here are only a first estimate; the governing design manual, the fitting manufacturer's data and qualification testing control the final rating.

  • Bend radius: 5052-O tube bends well at centreline radii of about 2 – 3 × OD on a mandrel bender; thin walls need closer-fitting mandrels and wiper dies to avoid wrinkling.
  • Flaring: 37° single flares (AN/MS style) are routinely made on 5052-O; harder tempers are prone to splitting at the flare.
  • Support spacing: clamp lines often enough to keep vibration frequencies away from engine and propeller excitation.
  • Galvanic contact: separate from stainless or titanium fittings with sealant or anodised and primed surfaces; never let carbon-fibre structure touch bare tube.
  • Chafing: use cushioned clamps and sleeves where lines pass through structure.
Working with 5052 tube

Bending, welding, machining and finishing

Bending

Use O temper for anything but gentle bends. Draw bending with a mandrel controls ovality; keep tube clean and lubricated to avoid scoring that could start fatigue cracks.

Welding

TIG and MIG weld readily with ER5356 filler. In O temper the joint keeps nearly full base strength; in H3x tempers the heat-affected zone softens to near annealed values.

Brazing

5052 is difficult to braze because magnesium oxide hinders wetting; where brazed joints are required, 3003 or 6063 tube is usually chosen instead.

Machining

Soft tempers can be gummy. Sharp, high-rake tools and cutting fluid give clean cut-offs and end preparation.

Annealing

Cold-worked tube can be returned to O by annealing at about 345 °C with controlled cooling, restoring formability after heavy working.

Cleaning

Lines for fuel, oxygen or hydraulic service are cleaned, capped and bagged to the cleanliness level the system specification calls for.

Applications

Where 5052 tube is used

  • Aircraft fuel systems: feed, transfer, vent and drain lines in O temper
  • Oil and lubrication: engine and gearbox oil lines on light aircraft and helicopters
  • Pneumatic and instrument lines: pitot-static, vacuum and low-pressure air lines
  • Hydraulic return and low-pressure lines where the system design allows aluminium
  • Environmental-control ducting and cooling-air ducts in welded assemblies
  • Ground support equipment: handrails, platforms, stands and refuelling equipment
  • Marine and offshore: seawater-spray piping, railings, masts and canopy frames
  • Transport and industry: fuel and oil lines, HVAC conduits, pneumatic systems and machine frames

A representative case is a light-aircraft fuel system: wing-tank feed lines in 3/8 in × 0.035 in 5052-O tube, bent on a mandrel bender, 37°-flared, joined with anodised aluminium fittings and supported in cushioned clamps. The same alloy in H32 sheet forms the tanks, giving consistent corrosion behaviour across the system.

Buyer checklist

Common mistakes when buying 5052 tube

  • Ordering a hard temper for bent lines. H32 and H34 look attractive for their strength, but they split at flares and tight bends. If the tube will be formed, order O and let the design use O allowables.
  • Accepting seamed tube for pressure service. Porthole-die extruded tube is cheaper but has extrusion welds along its length. Fuel and hydraulic lines should be drawn seamless, and the order should say so.
  • Leaving out the specification revision. AMS documents change over time. Quoting the revision letter avoids disputes over test requirements and tolerances at receiving inspection.
  • Ignoring internal cleanliness. Drawing lubricant and debris inside tube can contaminate fuel and hydraulic systems. Ask for cleaned and capped tube if lines go straight into assembly.
  • Mixing imperial and metric sizes. Aircraft fittings are sized to imperial tube (for example 3/8 in OD). A 10 mm tube will not seal in a 3/8 in fitting, so state the unit system clearly.
  • Forgetting galvanic isolation. Bare 5052 in contact with stainless fittings or carbon-fibre structure will corrode at the joint; plan sealant, primer or anodised interfaces in the design.

FAQ

5052 aerospace aluminium (aluminum) tube: FAQs

Which temper of 5052 tube is used for aircraft fuel lines?

5052-O, the annealed temper. It bends and flares without cracking and still has enough strength for fuel, oil, vent and pneumatic pressures. AMS 4069 and AMS 4071 both cover 5052-O drawn seamless tube.

What is the difference between AMS 4069 and AMS 4071?

Both cover drawn seamless 5052-O tube. AMS 4069 is the close-tolerance general tubing specification; AMS 4071 is written for hydraulic tubing and covers a defined OD range with requirements suited to pressure service.

Can 5052 tube be used for high-pressure hydraulic lines?

Usually not for 3,000 psi or higher pressure lines. Those use 6061-T6, 2024-T3, titanium or stainless steel. 5052-O is used for return, case-drain and low-pressure lines where the system design allows it.

How tight can 5052-O tube be bent?

On a mandrel bender, centreline radii of about two to three times the OD are routine. Thin walls and harder tempers need larger radii; always follow the bend tables of your design manual.

Is 5052 tube seamless?

Aerospace 5052 tube is drawn seamless, made from extruded hollow blooms and cold drawn to size. Porthole-die extruded tube, which has longitudinal weld seams, is not normally accepted for pressure lines.

How much does 5052 tube weigh?

Weight per metre is π × (OD − wall) × wall × 2.68 ÷ 1000 in kilograms, with sizes in millimetres. A 25.4 × 1.65 mm tube weighs about 0.33 kg per metre.

Do you supply 5052 tube with certificates internationally?

Yes. Metline ships 5052 tube worldwide with EN 10204 3.1 mill certificates, cut to length and export packed, on EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP terms.
