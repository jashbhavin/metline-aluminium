7050 Aerospace Aluminium Forgings (T74 / T7452)

7050 forgings combine high strength, good fracture toughness and strong resistance to stress-corrosion cracking in thick sections, which makes them a first choice for bulkheads, wing and landing-gear fittings and engine mounts. Die forgings are ordered in T74 (AMS 4107) or T7452 (AMS 4333), and hand forgings in T7452 (AMS 4108). Metline supplies 7050 die forgings, hand forgings, rings and forged blocks with full certification to customers worldwide.

Overview

Why aircraft designers choose 7050 forgings

Forging works the metal into the shape of the part, so the grain flows around corners and lugs instead of being cut through as it is when a part is machined from plate. That grain flow improves fatigue life and short-transverse properties, which is exactly where heavily loaded aerospace fittings tend to fail.

7050 suits forging particularly well. Its zirconium addition keeps the grain structure fine and makes the alloy far less quench sensitive than 7075, so the centre of a thick forging still reaches high strength. Overageing to T74 then gives strong resistance to stress-corrosion cracking. This combination replaced 7075-T73 forgings on many airframes, since 7050-T74 offers similar corrosion resistance with noticeably higher strength.

The T74 temper was originally designated T736, and some older drawings and certificates still use that name. Forgings that are compressed after quenching to relieve residual stress carry the T7452 temper, which is preferred for hand forgings and large die forgings that will be machined heavily.

Tempers

T74 versus T7452 forgings

7050-T74 (formerly T736)

Solution heat-treated, quenched and overaged. The standard condition for closed-die forgings where the forged shape is close to the final part and residual stress is not a major concern.

7050-T7452

Solution heat-treated, then compressed by about 1 – 5 % permanent set to relieve quench stresses before overageing. Used for hand forgings and large die forgings that will be extensively machined, because they distort much less.

Forging stock (F)

Bar or billet supplied as fabricated for the forging shop, with heat treatment carried out after forging.

Supply range

Forging types and size range

Closed-die forgings

Near-net-shape parts produced in matched dies: fittings, frames, bulkhead sections and landing-gear components. Economic for repeat parts where tooling cost can be spread over the programme.

Hand (open-die) forgings

Blocks, bars and simple shapes worked between flat or simple dies. Ideal for prototypes, low volumes and large machined parts.

Rolled rings

Seamless rings for engine mounts, launch-vehicle joints and rotating structures.

Forged discs and shafts

Upset discs and stepped shafts for hubs and actuator parts.

Size limits depend on press capacity and the heat-treatment and quench equipment available for the part. Hand forgings up to 203 mm thick are covered by AMS 4108; larger sections and very large die forgings are quoted case by case. Quality

Inspection and certification of 7050 forgings

  • Chemical analysis per cast and tensile testing per lot in the directions the specification requires
  • Electrical conductivity and hardness checks to confirm the overaged temper
  • Ultrasonic inspection to AMS-STD-2154 at the class ordered
  • Grain-flow macro-etch and first-article inspection on new die forgings where specified
  • Fluorescent penetrant inspection of machined or forged surfaces when called up
  • EN 10204 3.1 certificate with full traceability to cast, heat-treatment lot and forging number
Working with 7050

Machining and finishing 7050 forgings

7050 forgings machine cleanly with sharp carbide tools, high speeds and flood coolant. T7452 forgings hold their shape better than T74 when large amounts of material are removed. Balance roughing between faces and allow a finishing pass after stress has redistributed.

Fusion welding is not recommended. Parts are fastened, bushed and sealed into the structure. Surface protection is normally chromic, sulphuric or boric-sulphuric anodising, or conversion coating plus primer. Fatigue-critical lugs and fillets are often shot peened, and interference-fit bushings or cold expansion of holes are used to extend fatigue life.

Applications

Typical 7050 forging applications

  • Fuselage: main frames, pressure-bulkhead parts and door-surround fittings
  • Wing: wing-to-body fittings, spar fittings and flap-track and slat-track parts
  • Landing gear: trunnion fittings, side braces, wheel and brake parts in aluminium designs
  • Engines and nacelles: engine mounts, pylon fittings and thrust-reverser parts
  • Helicopters: rotor hubs, swashplate parts and transmission mounts
  • Defence: structural forgings for armoured vehicles and missile airframes

FAQ

7050 aerospace aluminium (aluminum) forgings: FAQ

What is the difference between 7050-T74 and T736?

They are the same temper. T736 was the original designation and was later renamed T74. Older drawings and certificates may still show T736.

What does the 52 in T7452 mean?

After quenching the forging is compressed by about 1 – 5 % permanent set to relieve residual stress, then overaged. This reduces distortion during machining.

Which specification covers 7050 hand forgings?

AMS 4108 covers 7050-T7452 hand forgings up to 8 in thick. AMS 4107 covers T74 die forgings and AMS 4333 covers T7452 die forgings.

Why use 7050 instead of 7075 for forgings?

7050 keeps high strength in thick sections and has better toughness, while T74 gives stress-corrosion resistance close to 7075-T73. The result is a stronger forging with similar durability.

Can 7050 forgings be welded?

Fusion welding is not recommended because of cracking and strength loss in the weld zone. Forgings are joined mechanically.

Can you supply prototype forgings without die tooling?

Yes. Hand forgings and forged blocks are a practical route for prototypes and small batches; die forgings make sense once volumes justify the tooling.
