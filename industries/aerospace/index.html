Overview

Why aircraft are built from aluminium

Weight is everything in flight. Every kilogram saved means more payload, more range or less fuel. High-strength aluminium alloys offer one of the best strength-to-weight ratios of any affordable structural material, which is why aluminium makes up most of the structure of a typical commercial airliner.

Aerospace alloys are chosen for more than raw strength. Fatigue resistance decides how long a wing skin lasts, fracture toughness decides how a crack behaves, and resistance to stress corrosion cracking decides whether a forging can be trusted for decades. Tempers such as T7351 and T7451 exist precisely to balance those properties.

Applications

Where aluminium is used

Fuselage skins

2024-T3 and clad sheet for pressurised skins with excellent fatigue performance.

Wing structure

7075 and 7050 plate for spars, ribs and upper wing skins under compression.

Fittings and frames

Thick plate and forgings machined into bulkheads, brackets and landing gear parts.

Stringers and floor beams

Extruded sections in 2024, 7075 and 6061.

Spacecraft and launchers

2219 for cryogenic fuel tanks thanks to its weldability and low-temperature toughness.

Drones and UAVs

6061 and 7075 for lightweight frames and machined components.

FAQ

Aerospace questions

Which aluminium is most used in aircraft?

2024 and 7075 are the classic aerospace alloys: 2024 for fatigue-critical skins and 7075 for high-strength structure. 7050 has largely replaced 7075 in thick sections.

What does T7351 mean?

An over-aged, stress-relieved temper for 7xxx alloys that trades a little strength for much better resistance to stress corrosion cracking.

Can you supply material with certification?

Tell us the specification and certification you need with your enquiry and we will confirm what can be supplied.

Sourcing aerospace aluminium?

Send us the alloy, temper, specification, sizes and quantity. Our team will reply with availability, documentation and lead time.
