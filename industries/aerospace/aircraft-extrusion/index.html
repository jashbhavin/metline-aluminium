Aircraft Aluminium Extrusions

Aircraft extrusions are aluminium shapes, bars and tubes pressed through a die and then heat treated and stretched to aerospace specifications. They form the stringers, longerons, frames, spar caps, seat tracks and floor beams of almost every metal airframe. Metline supplies 2024, 7075, 7050, 7150, 6061 and other aerospace alloys as standard AND shapes, shapes to customer drawings, seat-track sections, drawn tube and bar, certified to AMS and ASTM, to customers worldwide.

Overview

What is an aircraft extrusion?

An aircraft extrusion is an aluminium section with a constant cross-section, made by forcing a heated billet through a steel die. For aerospace use it is then solution heat treated, quenched, stretched to remove residual stress and straighten it, and aged. The suffix "511" in tempers such as 2024-T3511 or 7075-T6511 records that stretching step and minor straightening.

Extrusions carry load along their length, which is exactly what stringers, longerons, frames, spar caps and floor beams need. Because the die can put material precisely where the bending or buckling load demands it, an extruded stringer with a bulb or flange is lighter than one machined from plate or roll-formed from sheet for the same job.

The aerospace extrusion market divides into three groups: standard shapes published as AND (Army-Navy) or similar drawings, which anyone can supply; proprietary airframe-maker shapes, which can only be produced against drawings the customer is authorised to use; and custom shapes designed for a specific programme. Metline supplies all three, provided the drawing rights are clear.

What buyers usually need to specify

  • Alloy, temper and the material specification with revision (for example 7075-T73511 to AMS-QQ-A-200/11)
  • Shape: AND number, customer drawing or section sketch with tolerances
  • Length (common aerospace lengths are 10, 12, 20 and 24 ft) and quantity
  • Any extra tests: ultrasonic inspection, conductivity, fracture toughness or first article inspection

Tube and bar

Aircraft tube and bar alongside shapes

Many aircraft programmes buy tube and bar from the same source as their shapes, so they can be certified and shipped together. Drawn tube is cold drawn over a mandrel after extrusion, giving tighter wall tolerance and a smoother finish than extruded tube; it is the normal choice for hydraulic, fuel and control-rod tubing. Extruded tube is used for larger sizes and heavier walls.

Tempers

Aircraft extrusion tempers explained

Temper designations on aerospace drawings carry a lot of information. The first digits after the T describe the heat treatment; the trailing "510" or "511" describes stress relief by stretching. Choosing the wrong one is a common cause of distortion, stress-corrosion cracking or rejected parts.

T3511 (2024)

Solution treated, stretched, naturally aged. Good fatigue and fracture toughness; the default for 2024 stringers and frames.

T42 (2024)

Solution treated and naturally aged by the user rather than the mill, typically after forming. Slightly lower guaranteed properties than T3511.

T8511 (2024, 2219)

Solution treated, stretched and artificially aged. Higher yield and better stress-corrosion resistance than T3511, but lower toughness.

T6511 (6061, 7075, 2014)

Solution treated, stretched and aged to peak strength. For 7075, use only where stress-corrosion is not a concern.

T73511 / T76511 (7075)

Over-aged for stress-corrosion (T73) or exfoliation (T76) resistance. T73 gives up roughly 10 – 15 % strength compared with T6.

T74511 / T77511 (7050, 7150)

Modern over-aged tempers balancing strength, toughness and corrosion resistance; T77 uses a three-step ageing process to keep most of the T6 strength.

T3510 and T6510 are the same as T3511 and T6511 except that no straightening is allowed after stretching. They are specified when residual stress must be kept to an absolute minimum, at the cost of looser straightness tolerances.

Tolerances

Cross-section tolerances

Unless a drawing gives tighter limits, aerospace extrusions follow the standard tolerance tables of ANSI H35.2 (referenced by ASTM B221 and AMS-QQ-A-200). Tolerances depend on the dimension, on whether it is a metal or a space dimension, and on the circumscribing-circle size. Hard 2xxx and 7xxx alloys carry slightly wider tolerances than 6xxx alloys. Processing

Cutting, machining and finishing

Cut lengths

Saw cutting to length, with squareness and burr-free ends; standard lengths of 10, 12, 20 and 24 ft or metric equivalents.

CNC machining

Seat-track slots, lightening holes, joggles and end fittings machined to drawing on request.

Heat treatment

Re-solution treatment and ageing of formed parts per AMS 2770 can be arranged through approved processors.

Forming

Stretch forming of frames and stringers is usually done in W or T3/T4 condition before final ageing.

Surface treatment

Chromic, sulphuric or boric-sulphuric anodising, or conversion coating, through qualified processors.

Inspection

Dimensional checks against drawing, conductivity and hardness for 7xxx tempers, ultrasonic inspection where specified.

Applications

Where aircraft extrusions are used

  • Wings: stringers, spar caps and chords, rib caps and leading-edge supports
  • Fuselage: stringers, longerons, frame sections, window and door surrounds
  • Floors: floor beams, seat tracks, cargo rails and roller tracks
  • Empennage and control surfaces: stiffeners and hinge extrusions for ailerons, elevators and rudders
  • Helicopters: tail-boom stringers, hollow blade spars on older designs, transmission supports
  • Interiors and systems: galley frames, overhead bins, avionics racks and ducting
  • Light and sport aircraft: 6061-T6 wing spars, streamline struts and tubular frames

A typical example: a narrow-body fuselage uses 2024-T3511 or 7075-T73511 zee and hat stringers riveted to the skin at roughly 150 – 250 mm spacing, crossed by formed or extruded frames. Upper-wing stringers on large transports use stronger 7150 or 7055 tempers because the upper skin is loaded mainly in compression.

Extrusions are usually the lowest-weight answer for long, constant-section load paths. Machining from plate makes sense for short parts or where the section must change along the length; roll-forming suits thin, constant-thickness sections in 2024 or 7075 sheet, often produced in O or W condition and heat treated afterwards.

Common buying mistakes

  • Ordering 7075-T6511 for a part whose drawing requires T73511: the two cannot be told apart visually but behave very differently in service
  • Quoting an outdated specification revision, which can lead to certificate rejection at goods-in inspection
  • Assuming any supplier can reproduce a proprietary shape without the drawing and the right to use it
  • Tightening as-extruded tolerances instead of allowing machining stock, which raises cost without improving the finished part
  • Forgetting to state test requirements such as ultrasonic inspection or conductivity at the quote stage
Packing

Packing and shipping aerospace extrusions

Aerospace extrusions are long, heat-treated parts with tight straightness tolerances, so packing must stop bending, chafing and moisture. Each length is identified with alloy, temper, specification and lot number so that traceability is kept through to the end user.

  • Interleaving paper or film between lengths to prevent surface rubbing
  • Rigid tubes or reinforced cartons for short lengths and small sections
  • Wooden cases with internal supports at regular intervals for long lengths
  • Desiccant and moisture barrier for sea freight
  • Certificates, packing list and heat/lot references enclosed with each consignment

Ordering

How to order aircraft extrusions

  1. SpecifyAlloy and temper, AND number or drawing, length, quantity and the governing specification with its revision (e.g. AMS-QQ-A-200/11, AMS 4154).
  2. ConfirmWe confirm the specification can be met, the tolerance class, any extra tests and the delivery terms (EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP).
  3. InspectMaterial is checked for dimensions, straightness and surface condition; tests named on the order are carried out or arranged with a third party.
  4. DocumentEach lot ships with a mill test certificate to EN 10204 3.1 showing chemistry and mechanical results, traceable to the cast or lot number.
  5. Pack and shipExport packing in sealed, moisture-protected wooden cases, coils on pallets or reels, shipped by sea or air to your port or door.

FAQ

Aircraft aluminium (aluminum) extrusions: frequently asked questions

What does the "511" in T3511 or T6511 mean?

The extrusion was stress-relieved by stretching after quenching and may have received minor straightening afterwards. It reduces distortion during machining.

Can you supply proprietary airframe-maker shapes?

Yes, where the customer holds the right to use the drawing and supplies it with the order, or for parts already approved under the customer's programme.

Which is better for stringers, 2024 or 7075?

2024-T3511 has better fatigue and damage tolerance and suits tension-loaded areas such as the lower wing and fuselage. 7075, 7050 and 7150 give higher strength for compression-loaded areas such as the upper wing.

What lengths are aircraft extrusions supplied in?

Common aerospace lengths are 10, 12, 20 and 24 ft, with metric or cut-to-length options on request.

Are AND shapes still used?

Yes, for repairs, light aircraft, ground equipment and secondary structure. Many newer programmes use drawing-specific shapes instead.

Do you provide first article inspection?

For parts machined to drawing, first article inspection to AS9102 can be arranged on request, together with EN 10204 3.1 material certificates.
