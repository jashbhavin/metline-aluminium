Overhead line conductor

ACSR Conductor (Aluminium Conductor Steel Reinforced)

ACSR is the most widely used bare overhead conductor in the world: layers of hard-drawn 1350 aluminium wire carry the current, wrapped around a galvanised steel core that carries much of the mechanical load. The combination gives high strength for long spans and heavy ice at a moderate cost. Metline supplies ACSR in ASTM, IEC/EN, BS and IS constructions, with a choice of steel core coatings, to utilities and contractors worldwide.

Overview

How ACSR works

Aluminium conducts well and is light but not very strong; steel is strong but a poor conductor. ACSR puts each where it works best. The steel core, a single wire or a stranded group of 7, 19 or more wires, gives the conductor high tensile strength and a low coefficient of expansion. The aluminium layers around it carry almost all the current.

The ratio of steel to aluminium sets the character of the conductor. A 6/1 construction (six aluminium wires around one steel wire) has a high steel share and is strong for its size, used on rural distribution lines with long spans. A 54/7 or 45/7 construction has less steel and more aluminium, so it carries more current per kilogram and is used on high-voltage transmission. Constructions such as 26/7 and 30/7 sit in between.

Because the steel carries a growing share of tension as the conductor heats and the aluminium expands, ACSR sags less at high temperature than all-aluminium conductors. This "knee point" behaviour is one reason ACSR remains the default for long spans, river crossings and lines in heavy ice or wind areas.

Why ACSR is so widely used

  • High rated strength for long spans, crossings and heavy ice or wind loading
  • Less sag at high temperature than AAC, AAAC or ACAR
  • Wide choice of standard constructions and decades of design data
  • Moderate cost per unit of strength and ampacity

Ampacity is not a fixed catalogue value: it depends on ambient temperature, wind, solar radiation, emissivity and the maximum conductor temperature allowed. It is calculated per project using IEEE 738 or CIGRE methods. Where more capacity is needed on existing towers, high-temperature conductors such as ACSS (annealed aluminium on a high-strength steel core, ASTM B856) can operate at much higher temperatures with limited extra sag.

A common error is to say ACSR conducts better than all-aluminium designs because of its core. It does not: the steel adds strength, not conductivity. Choose ACSR for mechanical reasons; choose AAAC or ACAR where corrosion, weight or losses dominate. See our 6201 AAAC and ACAR pages.

Applications

Where ACSR is used

  • High-voltage and extra-high-voltage transmission: single and bundled conductors on lattice towers
  • Rural distribution: long spans between poles with 6/1 and 6/1-type constructions
  • River, valley and highway crossings: high-strength constructions with extra steel
  • Heavy ice and high-wind regions: where mechanical loading governs conductor choice
  • Line extensions and upgrades: matching existing ACSR circuits and hardware

FAQ

ACSR conductor: frequently asked questions

What span conditions suit ACSR?

Long spans, river and valley crossings, and regions with heavy ice or wind loading, where its high rated strength and lower high-temperature sag reduce the number or height of structures needed.

Does the steel core affect conductivity?

Only slightly. The steel carries very little current, so resistance is set by the aluminium area. The steel adds weight and, in alternating current, small magnetic losses, but its purpose is strength.

How do I choose between ACSR and AAAC?

Run a sag-tension study for the spans and loads. Choose ACSR when strength and high-temperature sag govern; choose AAAC when corrosion, weight or losses matter more and spans are moderate, as on many coastal distribution lines.

Which steel core coating should I specify?

Class A zinc for normal atmospheres; heavier zinc (class B or C), zinc-aluminium alloy or aluminium-clad steel for coastal and industrial areas. Greasing the core adds protection in very corrosive environments.

What do ACSR code names like Drake, Zebra or Panther mean?

They identify standard constructions. North American ASTM sizes use bird names (Drake is 795 kcmil, 26/7), and BS and IS sizes use animal names (Zebra is 54/3.18 + 7/3.18 mm). IEC 61089 uses a systematic notation of aluminium and steel areas instead.

Can you supply ACSR to a utility specification?

Yes. Send the specification, construction, core coating and drum lengths. Metline supplies aluminum conductor steel reinforced (ACSR) with test reports and export packing to projects worldwide.
