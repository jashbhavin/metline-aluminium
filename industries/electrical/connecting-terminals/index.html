Power cable terminations

Aluminium & Bimetal Cable Lugs

A cable lug joins the end of a power cable to a busbar, breaker or transformer terminal, and it must match the conductor metal it is crimped to. Use aluminium lugs on aluminium cable, copper lugs on copper cable, and bimetal copper-aluminium lugs wherever an aluminium cable meets a copper terminal. Metline supplies aluminium and bimetal lugs, and the aluminium and copper-clad aluminium stock used to make them, to electrical contractors and lug makers worldwide; copper lugs are covered here only so the right lug is chosen.

Overview

Why the lug metal must match the cable

A compression lug is a tube (the barrel) that is crimped onto the stripped conductor, joined to a flat palm with a bolt hole. Once crimped, the barrel and the strands form a single cold-welded mass, so the lug becomes part of the conductor. If the two metals differ, every problem of a copper-aluminium contact is built into that joint.

Three physical facts drive terminal selection. First, aluminium and copper sit about half a volt apart in the galvanic series, so where moisture bridges the two, the aluminium corrodes. Second, aluminium expands roughly 40 % more than copper with temperature (about 23 against 17 µm/m·K), so a mixed joint works loose as it heats and cools under load. Third, aluminium carries a hard, insulating oxide skin and creeps under sustained pressure more than copper, so aluminium joints need oxide-breaking compound, enough crimp compression and bolted hardware that maintains clamp force.

Matching the metals removes the first two problems, and correct crimping and hardware deal with the third. Bimetal lugs move the copper-aluminium interface into a factory-made, sealed, metallurgically bonded joint, where moisture cannot reach it and expansion is controlled.

Bimetal Cu-Al lugs

Bimetal cable lugs

A bimetal lug has an aluminium barrel for the aluminium cable and a copper palm for the copper terminal. The two parts are joined, most often by friction welding, into a solid-state bond with no air gap and only a very thin interface layer. The barrel is usually pre-filled with oxide-inhibiting compound and sealed with a cap.

Straight bimetal lug

The standard single-hole design for aluminium cable to copper equipment terminals in panels and substations.

Bimetal lug with copper ring

A copper ring or washer on the palm increases the copper contact area around the bolt.

Bimetal connector (sleeve)

An in-line sleeve with aluminium and copper halves that splices an aluminium cable to a copper cable.

Two-hole bimetal lug

Two bolt holes prevent rotation and spread contact pressure on large busbars.

Bimetal lugs are standard wherever aluminium feeder cables meet copper-terminated equipment: transformer secondary bushings, main breakers, solar inverters, and distribution boards fed by aluminium service cables.

Aluminium lugs

Aluminium cable lugs

Aluminium lugs are made from electrical-grade aluminium tube or bar, pressed or machined to shape. They are used on aluminium cables terminating on aluminium busbars or on terminals rated for aluminium, typically marked "AL" or "AL/CU".

General-purpose barrel length for low-voltage panels and feeders.

Aluminium lugs should not be used on copper conductors: copper strands inside an aluminium barrel create a hidden galvanic couple and an expansion mismatch.

Copper lugs

Copper cable lugs

Copper lugs are made from seamless electrolytic copper tube (C11000 or oxygen-free grades) and are usually electro-tin-plated. They range from small 1.5 mm² terminals in control panels to 1000 mm² lugs on generator and transformer leads. Metline does not supply copper lugs; they are described here because copper cable needs them, and because a copper conductor should never be fitted with an aluminium lug.

The cost difference reflects the metal price as much as the manufacturing. For large feeders, aluminium cable with aluminium or bimetal lugs is often the lowest installed cost, even after allowing for the larger conductor size aluminium needs.

As a rule of thumb, an aluminium conductor needs about one standard size larger than copper for the same current, for example 240 mm² aluminium in place of 150 – 185 mm² copper, depending on installation method and the cable standard's rating tables. The lug follows the conductor: a 240 mm² aluminium cable takes a 240 mm² aluminium or bimetal lug, never a copper lug sized for the copper equivalent. Remember too that the larger aluminium conductor needs a larger barrel and sometimes a larger bolt, so check that the equipment terminal has room for it before choosing aluminium cable.

Installation

Crimping and installation, step by step

  1. StripStrip insulation to the barrel depth plus a small allowance, without nicking strands.
  2. CleanFor aluminium conductors, wire-brush the strands to break the oxide, then insert immediately into the compound-filled barrel.
  3. CrimpUse the tool and die matched to the lug and conductor. Start at the palm end and work toward the cable, so compound and air are pushed out.
  4. InspectCheck die marks and crimp count, that the barrel is not cracked, and that the conductor is fully inserted. Remove excess compound.
  5. SealFit heat-shrink or cold-shrink over the barrel and cable in damp or outdoor locations.
  6. BoltClean the contact faces, use plated steel bolts with flat and conical spring washers, and tighten to the specified torque.

Common installation mistakes

  • Using a die from another lug maker or for a different conductor class
  • Crimping a bimetal lug on the copper palm or across the friction-weld zone
  • Fitting an aluminium lug to copper cable, or a plain copper lug to aluminium cable
  • Skipping wire-brushing and compound on aluminium strands
  • Bolting aluminium palms with plain washers that let clamp force relax
  • Leaving copper-aluminium contact faces exposed to rain or condensation

Engineering

Why cable terminations fail, and how to prevent it

Most termination failures are not sudden. A joint starts with a slightly high resistance, runs a little warmer than the cable, and each heating and cooling cycle loosens it a little more. Resistance rises, temperature rises with it, and eventually the joint overheats, discolours the insulation and can start a fire. Thermographic surveys of switchboards routinely find this pattern at copper-aluminium joints made without bimetal lugs.

Failure mechanismRoot causePrevention
Galvanic corrosionBare copper and aluminium in contact with moistureBimetal lugs, tin plating, sealed joints
Oxide filmAluminium oxide left on strands or palmWire-brushing, contact compound, plated palms
Under-crimpingWrong die, too few crimps, worn toolMatched tool and die, die-code check, tool calibration
Clamp-force relaxationAluminium creep and different expansion of bolt and palmConical spring washers, correct torque, periodic re-torque where specified
Thermal cycling looseningMixed metals expanding at different ratesMatching metals, long-barrel lugs, adequate contact area
Strand damageNicked strands when strippingProper stripping tools, inspection

IEC 61238-1 reproduces this ageing in the laboratory. Sample joints are cycled between ambient and a high conductor temperature hundreds of times, with short-circuit pulses added, and their resistance is compared with that of an equal length of cable. A lug that passes has shown it can survive decades of load cycles without runaway heating.

Contact area also matters at the bolted end. Two-hole lugs and copper rings increase the area under pressure and reduce current density at the contact spots, which keeps the joint cooler. For aluminium palms, a wide flat washer under a conical spring washer spreads the bolt load and keeps it there as the metal relaxes.

Examples

Typical termination arrangements

Transformer to main switchboard

A distribution transformer with copper bushings feeds a switchboard through parallel aluminium cables. Each cable end gets a long-barrel bimetal lug with a two-hole palm on the copper bushing and at the copper breaker terminals.

Solar farm inverter

Large aluminium AC cables leave string or central inverters with copper terminals. Bimetal lugs at the inverter and aluminium lugs on aluminium busbars in the combiner station keep every joint like-to-like.

Industrial motor

A copper motor cable terminates in the motor box with tin-plated copper lugs; no transition is needed because cable and terminal are both copper.

Building riser

Aluminium rising-main cables feed floor distribution boards. Tin-plated aluminium or bimetal lugs connect to the board busbar depending on its metal and plating.

IEC 61238-1 testing is the key performance proof: the crimped lug and cable are heat-cycled hundreds of times and subjected to short-circuit current, and the joint resistance must stay stable. Ask for type-test evidence for the exact lug, die and conductor combination you will use.

Applications

Where each lug type is used

  • Power distribution: aluminium feeder cables to copper breaker and transformer terminals through bimetal lugs
  • Commercial and residential buildings: aluminium service cables into copper-terminated distribution boards
  • Renewable energy: large aluminium DC and AC cables in solar farms, wind turbines and battery storage
  • Industrial plant: motors, generators, drives and control panels, mostly copper lugs
  • Rail and transport: traction substations and rolling-stock power cables
  • Marine and offshore: tin- or nickel-plated copper lugs for salt atmosphere
  • Telecom and data centres: grounding conductors to copper ground bars, and DC power cabling
  • Mining and heavy industry: long-barrel lugs on trailing and feeder cables subject to vibration

FAQ

Cable lugs: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between copper, aluminium and bimetal lugs?

Copper lugs are for copper cable, aluminium lugs for aluminium cable on aluminium terminals, and bimetal lugs have an aluminium barrel and copper palm so an aluminium cable can be bolted to a copper terminal without a galvanic couple.

Can I use a copper lug on aluminium cable?

Not a plain copper lug. Aluminium strands inside a copper barrel corrode where moisture enters and loosen with thermal cycling. Use an aluminium or bimetal lug, or a tin-plated lug specifically rated for aluminium conductors.

Can aluminium lugs be used with copper conductors?

No. Aluminium lugs are for aluminium conductors only. For any copper-aluminium transition use a bimetal lug or connector.

How do bimetal lugs prevent corrosion?

The copper and aluminium are friction welded into a solid bond with no gap for moisture, and the joint lies outside the crimp zone. The galvanic pair is therefore sealed instead of exposed.

Are bimetal lugs suitable for medium voltage?

Yes. Designs for cables up to about 36 kV are available, provided they are type tested to IEC 61238-1 and used with suitable cable terminations for insulation and stress control.

Why must aluminium conductors be wire-brushed before crimping?

Aluminium forms an insulating oxide within seconds. Brushing breaks it, and the compound in the barrel stops it reforming, so the crimp cold-welds the strands to the barrel with low resistance.

How do I check a crimp is correct?

The die code stamped on the barrel should match the lug, the specified number of crimps should be present, the barrel should be uncracked, and the conductor should be fully inserted. A pull test or resistance check on sample joints confirms the procedure.

Terminating aluminium cable, or connecting it to copper equipment?

Tell us the cable size, terminal metal and quantity. We will recommend aluminium or bimetal lugs for each joint and quote a delivered price.
