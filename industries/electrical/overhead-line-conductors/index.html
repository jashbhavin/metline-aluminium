Overhead line conductors

AAAC: All Aluminium Alloy Conductors for Overhead Lines

AAAC (all aluminium alloy conductor) is a concentric-lay stranded conductor made entirely of heat-treated aluminium-magnesium-silicon alloy wire, usually 6201-T81. With no steel core it is light, corrosion-resistant and free of galvanic attack, yet strong enough to replace ACSR on many distribution and sub-transmission lines. Metline supplies AAAC to ASTM B399, EN 50182, IEC 61089, BS 3242 and DIN 48201 tables, with test certificates and export drums, worldwide.

Overview

What is AAAC?

AAAC was developed to combine the corrosion resistance of all-aluminium conductors with strength close to that of steel-reinforced designs. Every strand is the same heat-treatable alloy, so the conductor behaves as one homogeneous material.

The wire is drawn from 6201 (or the closely related 6101-family alloys used in some national standards), solution heat-treated, drawn and artificially aged to the T81 temper. Magnesium and silicon form fine Mg2Si precipitates that roughly double the strength of pure 1350 wire, at the cost of conductivity falling from about 61 % to 52.5 % IACS.

Because all strands share one expansion coefficient and modulus, AAAC has a simple, predictable sag-tension behaviour. Its hard surface resists nicks and scratches during stringing, which helps keep corona loss and radio interference low on higher-voltage lines. And with no steel inside, there is no galvanic cell to attack the conductor from within in coastal, desert-saline or industrial air.

Where AAAC is the right choice

  • Coastal and industrial areas where steel cores corrode and ACSR life is short
  • Distribution and sub-transmission lines with medium spans
  • Reconductoring where a lighter conductor with similar strength reduces tower and pole loading
  • Lines that need a smooth, damage-resistant surface for low corona and noise

Construction

How AAAC is stranded

AAAC is built from identical round wires laid in concentric layers around a single centre wire, with each layer laid in the opposite direction to the one beneath and the outer layer right-hand unless specified otherwise. Standard constructions follow the 1 + 6 + 12 + 18 + 24 + 30 pattern.

ConstructionLayersTypical conductor sizesTypical use
7 wires (1 + 6)1about 15 – 120 mm²LV and MV distribution
19 wires (1 + 6 + 12)2about 120 – 220 mm²MV distribution, sub-transmission
37 wires (… + 18)3about 220 – 500 mm²Sub-transmission and transmission
61 wires (… + 24)4about 500 – 1,000 mm²Transmission and heavy feeders
91 wires (… + 30)5above about 1,000 mm²Large transmission conductors

Lay ratios, mass per kilometre and resistance include a small allowance for stranding: helically laid wires are slightly longer than the conductor, so a 7-wire conductor weighs and resists about 1 – 2 % more than its straight-wire area suggests.

During stringing, AAAC should be pulled under controlled tension through sheaves with a groove diameter large enough for the conductor, since its hard strands can be marked by undersized or worn sheaves. Allow for initial creep in sag-tension calculations: like all aluminium conductors, AAAC elongates slowly under everyday tension during its first years, and lines are commonly over-tensioned or pre-stressed at installation to compensate.

Claims that AAAC always carries more current than ACSR need care. For the same overall diameter, AAAC and ACSR have similar resistance because AAAC fills the space occupied by the steel core with conducting alloy. Its real advantages are weight, corrosion life and the simplicity of a single material; ACSR's are strength and lower sag on very long spans.

Related alloys

6101, 8xxx and other conductor alloys

6101

An Al-Mg-Si alloy with higher conductivity than 6201 and lower strength. Common for busbar in T6/T61/T63 tempers and used in some conductor specifications.

6201

The standard AAAC and ACAR-core alloy, with the best strength among conductor wires at 52.5 % IACS.

8xxx (8030, 8176)

Al-Fe-based alloys with small copper or other additions, designed for building and distribution cable conductors where creep resistance and termination reliability matter. Not used for overhead AAAC.

Thermal-resistant Al-Zr

For lines that must run at 150 – 230 °C; see our page on heat-resistant aluminium alloy wire.

FAQ

AAAC aluminium (aluminum) alloy conductor: FAQs

What is AAAC made of?

Concentric layers of identical heat-treated aluminium-magnesium-silicon alloy wires, usually 6201-T81. There is no steel or other core material.

Is AAAC better than ACSR?

Neither is better in every case. AAAC is lighter, resists corrosion far better and has a harder surface; ACSR is stronger and sags less on very long spans and under heavy ice. Choose on span length, loading, environment and lifetime cost.

What is the conductivity of AAAC?

About 52.5 % IACS for 6201-T81 wire (resistivity up to 32.84 nΩ·m at 20 °C), or 53 % for the higher-conductivity class. That compares with 61 % for 1350 wire used in AAC and ACSR.

What do code names like Oak, Ash and Yew mean?

They are standard size names from BS 3242 and EN 50182 tables. Each code fixes the stranding, wire diameter and therefore area, mass, strength and resistance, so ordering by code avoids ambiguity.

Can AAAC use the same fittings as ACSR?

Suspension clamps can often be shared if the diameter matches, but compression dead-ends and joints differ because AAAC has no steel core. Use fittings specifically rated for AAAC.

How is AAAC supplied for export?

On wooden or steel drums sized for the stringing equipment, with conductor ends sealed, drum lagging for sea freight, drum lists and test certificates.

Specifying AAAC for a new line or reconductoring?
