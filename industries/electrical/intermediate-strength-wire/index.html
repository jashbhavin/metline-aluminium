Intermediate-Strength Aluminium Alloy Wire & Overhead Conductors

Intermediate-strength aluminium alloy wire sits between pure electrical-grade 1350 wire and high-strength 6201 alloy: it is noticeably stronger than 1350 while keeping conductivity close to 57 – 58.5 % IACS. It is used to build all-alloy conductors that need more span or sag performance than AAC without the resistance penalty of standard AAAC. Overview

Where intermediate-strength wire fits

Every overhead conductor is a compromise between conductivity and strength. Pure aluminium (1350) conducts best but is relatively weak; heat-treated Al-Mg-Si alloy (6201-T81) is twice as strong but loses about 15 % of its conductivity. Intermediate-strength alloys fill the gap between the two.

They are aluminium-magnesium-silicon (6101-type) or iron-bearing compositions with lower alloy content than 6201, processed so that more of the magnesium and silicon stay out of solid solution. The result is a wire with tensile strength typically in the range of about 230 – 290 MPa and conductivity of 57 – 58.5 % IACS, depending on the class and diameter. Some national standards define two classes on exactly those conductivity floors; IEC 62641:2022 also added further alloy designations to the international system alongside the established 1350 and 6201 types.

For a utility, the practical gain is a conductor that runs cooler and with lower losses than standard AAAC at the same current, while keeping much of AAAC's span and sag capability and its freedom from a steel core. That makes it attractive for distribution feeders and sub-transmission lines where both energy losses and pole or tower spacing matter.

Conductor family

AAC, AAAC, ACSR and ACAR compared

AAC – all aluminium conductor

Concentric strands of 1350-H19 only (ASTM B231, IEC 61089 A1). Highest conductivity per unit area and the lowest cost per amp, but limited strength. Best for short urban spans, substation buses and coastal sites where steel would corrode.

AAAC – all aluminium alloy conductor

Strands of 6201-T81 or equivalent heat-treated alloy (ASTM B399, EN 50182). Roughly twice the strength of AAC, a hard surface that resists damage, and no galvanic couple. A common replacement for ACSR on distribution and sub-transmission lines.

ACSR – aluminium conductor steel reinforced

1350 layers around a galvanised or aluminium-clad steel core (ASTM B232, EN 50182). The highest strength-to-cost ratio for long spans, river crossings and heavy ice, at the cost of extra weight and some corrosion risk in the core.

ACAR – aluminium conductor alloy reinforced

1350 outer layers around a 6201 alloy core (ASTM B524). Better conductivity than AAAC and better strength than AAC, with no steel. The mix of wires can be tuned for a given strength-to-resistance target.

Intermediate-strength wire is used either as a single-material all-alloy conductor, giving a low-loss alternative to AAAC, or in combination with 1350 wires in ACAR-style constructions. When you compare offers, ask for rated tensile strength, DC resistance at 20 °C and mass per kilometre for each option, rather than relying on conductor names alone.

Selection

How to choose the right conductor

  1. Start with the loadRequired ampacity at your ambient conditions sets the minimum conductor area. Lower-resistance wire (1350 or intermediate-strength) reduces area or losses for the same current.
  2. Check span and sagSpan length, ice and wind loading and clearance limits set the strength needed. Long spans and heavy ice favour ACSR or AAAC; short spans allow AAC.
  3. Assess the environmentCoastal, desert-saline or industrial atmospheres favour all-aluminium or all-alloy conductors, or aluminium-clad steel cores, over galvanised steel.
  4. Count lifetime lossesA conductor with slightly higher purchase cost but lower resistance often pays back through energy savings over a 40-year life; this is where intermediate-strength alloys earn their place.
  5. Match the hardwareConfirm that clamps, joints and dead-ends are available and qualified for the chosen conductor and its operating temperature.

Quality

Tests, certificates and drum packing

  • Wire tests: diameter, tensile strength, elongation, resistivity, and wrapping or bend tests as the standard requires
  • Conductor tests: lay ratio and direction, mass per kilometre, DC resistance, and rated tensile strength on type tests
  • Surface: clean, smooth strands; grease applied to inner layers or the full conductor where specified for corrosive sites
  • Drums: wooden or steel drums sized to the stringing equipment, with conductor ends secured and lagging for sea freight
  • Documents: EN 10204 3.1 inspection certificate, drum list with lengths and gross/net masses

FAQ

Intermediate-strength wire and aluminum overhead conductors: FAQs

What does "intermediate strength" mean for aluminium conductor wire?

It describes an alloy wire stronger than 1350-H19 (about 160 – 200 MPa) but weaker than 6201-T81 (about 305 – 330 MPa), with conductivity of roughly 57 – 58.5 % IACS instead of 52.5 % for 6201. It trades a little strength for noticeably lower resistance.

Is an intermediate-strength conductor the same as AAAC?

It is an all-alloy conductor, so it belongs to the AAAC family in construction, but the wire has higher conductivity and lower strength than standard 6201-T81 AAAC. Sag-tension calculations must use its own rated strength and modulus.

When should I choose ACSR over an all-alloy conductor?

For long spans, large river or valley crossings, and heavy ice or wind loading, where the steel core's strength allows fewer or lower towers. In corrosive coastal sites and on short to medium spans, AAAC or intermediate-strength all-alloy conductors usually give a longer, maintenance-free life.

Why does the conductor price follow the aluminium market?

Metal is the largest part of the cost. Conductor prices are usually built from the LME aluminium price plus a regional premium, a conversion charge for drawing and stranding, drums and freight.

What information do you need for a quote?

Conductor type and size (or wire alloy and diameter), governing standard, total length and length per drum, drum type, any grease requirement, inspection needs and the delivery destination.
