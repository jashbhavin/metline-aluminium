6061 T6 and T4 Aluminium Forgings for Automotive Use

6061 forgings combine the alloy's good strength, corrosion resistance and machinability with the refined grain flow of a forged part, which gives better fatigue and impact performance than a casting or a part machined from bar. In vehicles, 6061-T6 is the classic alloy for forged wheels and is widely used for suspension, steering and brake components. Metline supplies 6061 die forgings, open-die forgings, rolled rings and forged bar, heat-treated and tested to ASTM, AMS or EN requirements, to customers worldwide.

Overview

Why forge 6061 for automotive parts?

Forging shapes a heated billet between dies so that the metal's grain follows the outline of the part. A forged control arm or wheel spoke has continuous grain flow around radii and through load paths, no internal shrinkage porosity, and a fine, uniform structure. The practical result is higher fatigue strength and toughness than a cast part of the same alloy family and shape.

6061 is one of the most forged aluminium alloys because it works well at forging temperature, responds predictably to solution treatment and ageing, and resists corrosion without coatings. After T6 heat treatment it delivers a minimum of about 260 MPa tensile and 240 MPa yield strength in die forgings, with typical values near 310 MPa and 275 MPa.

For vehicle makers, forged 6061 is a way to cut unsprung mass. Replacing a steel or cast-iron suspension part with a forged aluminium part typically saves around 40 – 50 % of its weight, which improves ride, handling and energy use. In Europe 6082 is often preferred for suspension forgings because of its slightly higher strength; in North America and Asia, 6061 is the more common choice, particularly for wheels.

Advantages of 6061 forgings

  • Directional grain flow for high fatigue life under cyclic road loads
  • Sound internal structure, free of the porosity found in castings
  • Good corrosion resistance in road-salt environments, even uncoated
  • Machines cleanly and anodises or paints well for visible wheels

Process

How 6061 automotive forgings are made

  1. Forging stockHomogenised cast billet or extruded bar is cut to a weight that fills the die with controlled flash.
  2. Pre-heatingStock and dies are heated; 6061 is forged at roughly 430 – 480 °C, with dies kept hot to avoid chilling the metal.
  3. ForgingHydraulic presses, screw presses or hammers form the part in one or more impressions. Wheels are often forged and then flow-formed in the rim area.
  4. TrimmingFlash is trimmed and the part is cleaned.
  5. Solution treatment and quenchAbout 530 °C, followed by a rapid water or polymer quench to hold the alloying elements in solution.
  6. AgeingArtificial ageing at about 160 – 175 °C for several hours develops T6. T4 parts are naturally aged instead.
  7. Machining and finishingBores, faces and threads are machined; parts may be shot-peened, anodised or painted.

The T652 temper adds a small cold compression after quenching to relieve residual stress, which reduces distortion when large forgings are machined.

Quality & testing

Inspection and testing of automotive forgings

Tensile and hardness

Specimens from prolongations or sacrificial parts; hardness on every part or by sampling.

Grain flow

Macro-etched sections confirm grain flow follows the part outline and that there are no laps or flow-through defects.

Ultrasonic

Volumetric inspection to ASTM B594 or AMS-STD-2154 for safety-critical forgings.

Penetrant

Fluorescent penetrant inspection for surface cracks and laps.

Wheel testing

Finished wheels pass cornering fatigue, radial fatigue and impact tests such as SAE J328, SAE J175 or ISO 3006, or JWL in Japan.

Traceability

Heat lot, forging lot and heat-treatment batch are linked on the EN 10204 3.1 certificate.

Applications

Automotive parts made from 6061 forgings

  • Wheels: one-piece forged wheels and forged centres for multi-piece wheels, for cars, SUVs, trucks and buses
  • Suspension: control arms, links, knuckles and shock-absorber mounts
  • Steering: steering arms, yokes and column components
  • Brakes: performance caliper bodies and brackets, where stiffness and low weight matter
  • Driveline: yokes, flanges and pulleys
  • EV and battery systems: mounting brackets and high-load connection points on battery frames

Engine blocks, cylinder heads and intake manifolds are not forged; they are cast from Al-Si casting alloys. Forged pistons use 4032 or 2618 rather than 6061, because those alloys keep strength and dimensional stability at piston temperatures.

FAQ

6061 aluminium (aluminum) forgings: frequently asked questions

What are the minimum properties of 6061-T6 forgings?

ASTM B247 requires about 260 MPa (38 ksi) tensile and 240 MPa (35 ksi) yield strength for 6061-T6 die forgings up to 100 mm thick, with elongation depending on specimen direction. Typical production values are higher, around 310 MPa tensile and 275 MPa yield.

What is the difference between 6061-T4 and T6 forgings?

Both are solution treated and quenched. T4 is aged at room temperature and is softer and more ductile; T6 is artificially aged for maximum strength. Most finished automotive forgings are T6; T4 is used when a part will be formed or straightened before final ageing.

Why are forged wheels made from 6061?

6061 forges well, reaches high strength after T6 treatment, resists corrosion and machines and finishes cleanly. Its fatigue strength allows a lighter wheel than a cast A356 design of the same rating.

Should I choose 6061 or 6082 for a suspension forging?

Both work. 6082 gives slightly higher strength and is the common European choice; 6061 is more common in North America and Asia. The OEM specification and supply region usually decide.

Are 6061 forgings ultrasonically tested?

Safety-critical forgings are commonly tested to ASTM B594 or AMS-STD-2154. Tell us the acceptance class on your drawing and it will be included in the inspection plan.

Can Metline ship forgings internationally?

Yes. Metline supplies 6061 forgings worldwide with EN 10204 3.1 certificates, export packing and EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP terms.
