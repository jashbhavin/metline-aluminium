Embossed Aluminium Heat Shield Sheet for Vehicles

Embossed aluminium heat shield sheet is thin, soft O-temper aluminium (usually 1050, 1060, 1100 or 3003) with a raised hemispherical or stucco pattern rolled into it. The bright surface reflects most radiant heat from exhausts, turbochargers and catalytic converters, while the embossing makes very thin gauges stiff enough to press into shields. Overview

How an embossed aluminium heat shield works

Most of the heat that damages components near an exhaust system arrives as infrared radiation, not by conduction. A clean, bright aluminium surface has very low emissivity, so it reflects the large majority of that radiation and re-emits little of what it absorbs. Placed between a hot exhaust and a fuel line, floor pan or plastic part, a thin aluminium shield can cut the temperature of the protected component dramatically.

Embossing does three jobs. It stiffens foil-thin metal in every direction, so a 0.3 – 0.5 mm sheet behaves like a much thicker flat sheet and resists vibration and drumming. It breaks up the surface so the shield can be deep-drawn into complex shapes without wrinkling badly. And it creates an air gap where the shield is layered against an insulator or a second sheet, which slows conduction.

Shields are made either as a single embossed layer or as a sandwich: two embossed aluminium skins around a glass-fibre, ceramic-fibre or mineral mat, crimped at the edges. Aluminium shields are used where the shield itself stays below roughly 300 – 400 °C; closer to manifolds and turbochargers, aluminised or stainless steel is used for the hot face.

What buyers specify

  • Alloy and temper: 1050 / 1060 / 1100 for maximum formability, 3003 for extra strength at temperature
  • Base gauge and embossed height, which together set stiffness and formability
  • Pattern type (hemispherical, stucco, diamond) and its orientation to the rolling direction
  • Surface cleanliness and lubricant suitable for deep drawing and later crimping

Thermal properties relevant to shielding

Melting range
about 640 – 660 °C (1xxx); 643 – 654 °C (3003)
Thermal emissivity
low on clean, bright sheet (in the order of 0.05 – 0.1); rises as the surface oxidises or becomes dirty
Thermal conductivity
about 190 – 230 W/m·K, which spreads hot spots across the shield
Softening
O temper is already annealed, so heat does not cause further strength loss below about 300 °C; creep becomes relevant above this

Some marketing material quotes radiant ratings above 1,000 °C and direct-contact ratings near 600 °C for aluminium shields. Those figures describe insulated multi-layer assemblies at a stand-off distance, not the sheet itself; aluminium melts at about 650 °C, so the shield must always be designed with an air gap from the heat source.

Fabrication

Pressing, crimping and assembly

Deep drawing

O-temper 1xxx sheet stretches easily, but embossing reduces the effective elongation. Generous radii and draw beads keep the pattern even and avoid splitting at dimple tops.

Edge crimping

Multi-layer shields are closed by folding or hemming the edges of the skins. Soft 1050 / 1060 folds tightly without cracking.

Fastening

Shields are bolted, clipped or riveted, often with steel grommets or spacers to keep the air gap and isolate vibration.

Galvanic contact

Use coated fasteners or isolating washers where an aluminium shield meets bare steel in a wet underbody zone.

Acoustic role

Embossed and perforated shields also absorb or damp noise; perforation patterns can be combined with embossing.

Recycling

Single-alloy shields are easy to recycle; multi-layer shields need the insulating mat separated.

Applications

Where embossed heat shields are used

  • Exhaust and catalytic converter shields: under-floor tunnel and silencer shields protecting the floor pan and cabin
  • Turbocharger and manifold surrounds: outer aluminium layers over a steel hot face
  • Fuel tank and fuel line shields: keeping fuel below vapour-lock temperatures
  • Engine bay and firewall: bulkhead and dash insulators combining heat and noise control
  • Steering rack, starter and brake lines: small local shields near hot components
  • EV applications: thermal barriers between battery modules, inverters and the cabin floor
  • Outside automotive: generator sets, ovens, tunnel linings and industrial pipe insulation cladding

FAQ

Embossed aluminium (aluminum) heat shields: frequently asked questions

Which alloy is best for an embossed heat shield?

1050, 1060 and 1100 in the O temper give the best formability for deep-drawn shields. 3003-O is about 30 % stronger and holds its strength better at temperature, so it suits larger or hotter shields.

What temperature can an aluminium heat shield withstand?

The sheet itself should stay below roughly 300 – 400 °C in service and never approach its melting range of about 650 °C. Shields work by reflecting radiant heat across an air gap; hotter positions use aluminised or stainless steel.

Why is heat shield aluminium embossed?

Embossing stiffens thin sheet in every direction, reduces vibration noise, helps deep drawing and creates air gaps in layered shields. It allows a 0.3 – 0.5 mm sheet to do the job of a much thicker flat sheet.

What thickness is used for automotive heat shields?

Most pressed shields use 0.3 – 0.8 mm base gauge before embossing; thinner foil is used for wraps and thicker gauges up to about 1.5 mm for structural shields.

Does the shield stay reflective in service?

Reflectivity falls as the surface oxidises and collects road dirt, but aluminium oxide is thin and the shield remains effective. Keeping the hot face clean and dry helps.

Can Metline supply embossed coil for progressive presses?

Yes. We supply embossed coil slit to width, or cut blanks, worldwide with mill certificates and export packing.
