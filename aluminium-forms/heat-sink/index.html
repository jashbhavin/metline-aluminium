Aluminium Heat Sink Extrusions: 6063 & 6061

An aluminium heat sink extrusion is a finned profile, usually in 6063-T5 or 6063-T6, that pulls heat out of a power device and passes it to the surrounding air. Overview

What an extruded aluminium heat sink does

A heat sink is a heat exchanger in its simplest form: a solid base that collects heat from a component, and fins that spread that heat over a large surface where air can carry it away. Extrusion makes the base and the fins in one piece, so there is no joint between them to add thermal resistance.

Aluminium dominates this market because it combines good thermal conductivity with low density, low cost and easy extrusion. Copper conducts roughly twice as well, but it is more than three times as heavy and far harder to extrude into thin fins. For most power electronics, LED lighting and drive enclosures, an aluminium profile with well-chosen fin geometry outperforms a heavier copper part of the same cost.

The important point for buyers is that alloy conductivity is only one link in the thermal chain. Cooling performance is set by the whole path: junction to case, the thermal interface material, spreading through the base, conduction along each fin, and convection from the fin surface to the air. A heat sink in a higher-conductivity alloy with poor fin spacing for its airflow will run hotter than a well-designed 6063 profile. The material is chosen to make a good geometry possible, and the geometry does most of the work.

Why 6063 is the default heat sink alloy

  • High thermal conductivity for a heat-treatable alloy: about 200 – 209 W/m·K in T5/T6
  • Extrudes easily into thin, tall fins with sharp detail and a smooth surface
  • Press-quenched T5 temper needs no separate furnace treatment, which keeps cost down
  • Anodises evenly, so black anodising for better emissivity is straightforward

Profile design

Fin geometry and extrusion design rules

The die, not the alloy, sets the practical limits of a heat sink profile. Tall, thin, closely spaced fins create long die "tongues" that can deflect or break under extrusion pressure. The guidance below reflects common extrusion practice; the final limits depend on the profile size, the circle diameter of the die and the press, so treat them as starting points to confirm with die design.

Design featureCommon starting pointWhy it matters
Fin aspect ratio (fin height ÷ gap)about 6:1 – 10:1 for standard dies; higher needs special tooling or bonded finsTall narrow gaps weaken the die tongue and raise scrap
Minimum fin thicknessabout 1.0 – 1.5 mm at the tip for most sizesThinner fins tear or vary in thickness along the length
Fin taperThicker at the root than at the tipImproves conduction where heat flow is highest and strengthens the die
Base thicknessThick enough to spread heat from the source footprint; typically several times the fin thicknessA thin base leaves fins far from the heat source doing little work
Fin spacingWider for natural convection, closer for forced airToo tight a gap chokes natural airflow; too wide wastes surface in fan-cooled designs
Mounting faceMachine if interface flatness is criticalAs-extruded flatness is not good enough for thin thermal interface layers

Extruded

One-piece profile with integral fins. Lowest cost per metre once the die exists; the right choice for most LED, drive and inverter cooling.

Bonded or brazed fin

Fins inserted into a grooved base and bonded with epoxy or brazed. Allows much higher fin density and taller fins than a single extrusion.

Skived

Fins sliced and lifted from a solid block of 1xxx or 6063 aluminium (or copper). Very thin, dense fins with no interface.

Folded fin

Corrugated sheet fins brazed or bonded to a base. High surface area for ducted, fan-driven airflow.

Cold-forged pin fin

Pins forged from 1050/1070 or 6063 slugs. Works well where airflow direction is uncertain.

Machined plate

Liquid cold plates and heat spreaders machined from 6061 or 6082 plate, often with pressed tube or friction-stir welded lids.

Dimensional tolerances for extruded heat sinks follow EN 755-9 (profiles) or ANSI H35.2 for ASTM B221 orders. These standards control cross-section, straightness, twist and flatness, but the flatness they allow is coarse compared with a 0.1 mm thermal interface. When a power module sits on the base, specify a machined mounting face with a flatness tolerance and surface roughness on the drawing.

Theoretical weight is the net metal cross-section in mm² multiplied by 0.0027 kg per metre. A heat sink with a 2,400 mm² section therefore weighs about 6.5 kg per metre. Use the actual net area from the CAD model, not the envelope, because fins and gaps can make the envelope several times larger than the metal area.

Surface finish

Anodising, coating and the interface

Bare extruded aluminium has very low emissivity, so it radiates little heat. Black anodising lifts emissivity to around 0.8 – 0.9, which helps noticeably in natural convection, where radiation can carry a meaningful share of the heat. In fan-cooled designs convection dominates and the finish matters much less, so clear anodising or a conversion coating is often enough.

Anodic film is an electrical insulator and a thin thermal barrier. Where a device must be earthed through the heat sink, or where the interface must be as thin as possible, mask the mounting face or machine it after anodising. Trivalent or chromate-free conversion coatings protect the surface while keeping it electrically conductive.

  • Clean the mounting face of oil and oxide before applying interface material
  • Specify flatness and roughness on the contact face rather than relying on the extrusion standard
  • Choose the interface (grease, pad, phase-change material) to suit the clamping force and the flatness achieved
  • Orient fins vertically in natural convection so warm air can rise freely between them
  • Protect fins in transit with partitioned packing; bent fins disturb airflow and look like damage
Applications

Where aluminium heat sinks are used

  • LED lighting: street lights, high-bay and flood luminaires, where the profile is often also the housing
  • Power electronics: IGBT and MOSFET modules in inverters, motor drives, welding machines and UPS systems
  • Renewable energy: solar string inverters and battery storage converters
  • EV charging and e-mobility: charger power stages, on-board chargers and DC–DC converters
  • Telecom and IT: outdoor radio units, rectifiers and network equipment enclosures
  • Audio and consumer electronics: amplifier heat sinks and fanless PC cases
  • Industrial controls: thyristor stacks, power supplies and rail-mounted control gear

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) heat sink extrusions: FAQ

Which aluminium alloy is best for heat sinks?

6063 in T5 or T6 temper for extruded heat sinks. It has thermal conductivity of about 200 – 209 W/m·K, extrudes into thin fins easily and anodises well. 1050 or 1070 conducts a little better but is too soft for tall extruded fins, and 6061 is stronger but about 20 % less conductive.

Is 6063-T5 or 6063-T6 better for a heat sink?

T5 has slightly higher thermal conductivity and lower cost because it is press-quenched and aged without a separate solution treatment. T6 is stronger. Unless the profile carries mechanical load or clamping force, T5 is the usual choice.

Does black anodising improve heat sink performance?

In natural convection, yes: raising emissivity from about 0.05 – 0.1 to about 0.8 – 0.9 increases radiated heat, which can be a meaningful share of the total. In forced-air designs the gain is small, because convection does most of the work.

How flat is the base of an extruded heat sink?

As-extruded flatness follows EN 755-9 or ANSI H35.2 and is too coarse for a thin thermal interface. Specify a machined mounting face with a flatness and roughness tolerance on the drawing where a power module or LED board sits.

How do I calculate the weight of a heat sink profile?

Multiply the net metal cross-section in mm² by 0.0027 to get kg per metre. A 2,400 mm² section weighs about 6.5 kg/m. Always use the net area of the metal, not the overall envelope.

Can you supply heat sinks cut, machined and anodised?

Yes. Metline supplies heat sink profiles cut to length, CNC machined, anodised or conversion coated and packed to protect the fins, with material certificates, to customers worldwide.
