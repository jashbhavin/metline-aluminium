Aluminium Forged Shafts: Solid, Stepped, Hollow and Flanged

An aluminium forged shaft is a shaft blank shaped by open-die or closed-die forging so that the grain follows its steps, shoulders and flanges, giving better fatigue life than a shaft turned from bar. Metline supplies forged shafts in 6061, 6082, 2014, 7075 and 7050, rough forged, proof machined or finished to drawing, with ultrasonic testing and EN 10204 3.1 certificates, to customers worldwide.

Overview

Why forge an aluminium shaft?

Most small aluminium shafts are simply turned from extruded or rolled round bar. Forging becomes worthwhile when a shaft has large changes in diameter, an integral flange, or a duty where fatigue governs the design. Forging the steps and flanges close to final shape keeps the grain flowing around each shoulder instead of cutting through it, and saves the metal that would otherwise be machined away from a bar as large as the biggest diameter.

In a shaft machined from bar, the grain runs straight along the axis. At every shoulder, where the diameter changes and stress concentrates, the machined surface cuts across the grain ends, and that is exactly where fatigue cracks start. In a forged shaft the grain bends with the contour of the shoulder or flange, so the most heavily stressed surface is parallel to the grain. Forging also consolidates any residual porosity in the cast billet, which improves the ultrasonic quality of large diameters.

Aluminium shafts are chosen for low rotating mass and inertia, corrosion resistance and non-magnetic behaviour: propeller and drive shafts for motorsport and drones, rotor shafts for lightweight motors, actuator and linkage shafts in aircraft, rollers and spindles in packaging and printing machines, and shafts for marine and food equipment that must not rust.

Forged shaft or bar-stock shaft?

  • Choose bar for simple, short, low-volume shafts with small diameter steps
  • Choose a forging when the largest diameter is much bigger than the body, as with flanged or collared shafts
  • Choose a forging when fatigue loads dominate and the drawing calls for grain flow or UT class AA/A
  • Choose a hollow forging when weight or inertia matters and the bore is large
Shaft types

Types of aluminium forged shafts

Stepped shaft

Several diameters forged in one piece for bearing seats, seal faces and gear hubs. Open-die forging with stepped tooling produces the profile; final diameters are turned.

Flanged shaft

An integral flange upset at one or both ends for a bolted coupling, removing a welded or shrink-fitted flange and its failure point.

Hollow shaft

Forged over a mandrel or bored after forging to cut weight and inertia. For a given outside diameter a tube loses little torsional stiffness while losing much of its mass.

Rotor and spindle shaft

Balanced, precision-machined shafts for small motors, drones, fans and spindles, usually in 7075 or 2014 for strength at small diameters.

Eccentric and cranked shaft

Offset journals forged close to shape in closed dies, used in light pumps, compressors and mechanisms.

Shaft blank / forged bar

A simple forged round with a machining allowance, for customers who do all the turning in-house. See forged bar.

Alloy selection

Which aluminium alloy for a forged shaft?

Shafts are usually sized by torsional and bending fatigue, so fatigue strength matters as much as tensile strength. Engineering

Design considerations for aluminium shafts

Stiffness

Aluminium has about one-third the elastic modulus of steel (69 GPa against 200 GPa; shear modulus about 26 GPa). An aluminium shaft of the same diameter twists and deflects three times as much, so aluminium shafts are usually larger in diameter, often hollow, to recover stiffness and critical speed.

Critical speed

Whirling speed depends on stiffness divided by mass. Because aluminium is a third as stiff but also a third as dense as steel, a larger-diameter aluminium tube can reach the same or a higher critical speed than a steel shaft, which is why long propeller shafts are often aluminium.

Bearing journals

Aluminium is soft compared with bearing steel. Use bearings with a steel inner race, press on a steel sleeve, or hard anodise the journal (Type III, typically 25 – 50 µm) for wear and fretting resistance.

Thermal expansion

Aluminium expands about twice as much as steel (23 against 12 µm/m·°C). Interference fits with steel bearings loosen as the assembly warms, so fits must be checked at the operating temperature.

Stress raisers

Generous fillet radii at shoulders, rolled rather than cut threads where possible, and smooth keyway ends matter more in aluminium than in steel because fatigue strength is notch sensitive.

Galvanic contact

Where an aluminium shaft meets steel gears or couplings in a wet environment, isolate the metals or seal the joint to avoid galvanic corrosion of the aluminium.

Forged shafts are quoted with a machining allowance on every diameter and face, enough to remove the forging skin, oxide and any surface laps and to correct the distortion that follows quenching. FAQ

Aluminium forged shafts: frequently asked questions

Is a forged aluminium shaft stronger than one machined from bar?

Its static strength is similar for the same alloy and temper, but its fatigue performance at shoulders and flanges is better because the grain follows the contour instead of being cut through. Forging also reduces material waste when the shaft has large steps or flanges.

Which aluminium alloy is best for a drive shaft?

6061-T6 is the economical, corrosion-resistant choice. 7075-T6 gives the highest strength and allows thinner walls; 7075-T73 is preferred where stress-corrosion cracking is a concern. 2014-T6 is a long-established forging alloy with high strength.

Can aluminium shafts run in bearings directly?

Aluminium is too soft for most plain-bearing journals. Rolling-element bearings with a steel inner race work well; for wear surfaces, use a steel sleeve or hard anodise the journal.

Can you supply hollow forged shafts?

Yes. Hollow shafts are forged over a mandrel or deep-hole bored after forging. A hollow section keeps most of the torsional stiffness of a solid shaft with much lower weight and inertia.

Do you supply finished shafts or only forgings?

Both. Shafts can be supplied rough forged, proof machined for ultrasonic inspection, or finish machined with keyways, splines, threads and balancing to your drawing.

Why is aluminium not used for heavy industrial shafts?

Steel is about three times stiffer and has higher fatigue strength, so heavily loaded turbine, mill and gearbox shafts are steel. Aluminium is chosen where low mass, low inertia or corrosion resistance is the priority.
