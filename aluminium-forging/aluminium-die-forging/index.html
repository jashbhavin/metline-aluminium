Aluminium Die Forgings for Chassis, Housings and Structural Parts

An aluminium die forging is a part pressed between shaped steel dies so that the metal fills a cavity close to the finished geometry, with grain flow that follows the contour of the part. Compared with a casting or a part machined from plate, it gives higher fatigue strength, no porosity and less machining. Metline supplies closed-die forgings in 6061, 6082, 2014, 7075, 5083 and other alloys, as forged, heat treated or machined, to customers worldwide.

Overview

What is an aluminium die forging?

In closed-die (impression-die) forging, a heated aluminium billet is pressed between an upper and a lower die that together hold the negative shape of the part. The metal flows to fill the cavity, a thin flash escapes at the parting line and is trimmed off, and the forging is then heat treated and machined where needed.

Three things make a die forging different from the alternatives. First, the deformation closes any internal voids, so there is none of the gas or shrinkage porosity that limits castings. Second, the grain structure is stretched and aligned along the outline of the part, so ribs, bosses and lugs carry load along the grain rather than across it. Third, the part comes out near net shape, so a forging that would need 70 % of a plate blank machined away often needs only a few millimetres per face.

The trade-off is tooling. A set of forging dies is a real investment, and each new shape needs its own. Die forging therefore makes most sense for repeat parts, safety-critical parts and parts where weight matters enough to justify the die cost. For one-off or very low quantities, a hand (open-die) forging or forged block machined to shape is usually the better route.

Die forging is usually the right choice when

  • The part is fatigue- or safety-critical (suspension, steering, pressure, lifting)
  • A casting is too heavy or too variable, or a weldment is not strong enough
  • Annual volume justifies dedicated dies, typically from a few hundred pieces upward
  • Machining a plate or bar blank would waste most of the material
How it is made

The aluminium closed-die forging route

  1. Forging stockCast and homogenised billet or extruded bar is sawn to a calculated weight. Extruded stock gives finer, more uniform grain for small, thin-walled parts.
  2. PreheatingStock is heated in a controlled furnace, commonly about 430 – 480 °C for 6061 and 6082 and about 380 – 440 °C for 7075. Aluminium has a narrow forging window, so temperature control is essential.
  3. Heated diesUnlike steel forging, the dies are preheated (often a few hundred degrees Celsius on hydraulic presses) so the metal does not chill and crack on contact.
  4. Blocker and finisherComplex parts are pre-shaped in a blocker die, then completed in a finisher die. Hydraulic presses are preferred for aluminium because slower strain rates help the metal fill deep ribs.
  5. TrimmingFlash is trimmed and the part is cleaned, deburred and visually inspected.
  6. Heat treatmentSolution treatment, quench and ageing to T6, T73 or another temper; T652 or T7352 add cold compression to relieve quench stress.
  7. FinishingShot blasting, machining of functional faces and holes, inspection, and surface treatment as specified.

Designing for forging

Design guidelines for aluminium die forgings

Good forging design lowers die cost, reduces machining and improves properties. These are general guidelines; we review every drawing before quoting and suggest changes where they help.

FeatureGuideline for aluminiumWhy it matters
Parting lineOne flat plane where possible, through the largest sectionSimpler dies, less flash, predictable grain flow
Draft angleCommonly about 3 – 7° on conventional forgings; 0 – 1° possible on precision (no-draft) forgingsLets the part release from the die
Corner and fillet radiiGenerous; aluminium needs larger radii than steel for equivalent ribsSmall radii cause laps, folds and die wear
Ribs and websAvoid very thin, tall ribs; keep webs uniformThin sections chill and resist filling
Machining allowanceTypically a few millimetres per machined face, more on large partsCovers scale, draft and die wear
HolesForge as dimples or blind recesses; pierce or drill through holesThrough holes complicate dies
UndercutsAvoid; machine them afterwardsThe forging must eject from a two-part die

Blocker-type forging

Generous radii and drafts, thick sections; lowest die cost, most machining. Suited to low volumes and prototypes.

Conventional forging

The most common type: moderate draft and radii, ribs and webs close to final shape, machining on functional faces only.

Precision forging

Thin webs, sharp detail and little or no draft; some surfaces can be used as forged. Highest die cost, least machining.

A typical case is a cast suspension arm being redesigned as a forging: the forged 6082-T6 version can use thinner sections because there is no porosity to allow for, often saving a meaningful share of the weight while improving fatigue life. Another common case is a hydraulic manifold where castings leak through porosity after machining; a forged 6061-T6 block removes that failure mode.

Tolerances & inspection

Tolerances, testing and certification

Die-forging dimensional tolerances depend on size, weight and complexity. ANSI H35.2 gives standard tolerances for aluminium die forgings (length, width, thickness, die closure, mismatch, flash extension, straightness and flatness); tighter values are agreed on the drawing for machined features.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) die forgings: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between die forging and open-die forging?

Die (closed-die) forging presses the metal into a cavity shaped like the part, giving near-net shape and contoured grain flow but needing dedicated dies. Open-die forging works the metal between flat or simple tools into blocks, bars, discs and rings, with no part-specific tooling.

Which alloy is best for an aluminium die forging?

6082-T6 or 6061-T6 for most automotive and industrial parts, 5083 where seawater corrosion or welding matters, 2014-T6 for heavily loaded parts, and 7075-T73 or 7050-T74 for aerospace fittings.

Are aluminium die forgings stronger than castings?

Yes. A forged 6061-T6 or 6082-T6 part is free of porosity and has aligned grain, so its fatigue strength and ductility are clearly higher than a typical aluminium casting. That usually allows thinner walls and a lighter part.

What draft angle should I put on my aluminium forging drawing?

Conventional aluminium die forgings commonly use around 3 – 7° on external walls and slightly more on internal walls. Precision forgings can work with 0 – 1°. We confirm draft during the drawing review.

What quantity justifies die forging?

It depends on part size and complexity, but dies generally pay back from a few hundred pieces per year upward. For smaller quantities, consider a forged block or bar machined to shape.

Can die forgings be supplied machined and anodised?

Yes. Forgings can be delivered as forged, heat treated, rough machined or finish machined, with anodising, hard anodising or conversion coating as specified.

What certificates come with die forgings?

An EN 10204 3.1 certificate with chemistry, heat-treatment and mechanical test results is standard; ultrasonic, dimensional and first-article reports are added when ordered.

Have a part that should be forged?

Send the drawing, alloy, quantity and test requirements. We will review the design for forging and reply with a route, inspection plan and delivered price.
