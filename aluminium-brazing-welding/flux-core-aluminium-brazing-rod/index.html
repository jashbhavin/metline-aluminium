Flux Core Aluminium Brazing Rod

A flux core aluminium brazing rod is a hollow filler-metal rod or wire filled with brazing flux, so the flux is delivered exactly where and when the filler melts. It joins aluminium to aluminium, and with zinc-aluminium grades also aluminium to copper, without brushing on a separate flux. Metline supplies Al-Si (4047-type) and Zn-Al flux-cored rods, wire and rings with standard and caesium-modified fluxes, worldwide.

Overview

How a flux-cored aluminium rod works

Aluminium is covered by a tough oxide film that melts at over 2000 °C, far above any brazing temperature. Without a flux to break that film, molten filler will not wet the parent metal. A flux-cored rod carries the flux inside the filler, so the two arrive at the joint together.

As the joint heats, the flux in the core melts first, flows onto the surface and dissolves the oxide. A few tens of degrees higher, the filler sheath melts and is drawn into the joint by capillary action. Because the flux is sealed inside the rod, it is not knocked off during handling, it does not absorb moisture as quickly as an open paste, and the quantity applied is the same every time.

These rods braze rather than weld: the parent aluminium never melts. That distinction matters for repairs on thin sheet, tube and castings, where a TIG weld can burn through or distort the part. In maintenance shops the products are often called "aluminium welding rods", but the joint is a brazed one and must be designed with a lap or socket to give the filler a capillary gap to fill.

Why use flux-cored rather than bare rod and paste

  • No separate fluxing step, so less labour and less risk of a missed joint
  • Consistent flux-to-filler ratio, which gives repeatable joints on production lines
  • Less excess flux, so less residue to clean and lower flux consumption
  • Works with a simple propane, air-acetylene or oxy-fuel torch, or with induction heating

Melting ranges are nominal for the filler alloy. The standard K-Al-F flux melts at roughly 565 – 572 °C; caesium additions lower the flux melting point and improve tolerance of magnesium. Confirm the exact range on the product data sheet for each lot.

4047 (BAlSi-4 in AWS A5.8) is near the aluminium-silicon eutectic, so it melts over a very narrow range and flows freely. 4045 (BAlSi-5, about 10 % Si) melts over a slightly wider range and is sometimes preferred where a little more control is wanted. Zinc-aluminium fillers melt about 100 – 200 °C lower, which gives a much wider safety margin below the melting point of the parent metal.

Compatibility

Procedure

How to braze aluminium with a flux-cored rod

  1. Design the jointUse a lap, socket or sleeve joint with a gap of about 0.1 – 0.25 mm. A butt joint relies on the fillet alone and is much weaker.
  2. CleanDegrease, then brush with a stainless-steel brush used only on aluminium. Oil and heavy oxide stop the flux working.
  3. Heat the parent metal broadlyUse a soft, neutral flame and keep it moving. Heat the thicker part first. Aluminium does not glow before it melts, so watch the flux: when the rod tip touched to the joint shows flux melting and spreading, the joint is nearly hot enough.
  4. Let the work melt the rodTouch the rod to the joint, not the flame. When the parent metal is at temperature the filler melts and runs into the gap. Feed until a continuous fillet forms.
  5. Stop heating promptlyOverheating erodes the parent metal (the silicon in 4047 dissolves aluminium) and can melt thin walls. With Zn-Al rods, prolonged heat on Cu-Al joints grows brittle intermetallic layers.
  6. Cool and cleanCool in still air. Non-corrosive K-Al-F residue can stay on most parts; chloride flux residue must be washed off with hot water.

Safety

  • Work in good ventilation or with fume extraction: fluoride fluxes release fumes when heated
  • Wear eye protection and heat-resistant gloves
  • Store rods dry and sealed; damp flux spits and leaves porosity

Dissimilar joints

Flux-cored rods for copper-to-aluminium joints

Air-conditioner and refrigerator makers increasingly join aluminium tube or coils to copper pipework. A Zn-Al flux-cored filler is the usual method: it melts well below the aluminium tube, wets copper and aluminium with a caesium flux, and keeps the time at temperature short enough to limit brittle copper-aluminium intermetallic layers.

  • Design the joint so the aluminium tube fits over or inside the copper with a uniform capillary gap
  • Heat mainly the copper side, which conducts heat well and tolerates higher temperature
  • Keep brazing time short; a thick, grey interface layer is a sign of overheating
  • Protect the finished joint from moisture with a sleeve or coating, because copper and aluminium form a galvanic couple

Flux-cored rings in Zn-Al are widely used on production lines for these joints. Read more in our guide to brazing aluminium to copper.

FAQ

Flux core aluminium (aluminum) brazing rods: FAQ

Do I need to add flux when using a flux-cored rod?

No. The flux inside the rod is enough for normal joints. On heavily oxidised or contaminated parts, clean better rather than adding more flux.

What temperature does a flux-cored aluminium rod work at?

Al-Si (4047-type) rods melt at about 577 – 582 °C and are used with the joint at roughly 585 – 600 °C. Zinc-aluminium rods work much lower, at roughly 400 – 500 °C depending on aluminium content.

Can flux-cored rods join aluminium to copper?

Yes, with a zinc-aluminium rod and caesium-type flux. Al-Si rods are not normally used for Cu-Al joints because their temperature is too close to the melting point of the aluminium tube.

Can I braze 6061 with a 4047 flux-cored rod?

It is difficult: 6061 starts to melt at about 582 °C, almost the same as 4047. Use a zinc-aluminium rod for 6061, or design the joint so heat can be controlled very closely.

Is flux residue corrosive?

Residue from potassium or caesium fluoroaluminate fluxes is non-corrosive and can usually be left in place. Chloride-containing fluxes are corrosive and their residue must be washed off thoroughly.

Can these rods be used on 2024 or 7075?

No. High-strength 2xxx and 7xxx alloys start to melt at or below the working temperature of the filler, and their magnesium and zinc content interferes with the flux. Join them mechanically or by special welding processes.
