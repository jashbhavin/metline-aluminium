Brazing Rings & Preforms

A brazing ring or preform is filler metal formed into the exact shape and quantity one joint needs, so every joint on a production line gets the same amount of alloy in the same place. Metline supplies rings, washers, shims, strips and drawing-specific preforms in aluminium-silicon (4047, 4045) and zinc-aluminium alloys to AWS A5.8 and ISO 17672, to customers worldwide; silver and copper-based fillers are covered here for comparison.

Overview

What brazing preforms do, and why production lines use them

Hand-fed rod depends on the operator: one joint gets too little alloy and leaks, the next gets too much and leaves icicles inside the tube. A preform removes that variable. The filler is weighed out once, at the design stage, and then placed on the part before heating.

The commonest preform is a wire ring slipped over a tube before it enters a socket or fitting. When the assembly reaches brazing temperature, the ring melts and capillary action draws the liquid alloy into the clearance between tube and socket. Because the ring sits in a fixed position and holds a fixed volume, joint quality becomes a matter of temperature and time, which are easy to control on an induction coil, a tunnel furnace or a rotary flame machine.

Preforms also make automation practical. Rings can be loaded by vibratory feeders, washers can be stacked in fixtures, and strip can be fed from a coil, so a line can braze hundreds of identical joints per hour without an operator feeding rod. For manual work the benefit is different but just as real: less wasted alloy, fewer reworked joints, and a clear count of filler consumed per assembly for costing.

Benefits of pre-placed filler

  • The same alloy volume at every joint, so leak rates and fillet size stay consistent
  • Less filler used than with hand-fed rod, which adds up quickly on high-volume lines
  • Shorter cycle times and easy automation on induction, furnace and flame lines
  • Filler placed inside blind or hidden joints that a rod cannot reach
  • Simple process records: one preform per joint, alloy and lot traceable
Filler alloys

Brazing ring alloys: which family suits which metals

The base metals decide the alloy family; the heating method and service conditions then narrow the grade. Metline supplies the aluminium-based families, aluminium-silicon and zinc-aluminium; silver, phos-copper and copper fillers appear in the table only for comparison, because many assemblies that carry aluminium rings also have copper joints brazed with those alloys. Silver rings

Lowest working temperature of the high-strength fillers and the widest range of base metals. BAg-7 flows almost like a eutectic and is the usual choice for stainless steel and food or medical equipment; BAg-24 adds nickel for wetting on steel and carbide. Cadmium-free grades are now the norm for new designs.

Phos-copper rings

The standard for copper refrigeration and air-conditioning circuits. Phosphorus deoxidises copper, so copper-to-copper joints need no flux. Never use them on steel, stainless or nickel alloys: the phosphorus forms brittle phosphides at the interface.

Copper rings

Used almost only in controlled-atmosphere furnaces, where pure copper flows into very tight, even press-fit clearances on steel parts. The result is a strong, clean joint with no flux residue, common for hydraulic fittings and automotive steel assemblies.

Aluminium-silicon rings

4047 and 4045 melt about 50 – 60 °C below most aluminium alloys, which is the working window for flame, induction or controlled-atmosphere brazing of aluminium tube, fittings and heat-exchanger headers.

Zinc-aluminium rings fill the gap between these families. Because Zn-22Al is fully liquid at about 482 °C, well below the solidus of 3003 or 6063, it lets an aluminium tube be joined to a copper stub or service valve without the risk of melting the aluminium wall. That makes it the usual aluminium-based choice for mixed aluminium-copper refrigeration circuits, and the alloy Metline recommends when a drawing pairs aluminium tube with a copper fitting.

Engineering

How to size a brazing ring for a tube joint

A ring should hold enough alloy to fill the joint clearance completely and leave a small fillet at the mouth, plus an allowance for alloy that wets the surrounding surfaces. Too little filler leaves voids; too much runs into the bore or leaves a heavy bead that wastes filler.

  1. Calculate the clearance volumeVolume ≈ π × mean joint diameter × socket depth × radial clearance. For a 15.88 mm tube in a socket 12 mm deep with 0.075 mm clearance, that is π × 15.95 × 12 × 0.075 ≈ 45 mm³.
  2. Add the fillet and lossesAdd roughly 25 – 50 % for the fillet and wetting. In the example, about 60 – 70 mm³ of filler is needed.
  3. Choose the ring geometryRing volume = π × mean ring diameter × wire cross-section. A ring with about 17 mm mean diameter in 1.3 mm wire holds about 71 mm³, which fits the target; 1.0 mm wire (about 42 mm³) would be short.
  4. Set the ring positionPlace the ring at the socket mouth on the side that heats last, so the alloy is drawn toward the hotter metal and through the joint.
  5. Prove itSection a few trial joints and check fill. Adjust the wire diameter in small steps rather than changing alloy or heat first.
Filler familyRecommended joint clearance (radial)
Silver (BAg) with flux0.025 – 0.125 mm
Phos-copper (BCuP)0.025 – 0.125 mm
Copper (BCu), furnace atmosphere0 – 0.05 mm (light press fit works)

Flux

Bare, flux-coated and flux-cored rings

Bare rings

No flux on the ring. Use them with brushed or dispensed paste flux, in controlled-atmosphere or vacuum furnaces, or as phos-copper rings on copper-to-copper joints that are self-fluxing.

Flux-coated rings

A thin, bonded flux layer on the outside of the wire. The flux becomes active early in the heating cycle and protects the joint area as it heats, which suits flame and induction brazing of silver alloys.

Flux-cored rings

Flux sealed inside a seamed or seamless wire. It survives handling and feeding without chipping, releases at the moment the alloy melts and leaves less residue, so it suits automated lines and aluminium brazing.

Flux chemistry follows the alloy. Silver and copper-based fillers use fluoride-borate fluxes active up to roughly 800 – 900 °C (AWS type FB3 family). Aluminium fillers need non-corrosive potassium fluoroaluminate flux, often with caesium additions when the base alloy contains more magnesium. For aluminium and aluminium-to-copper joints, tell us your base metals and heating method and we will match flux and Al-Si or Zn-Al alloy as one system.

Applications

Where brazing rings and preforms are used

  • Air conditioning and refrigeration: copper return bends, distributor tubes, compressor and valve connections, mostly with BCuP-2 or BCuP-3 rings; silver rings for copper-to-steel or brass joints
  • Aluminium heat exchangers: 4047 or 4045 rings for tube-to-header and fitting joints on condensers, evaporators and charge-air coolers
  • Aluminium-to-copper refrigeration joints: Zn-Al rings for aluminium tube joined to copper stubs, service valves and compressor connections
  • Automotive and hydraulics: steel tube and fitting assemblies furnace-brazed with copper rings; fuel and brake line ends
  • Electrical: busbar, terminal and contact assemblies brazed with silver or phos-copper washers and shims for low-resistance joints
  • Tooling: carbide tips brazed to steel shanks using silver shims or tri-metal (silver-copper-silver) preforms that absorb thermal stress
  • Food, medical and laboratory equipment: cadmium-free silver rings on stainless steel and copper

FAQ

Brazing rings and preforms: frequently asked questions

Does every brazing ring need flux?

No. Phos-copper rings are self-fluxing on copper-to-copper joints, and copper or silver rings used in a hydrogen, nitrogen-hydrogen or vacuum furnace need no flux. Silver rings brazed in air and aluminium-silicon rings both need flux, either applied separately or built into a coated or cored ring.

What is the difference between a brazing ring and a preform?

A ring is one type of preform. "Preform" covers every pre-shaped piece of filler: rings, washers, shims, foil cut-outs, strip and custom stampings. Rings suit round tube and pin joints; the other shapes suit flat, area or irregular joints.

Can phos-copper rings be used on copper-to-brass joints?

Yes, but brass contains zinc, whose oxide the phosphorus cannot remove, so a flux is needed. For copper-to-steel or copper-to-stainless joints, use a silver alloy instead, because phosphorus embrittles iron and nickel.

How do I work out the right ring size?

Calculate the volume of the joint clearance, add 25 – 50 % for the fillet and wetting, then choose a wire diameter whose ring volume matches. The worked example above shows a 15.88 mm tube needing a ring of about 1.3 mm wire.

Flux-coated or flux-cored: which should I choose?

Coated rings release flux early and suit flame brazing, where the joint area needs protection while it heats. Cored rings protect the flux during handling and feeding and leave less residue, which suits automated induction lines and aluminium brazing.

Can you supply small sample quantities of a custom aluminium brazing ring?

Yes, for Al-Si and Zn-Al alloys. Send the drawing or ring dimensions, alloy and expected annual use, and we will quote samples for qualification together with production pricing.

Need aluminium brazing rings for a production line?

Send your joint drawing, base metals, heating method and annual quantity. We will propose the Al-Si or Zn-Al alloy, ring size and flux option and quote samples and volume supply.
