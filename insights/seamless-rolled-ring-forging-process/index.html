The Seamless Rolled Ring Forging Process, Step by Step

A seamless rolled ring starts as a solid billet that is upset, pierced into a doughnut and then squeezed between a driven main roll and an idling mandrel while axial rolls control its height, so the ring grows in diameter with grain flow running around the circumference. For aluminium, the result is a weld-free ring with better fatigue and toughness than one cut from plate or welded from bar.

Definition

What is a seamless rolled ring?

Ring rolling is a hot-forging process that converts a thick-walled pierced preform into a thinner-walled ring of larger diameter. Because the ring is never cut open and rejoined, there is no seam, weld or heat-affected zone anywhere around the circumference.

The decisive benefit is grain flow. During rolling the metal is elongated in the hoop direction, so the cast structure is broken up and grains align circumferentially. Hoop stress in a flange, bearing race or pressure ring then acts along the strongest direction of the material. A ring sawn from plate, by contrast, has the rolling direction running straight across it, so half of its circumference is loaded across the grain.

In aluminium, rolled rings typically range from small bearing-sized parts to diameters of several metres, used where weight matters as much as strength: aerospace engine and fuselage frames, launch-vehicle tank joints, satellite interfaces, rail and machinery slewing rings, and large flanges for vacuum and cryogenic equipment.

Step by step

The nine stages of making an aluminium rolled ring

  1. Raw material selectionBillet is cut from direct-chill cast ingot or from a pre-forged bar of the specified alloy. Ingot chemistry and hydrogen content are controlled, and for aerospace work the stock is ultrasonically inspected before forging.
  2. HeatingBillets are soaked uniformly in an electric or gas furnace to the alloy's forging window. Aluminium has a narrow range: too cold and it cracks, too hot and grain-boundary melting begins. Tools are preheated too, because cold dies chill the surface.
  3. Upsetting (cogging)On an open-die press the billet is compressed axially into a thick pancake. This breaks up the cast structure and sets the volume and height for the next steps.
  4. PiercingA punch drives through the centre to create the hole, producing a doughnut-shaped preform. The thin wad left at the bottom is punched or sheared out.
  5. Preform check and reheatingPreform dimensions are measured against the rolling plan, and the part returns to the furnace so it enters the ring mill at forging temperature.
  6. Ring rollingThe preform is threaded over the mandrel. The main roll drives the ring and presses it against the mandrel, thinning the wall; conical axial rolls press on the top and bottom faces to control height. Wall reduction forces the diameter to grow until a measuring roll signals the target size.
  7. Heat treatmentFor heat-treatable alloys: solution treatment, rapid quench, optional stress relief by controlled compression or cold expansion (Tx52 tempers), then artificial ageing to T6, T652, T73 or T7352 as specified.
  8. MachiningRings are rough or finish turned, faced, drilled and contoured to the drawing. Leaving a sensible machining allowance protects against surface defects and quench distortion.
  9. Inspection and certificationDimensional check, hardness and conductivity survey, tensile tests from prolongations in the specified directions, ultrasonic inspection and, where required, macro-etch for grain flow. Results go on an EN 10204 3.1 certificate.

Typical forging temperature ranges for ring alloys

The ranges below are the commonly published working windows for metal temperature (ASM forging data). Actual practice is set by the forger's qualified procedure and the governing specification.

Aluminium cools fast and has a narrow window, so ring mills for aluminium work quickly and often reheat between rolling stages. Overheating risks incipient melting that no later heat treatment can repair.

Methods compared

Ring rolling methods and when each is used

MethodWhat it controlsStrengthsTypical products
Radial rollingWall thickness only; height freeSimple, fast, low tooling costSmall and medium rings with modest height tolerance
Radial-axial rollingWall and height togetherBest dimensional control and closest to net shapeAerospace frames, bearing races, precision flanges
Profile (contour) rollingShaped cross-section from profiled rollsLess machining, better grain flow at contoursFlanged, stepped and L-section rings
Vertical and horizontal mill layoutsOrientation of the ring during rollingVertical suits very large, heavy rings; horizontal suits general sizesLarge-diameter structural rings and general rings

Radial-axial machines dominate high-quality aluminium work because controlling height as well as wall reduces the machining allowance, which saves expensive alloy and keeps more of the favourable grain flow in the finished part.

Comparison

Rolled ring vs plate-cut, welded and cast rings

RouteGrain flowJoint or seamMaterial yieldBest for

Open-die forged ring (saddle / mandrel)Mostly circumferentialNoneMediumThick-walled, low-volume rings

Our view: when a ring carries cyclic hoop load, pressure or impact, pay for a rolled ring. For brackets and spacers with little load, a plate-cut ring is cheaper and quicker. For the difference between forged and rolled rings specifically, see forged ring vs seamless rolled ring.

Practical advice

Common mistakes when specifying rolled rings

Ignoring test direction

Properties differ by direction. Tangential is normally strongest; axial and radial are lower. Specify which directions must meet minimums.

Too tight a forged size

Under-allowing for machining risks unclean-up and leaves quench-stressed skin in the part. Agree allowance with the supplier.

Wrong temper for thick walls

7075-T6 in heavy sections is prone to stress-corrosion cracking; T73 or T7352, or 7050-T74, is the safer choice.

Skipping stress relief

Thin rings distort when machined after quench. T652 or T7352 tempers reduce movement.

FAQ

Seamless rolled rings: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a rolled ring and a forged ring?

Both start from a pierced preform. A forged ring is expanded on a saddle or mandrel under a press, while a rolled ring is formed continuously between rolls. Rolling gives closer tolerances, less machining and more uniform circumferential grain flow.

Which aluminium alloys are used for rolled rings?

6061 for general engineering, 7075 and 7050 for high strength, 2014 and 2219 for aerospace and cryogenic tanks, and 5083 where seawater or cryogenic toughness matters without heat treatment.

What does T652 mean on a ring forging?

Solution heat treated, stress relieved by a controlled amount of cold compression or expansion, then artificially aged. It has T6-level strength with lower residual stress, so the ring moves less during machining.

Why are rolled rings stronger than rings cut from plate?

Because the grain flows around the ring. Hoop stresses act along the grain, which improves fatigue life and toughness. A plate-cut ring is loaded across the grain over much of its circumference.

How are rolled rings inspected?

Typically by dimensional check, hardness and conductivity survey, tensile tests in the specified directions and ultrasonic inspection to a class in AMS-STD-2154 or similar, with results on a mill certificate.

Can Metline supply rings in steel or titanium?

No. Metline supplies aluminium only, including rolled rings, forged discs and forging stock in all common aerospace and engineering alloys.
