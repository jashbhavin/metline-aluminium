Aviation Manufacturers and the Aircraft Aluminium Supply Chain

A handful of airframe makers, led by Airbus and Boeing, sit at the top of a long supply chain of tier-one structure builders, machine shops, MRO providers and metal suppliers. Aircraft aluminium reaches them as plate, sheet, extrusions, bar and forgings in a small family of 2xxx, 6xxx and 7xxx alloys, bought to AMS or EN aerospace specifications with full traceability. This article maps that chain, the alloys it runs on and what to check before choosing a supplier.

Overview

Why aluminium still matters to aviation manufacturers

Composites get the headlines, but aluminium alloys still make up most of the structural weight of the narrowbody families that dominate airline fleets and order books, and they remain essential even on composite-intensive widebodies.

The Boeing 787 and Airbus A350 use carbon-fibre composites for roughly half of their structural weight, yet aluminium is still used for frames, floor structures, leading edges, fittings, seat tracks and countless brackets. On aircraft such as the A320 family and the 737, aluminium alloys form the majority of the fuselage, wing and empennage structure. Add business jets, regional aircraft, helicopters, satellites, launch vehicles and the ground equipment that supports them, and aerospace remains one of the most demanding markets for high-strength aluminium.

The reasons are practical. Aluminium alloys offer a well-understood combination of specific strength, fatigue performance, damage tolerance and corrosion behaviour, decades of certification data, a mature repair base, and a cost per kilogram far below titanium or carbon-fibre structure. Newer aluminium-lithium alloys such as 2050, 2195 and 2099 have pushed density down by several per cent while improving stiffness, which has kept aluminium competitive in new designs.

What makes aerospace aluminium different from commercial grades

  • Tighter chemistry limits, especially on iron and silicon, to improve fracture toughness
  • Specified tempers such as T351, T7351 and T7451 that balance strength with stress-corrosion resistance
  • Mandatory ultrasonic inspection of thick plate, bar and forgings
  • Full heat and lot traceability from the casting to the shipped piece
  • Purchase to AMS, AMS-QQ-A, EN aerospace or customer material specifications

Raw material normally flows down from a small number of qualified primary rolling mills, extruders and forge shops to the OEMs and their tier-one partners under long-term agreements. Service centres and distributors then serve the much larger population of tier-two and tier-three machine shops, MRO stations, tooling makers and ground-equipment builders that need smaller quantities, cut pieces and faster response.

Supply chain

How aircraft aluminium moves from mill to aircraft

  1. Primary productionRolling mills, extruders and forge shops cast ingot or billet, then roll, extrude or forge it and solution heat-treat, stretch and age it to the specified temper.
  2. QualificationFor flight hardware, the mill and its process are usually qualified by the OEM; the material is listed on an approved-source or qualified-products list for that specification.
  3. Distribution and processingService centres hold plate, sheet, bar and extrusions, cut to size, and pass on the mill certification and traceability unchanged.
  4. ManufactureMachine shops and tier suppliers mill, form, heat-treat and finish parts, with special processes (heat treatment, anodising, NDT) performed by accredited sources.
  5. Assembly and maintenanceParts are assembled into structures by the tier-one or OEM; MRO organisations later replace and repair them using the same material specifications.

Two consequences matter for buyers. First, the material specification flows down unchanged: a shop machining a wing fitting must buy plate to exactly the specification on the drawing, often from an approved mill. Second, documentation is part of the product. A plate without its certificate and heat number is, for aerospace purposes, scrap. Quality systems such as AS9100 (aerospace) and accreditation programmes for special processes are common requirements at the manufacturing tiers; for non-flight applications such as tooling, test rigs and ground equipment, ISO 9001 suppliers delivering certified aerospace-specification material are widely used.

Alloys

Aircraft aluminium alloys and where they are used

Alloy and temperKey characteristicsTypical aircraft applications
2024-T3 / T351High fatigue and damage tolerance, moderate strength; usually clad (Alclad) for corrosion protectionFuselage skins, lower wing skins, tension-dominated structure
2124-T851Higher-purity 2024 for thick plate with better toughnessThick machined parts, bulkheads
2219-T87 / T851Weldable, good at cryogenic and elevated temperatureLaunch-vehicle propellant tanks, high-temperature structure
7075-T6 / T651Very high strength, lower toughness and SCC resistance in T6Fittings, upper wing structure on older designs, machined parts
7075-T73 / T7351Over-aged for much better stress-corrosion resistance at some strength costForgings, thick fittings, landing-gear parts
7050-T7451High strength in thick sections with good toughness and SCC resistanceWing spars, ribs, bulkheads, frames machined from thick plate
7475-T7351 / T761Controlled-impurity 7075 variant with high fracture toughnessFuselage and wing skins, damage-tolerant structure
6061-T6 / T651Medium strength, weldable, corrosion resistantSecondary structure, ducting, fittings, ground equipment
Al-Li alloys (2050, 2195, 2099, 2196)Lower density, higher stiffness, good fatigueModern fuselage and wing structure, space tanks

Alloy choice in airframes is a trade between strength, fracture toughness, fatigue crack growth and stress-corrosion resistance. 2xxx alloys tend to win where tension and fatigue dominate, such as fuselage skins and lower wing covers, while 7xxx alloys are preferred where compression and static strength dominate, such as upper wing covers and highly loaded fittings. Over-aged T7x tempers trade a little strength for far better resistance to stress-corrosion cracking and exfoliation, which is why modern designs specify T7351, T7451 or T7651 rather than T6 for thick parts.

Buyer checklist

Seven checks before choosing an aircraft aluminium supplier

1. Specification coverage

Can the supplier certify to the exact specification and revision on your drawing, including tempers such as T7451 and thickness bands?

2. Certification and traceability

Every piece should be traceable to a heat and lot, with an EN 10204 3.1 (or equivalent) certificate showing chemistry, mechanicals and conformance to the specification.

3. Source of material

For flight hardware, ask whether the material comes from a mill qualified for that specification and whether the original mill certificate is supplied.

4. Inspection

Confirm ultrasonic class, flatness, dimensional checks and how non-conforming material is handled and recorded.

5. Quality system

Check the supplier's certification (ISO 9001, AS9100 or equivalent) against what your customer and application require.

6. Processing capability

Cut-to-size plate, saw-cut bar and blanks reduce your scrap and machining time; check cutting tolerance and how cut pieces keep their traceability marks.

7. Logistics and packing

Aerospace plate is damaged easily. Ask about interleaving, protective film, export crating and how documents travel with the shipment.

A useful rule: the more critical the part, the shorter and more qualified the supply chain should be. For primary structure, buy from or through the qualified mill route your customer specifies. For tooling, jigs, test rigs, ground support equipment, prototypes, interior parts and many MRO needs, a certified distributor that holds aerospace-specification material and can cut it to size is often the faster and more economical route.

FAQ

Aircraft aluminium (aluminum) supply: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium alloys are used most in aircraft?

2024 (fuselage skins and tension structure), 7075 and 7050 (wing structure, fittings and thick machined parts), 7475 (damage-tolerant skins), 6061 (secondary structure and fittings) and, increasingly, aluminium-lithium alloys such as 2050 and 2099.

What is the difference between 7075-T6 and 7075-T7351?

T6 is peak-aged for maximum strength. T7351 is over-aged and stress-relieved by stretching; it is somewhat weaker but far more resistant to stress-corrosion cracking and exfoliation, so it is preferred for thick plate and fittings.

Do aircraft parts have to be made from material from an approved mill?

For flight hardware, usually yes: OEM and customer requirements often limit sources to mills qualified for the specification. For tooling, ground equipment and many non-flight uses, certified material to the same specification from a distributor is normally acceptable.

What certification comes with aerospace aluminium plate?

A certificate to EN 10204 3.1 or equivalent showing heat and lot number, chemical analysis, tensile results, temper and conformance to the ordered specification, plus ultrasonic inspection results where the specification requires them.

Is aluminium being replaced by composites in aircraft?

Partly on new widebodies, where composites form about half the structure, but aluminium remains dominant in narrowbody and regional aircraft and is still widely used on composite aircraft for frames, floors and fittings.

Can Metline cut aerospace aluminum plate to size?

Yes. We supply aerospace-specification plate, bar, extrusions and forgings and can saw-cut plate and bar to size, keeping traceability marks and certificates with each piece.
