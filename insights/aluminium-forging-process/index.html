The Aluminium Forging Process: Methods, Routes and Temperature Windows

Forging shapes aluminium by pressing or hammering a heated billet so that the metal flows into the required form, with a grain structure that follows the part outline. It is the process of choice for parts that carry cyclic loads, must be light and cannot be allowed to fail. This guide explains how to pick a forging method, which temperatures each alloy needs, what goes wrong and how a forging moves from billet to finished part.

Starting point

When should a part be forged?

A forging is not always the cheapest route, but it is often the most reliable one. Compared with machining from plate or bar, forging aligns the grain flow with the part contour, closes internal porosity and puts material only where it is needed. Compared with casting, it delivers much higher ductility, toughness and fatigue strength.

In our experience, forging deserves a serious look when one or more of the following applies:

  • Load-bearing and fatigue-sensitive: the part sees long-term cyclic loading along a clear load path, such as suspension arms, wheel hubs, landing-gear and engine-mount fittings.
  • Weight pressure: mass must come down without reducing reliability or service life, as in aircraft, EVs, motorsport and robotics.
  • High failure cost: safety-critical duty, harsh environments or expensive downtime where a hidden casting defect is unacceptable.
  • Machining is wasteful: hogging the part from solid would turn most of the billet into chips, and batch-to-batch consistency is hard to hold.

If none of these apply, an extrusion, a casting or a part machined from plate may be cheaper. The rest of this guide assumes forging is on the shortlist and helps narrow it down to a specific method.

Process selection

How part size, complexity and volume decide the method

Two inputs narrow the choice quickly: the size and geometric complexity of the part, and the annual volume and need for repeatability. The matrix below is a first filter, not a substitute for an engineering review. It removes the obviously unsuitable options so that design and quoting can focus on two or three realistic routes.

Low volume (prototypes, tens to hundreds)High volume (thousands and more)
Large and simple (shafts, flanges, blocks, discs)Open-die forging; hand forgings machined to shapeOpen-die preforms followed by closed-die or ring rolling
Rings and annular partsOpen-die forged rings (punch and saddle)Seamless ring rolling

Small and complex, tight tolerancesClosed-die forging plus CNC machiningPrecision (near-net) forging, cold forging, forge-extrusion

Open-die forging

Billet worked between flat or simple dies by upsetting, drawing out and piercing. No part-specific tooling, so ideal for large parts, low volumes and preforms.

Closed-die (impression-die) forging

Billet pressed or hammered into a two-part die cavity. Excess metal escapes as flash and is trimmed. The workhorse for structural parts.

Precision forging

Closed-die forging with thin ribs, small draft and tight tolerances, so little machining is needed. Higher tooling cost, lower per-part cost at volume.

Ring rolling

A pierced preform is rolled between a driven roll and an idler, growing in diameter while the wall thins. Produces seamless rings with circumferential grain flow.

Cold forging

Room-temperature upsetting, extrusion and coining of soft alloys. Excellent dimensional consistency for small, high-volume parts.

Special routes

Isothermal forging, radial (rotary) forging and combined forge-extrusion for difficult shapes, long shafts and deep cavities.

Method 1

Open-die forging: large preforms, shafts and flanges

In open-die forging the billet is shaped by repeated blows or squeezes between open dies, with the operator or manipulator turning and repositioning it between passes. Typical operations are upsetting (reducing height and increasing diameter), drawing out (lengthening and reducing section) and piercing followed by hole expansion.

Because the tooling is generic, open-die forging suits one-off and low-volume parts, very large sections and preforms for later closed-die forging or ring rolling. The trade-off is larger machining allowances and more dependence on operator skill.

Rules of thumb used in open-die work

Total forging ratio
A cumulative reduction of about 3 – 5 or more is commonly specified to break down the cast structure; the drawing or specification governs
Upsetting stability
Keep billet height to no more than about 2.5 × diameter to avoid buckling
Piercing allowance
Allow extra height (often 10 – 20 %) when piercing and expanding deep holes, then confirm on the forming plan
Typical products
Forged bar, blocks, discs, stepped shafts, large flanges, ring preforms

Hand forgings are also the usual way to qualify a new part: a block forged to approximate shape is machined to the final drawing, giving forged properties without waiting for impression dies.

Method 2

Closed-die forging: complex structural parts

Closed-die forging squeezes a heated billet into cavities machined in an upper and lower die. The metal fills the cavity and the surplus flows out along the parting line as flash, which is trimmed off. Most structural forgings, from automotive control arms to aircraft brackets, are made this way, usually in two or more stages: a blocker or pre-forge stage that distributes the metal, followed by a finishing stage that fills the details.

Control pointWhy it matters
Billet volume and distributionToo little metal underfills the cavity; too much increases flash, forging load and die wear
Pre-forge to finish-forge sequenceMoving material first and filling detail second avoids laps and folds
Temperature and lubricationCold metal cracks and underfills; hot metal with patchy lubrication sticks and galls
Flash land and gutter designControls back-pressure, cavity fill and die life
Draft, fillets and rib proportionsGenerous radii and adequate draft help metal flow and release

Aluminium is forged on both hammers and presses. Hydraulic and mechanical presses dominate because their slower, controlled stroke suits aluminium's strain-rate sensitivity and allows heated dies. Hammers remain in use for smaller parts. Compared with machining the same part from bar, a closed-die forging gives more consistent properties and much less scrap. See our aluminium die forgings page for supply details.

Method 3

Precision forging: closer to finished dimensions

Precision (near-net) forging is closed-die forging taken further: thinner ribs and webs, smaller draft angles (often close to zero), tighter radii and tighter dimensional tolerances. Some surfaces can be used as forged, so machining is limited to interfaces, bores and sealing faces.

The process relies on heated dies, tightly controlled billet weight and temperature, and good lubrication. Tooling and process development cost more, so precision forging pays off when the volume is high enough, when machining time is a large share of part cost or when the forged surface and uninterrupted grain flow at the surface add fatigue value.

Typical candidates include aerospace brackets and fittings, compressor and pump components, connecting parts for robotics and EV structural nodes. See precision aluminium forgings.

Method 4

Seamless ring rolling

Ring rolling starts with an upset and pierced doughnut-shaped preform. On the ring mill, a driven main roll and an idler (mandrel) squeeze the wall while axial rolls control height. The ring grows in diameter as the wall thins, with grain flow running around the circumference, which is exactly the direction of the hoop stress in most ring applications.

Ring rolling uses far less material than cutting a ring from plate and produces rings from small bearing races up to several metres in diameter. Wall-thickness uniformity and roundness depend on preform sizing, pass schedule and steady temperature. Profiled (contoured) rings reduce machining further.

Common uses are flange rings, bearing and gear rings, pressure-vessel and tank rings, rocket and launch-vehicle structures, and wind and power-transmission components. See forged and rolled rings and our comparison of forged rings vs seamless rolled rings.

Method 5

Cold forging and other special routes

Cold forging forms the metal at or near room temperature by upsetting, forward or backward extrusion and coining. It suits small, high-volume parts with tight dimensional consistency: cold-forged heat sinks in 1xxx alloys, bushings, fasteners, connectors and housings. Work hardening during forming raises strength, and surface finish is excellent.

Heat-treatable alloys such as 6061, 2xxx and 7xxx can be cold forged only in a soft, annealed condition, usually through several stations or with intermediate anneals, followed by solution treatment and ageing. Large parts, thin walls and deep cavities are generally poor candidates. Success depends on three things: a forming load the press can deliver, stable lubrication (often a phosphate or polymer coating plus lubricant) and acceptable die life. When those cannot be controlled, warm or hot forging gives a wider window. See aluminium cold forging.

Isothermal forging

Dies are heated to about the billet temperature so the metal does not chill during slow forming. Allows very thin sections and complex aerospace shapes, at higher die cost.

Radial / rotary forging

Several hammers strike a bar or tube radially to reduce diameter and lengthen it. Used for stepped shafts, axles and long preforms.

Forge-extrusion

Combines closed-die forging with forward or backward extrusion to form deep cups, thin walls and splines in one sequence, improving material use.

From billet to part

Three typical process routes

Methods turn into routes: the sequence of operations a forging passes through before delivery. Three common examples:

Route 1: closed-die structural parts

Cut billet → heat → pre-forge → finish-forge → trim flash → solution treat, quench and age → straighten or size → inspection and NDT → machining.

Key controls: preform volume distribution, which decides whether the finish die fills without laps or local overload; and finish-forging temperature and transfer time, which decide crack risk and surface quality.

Route 2: open-die large parts and preforms

Cut billet → heat → upset and draw out in several heats → rough machine or saw → heat treat → ultrasonic inspection → machine.

Key controls: total reduction and its distribution through the section, reheating between passes so the metal never falls below the finish temperature, and stress relief for thick sections.

Route 3: seamless rolled rings

Cut billet → heat → upset → pierce → ring roll → heat treat → inspection → machine.

Key controls: preform size and pass schedule for wall uniformity and roundness, and a steady temperature so the ring rolls evenly around its circumference.

Heat treatment of forgings typically follows AMS 2770 or the equivalent customer specification. Thick 7xxx forgings are often supplied in T73 or T74 (T7452 for stress-relieved die forgings) to improve resistance to stress-corrosion cracking.

Process window

Forging temperature: think in windows, not set points

Aluminium alloys have a narrow forging range. Too cold and the metal cracks or will not fill the die; too hot and it risks incipient melting at grain boundaries, coarse grains and surface defects. Forging temperature is therefore best specified as a window: a start temperature at which the billet enters the press, and a finish temperature that must not be undercut during the last critical deformation.

Die temperature matters as much as billet temperature, because aluminium loses heat quickly to cold steel. Dies for aluminium are normally preheated: modestly for hammers and ring mills, and much hotter, often several hundred degrees, for press forging, which is one reason presses produce thinner sections. In isothermal forging the dies are held at about the billet temperature.

Cold forging

Room temperature. Small, high-volume parts in soft alloys or annealed tempers; high forming loads and very stable lubrication required.

Warm forging

Above room temperature but below recrystallisation. Lower loads than cold forging with good dimensional control; research shows useful formability gains for 7075 in a warm range around 140 – 220 °C.

Hot forging

Within the windows above. Largest deformation, deep cavities, thin ribs and big parts; the normal route for high-strength alloys.

Beyond temperature

Strain rate and lubrication change the picture

At the same temperature, the result depends on how fast the metal is deformed and how much friction it meets. Aluminium's flow stress rises with strain rate, so the same part may fill on a slow hydraulic press but crack or underfill under a hammer. High-strength 7xxx alloys have flow stresses well above those of carbon steel at forging temperature, while 1100 and 6061 are much easier to move.

Friction raises forging load and makes it harder to fill ribs and corners. Graphite-based lubricants, applied evenly to the dies, are standard for hot aluminium forging. Without effective lubrication the friction coefficient can be several times higher than with it, and die sticking and surface tearing follow.

A simple way to remember it

  • Too cold plus too fast or too aggressive a reduction tends to crack
  • Too hot plus uneven lubrication tends to stick, gall and tear the surface
  • Uneven heating produces both problems in different places on the same part

Coarse recrystallised grain at the surface is a particular concern in 6xxx and 2xxx forgings because it lowers fatigue strength and can show as "orange peel" after machining. It is controlled by the combination of forging temperature, strain and the solution heat treatment that follows. When a defect appears, check heating uniformity, transfer consistency and die thermal state before simply raising the overall temperature.

Project advice

Where early engagement saves the most time

  1. Before the drawing is frozenRun a design-for-manufacture review: parting line, draft, rib and web proportions, fillet radii, machining allowances and datum features. Small changes here cost far less than reworking dies later.
  2. During samplingDo not stop at "dimensions OK". Validate the temperature window, transfer time and die thermal state, typically by trial forgings at the cool, middle and hot sides of the window to find the defect boundaries.
  3. First-article approvalSection a forging to confirm grain flow, check mechanical properties in the critical directions and run NDT, as aerospace and automotive customers often require.
  4. Volume productionHold the qualified window and timing, monitor die wear and track properties lot by lot so output stays stable.

To quote a forging project efficiently, we need the drawing or 3D model with critical sections and tolerances, the alloy and temper, annual volume, the target standard and inspection requirements (heat treatment, NDT, surface) and any critical load cases or assembly constraints.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) forging process: frequently asked questions

What temperature is aluminium forged at?

Most wrought alloys are hot forged between roughly 360 and 480 °C, depending on the alloy. 6061 is typically forged at about 430 – 480 °C and 7075 at about 380 – 440 °C. Dies are preheated so the metal does not chill on contact.

Is forged aluminum stronger than cast aluminum?

Yes. Forging closes porosity and aligns grain flow with the part shape, so forgings have much higher ductility, toughness and fatigue strength than castings of similar composition.

Which aluminium alloys are best for forging?

6061 and 6082 for general structural parts, 2014 and 2618 for heavy-duty and elevated-temperature parts, and 7075, 7050 and 7175 for maximum strength. 1xxx and 3xxx alloys suit cold forging.

What is the difference between open-die and closed-die forging?

Open-die forging shapes metal between simple dies without enclosing it, suiting large, simple parts and low volumes. Closed-die forging fills a shaped cavity, producing complex near-net parts at higher volumes with part-specific tooling.

Do aluminium forgings need heat treatment?

Heat-treatable alloys (2xxx, 6xxx, 7xxx) are solution treated, quenched and aged after forging to reach tempers such as T6, T652, T73 or T7452. Non-heat-treatable alloys are used as forged or annealed.

How is forging quality checked?

By dimensional inspection, hardness and tensile tests from prolongations or sacrificial parts, macro-etch sections for grain flow, and ultrasonic or penetrant testing for internal and surface defects.
