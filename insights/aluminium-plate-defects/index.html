Aluminium Plate Defects: What Causes Them and How to Catch Them

Most aluminium plate defects start long before the plate reaches you: in the melt, the cast slab, the hot mill, the cold mill, the furnace or the packing line. Knowing where a defect comes from tells you whether it is cosmetic, whether it can be machined away, and whether the plate should be rejected. This guide sorts the common defects into dimensional, shape, surface, internal and property faults, explains each one in plain terms, and ends with a practical inspection and claims checklist for buyers.

Context

Why plate defects matter more than they look

A scratch on a plate destined for a machined base is irrelevant; the same scratch on a plate that will be bright-anodised for a façade is a rejection. Defects only make sense against the end use, which is why a good purchase order states the surface class, flatness and inspection level rather than simply "no defects".

Rolled aluminium plate travels a long route. Metal is melted, alloyed, degassed and filtered, then direct-chill (DC) cast into a slab that may weigh many tonnes. The slab is scalped (its surface milled off), homogenised, hot-rolled in several reversing passes, sometimes cold-rolled, then heat-treated, quenched, stretched, aged, sawn, inspected and packed. Each stage has its own family of faults, and a defect introduced early is often only revealed late, for example a casting inclusion that shows up as a lamination when the plate is sawn or ultrasonically tested.

For the buyer, the practical questions are always the same. Is the defect within the limits of the governing standard (ASTM B209, EN 485 or the purchase specification)? Does it affect the function of my part? Can it be removed by machining, trimming or re-cutting? And if not, is the evidence good enough to support a claim? The sections below are organised to answer exactly those questions.

Four ways to classify a defect

  • Critical: affects integrity, such as through-porosity, overheating (burning), laminations, cracks and properties below the specified minimum
  • Major: out-of-tolerance thickness, width, length or flatness; corrosion; deep scratches on a functional face
  • Minor: light handling marks, faint roll marks, small dents on a face that will be machined
  • Cosmetic: oil or water staining, dullness, slight colour variation, relevant only where appearance is specified
Dimensions

Dimensional defects: thickness, width, length and cladding

Dimensional faults are the easiest to measure and the least arguable. Tolerances are tabulated by alloy group, thickness and width in ANSI H35.2 (called up by ASTM B209) and in EN 485-3 for hot-rolled and EN 485-4 for cold-rolled products. Always measure with calibrated instruments at the positions the standard defines, normally at least 10 mm in from the edge, because edge thickness on hot-rolled plate is naturally less consistent.

Two points are worth remembering. First, the theoretical weight of plate is 2.70 kg per square metre per millimetre for most 6xxx alloys, about 2.66 for 5083 and 2.80 – 2.81 for 2xxx and 7xxx grades. If invoiced weight is consistently above theoretical weight, the plate is probably running at the top of the thickness tolerance. Second, cladding thickness on Alclad plate is a corrosion-protection requirement, not a cosmetic one; a thin cladding layer is a genuine defect even if the plate looks perfect.

Shape

Shape and flatness defects: waves, bow, camber and twist

Shape defects arise when different parts of the plate are elongated by different amounts during rolling, or when residual stresses are left unbalanced after quenching. If the edges are rolled thinner than the centre, they become longer and must buckle to fit: that is an edge wave. If the centre is rolled thinner, it buckles instead: a centre wave or "full centre". Flatness tolerances for each thickness and width band are given in the same standards as dimensional tolerances.

For heat-treatable plate the most important shape issue is not what you see on delivery but what happens during machining. A plate in T6 temper can be dead flat on arrival and still move when half its thickness is milled away, because quenching leaves the surface in compression and the core in tension. That is why heat-treatable plate for machining is ordered in the stress-relieved tempers T651, T7351 or T7451, where the plate is stretched by roughly 1.5 – 3 % after quenching. If parts warp after machining, check the temper on the certificate first: a T6 plate supplied against a T651 order is a common cause.

Shape defects on thin sheet can often be removed by roller or stretch levelling. On thick plate, levelling capacity is limited and severe bow is usually a reason to reject rather than to straighten.

From the cast slab

Defects that start in the melt and the cast slab

A large share of the serious defects in plate come from the cast slab. Hydrogen dissolves readily in molten aluminium and comes out of solution as the metal solidifies; oxide films and refractory particles become trapped if the melt is not filtered; and the slab surface carries a segregated, oxide-rich skin that must be scalped off before rolling. If any of these steps is weak, the defect is rolled into the plate and stretched out along the rolling direction.

Blisters (surface bubbles)

Raised, rounded bumps, sometimes appearing only after heat treatment. Caused by hydrogen or air trapped just under the surface, or by inadequate scalping that leaves the oxide-rich slab skin in place. Prevented by melt degassing, dry charge materials and adequate scalping depth.

Porosity and through-porosity

Gas pores in the slab that survive rolling as voids, sometimes connected through the thickness. Critical for pressure vessels, vacuum chambers and anything that must be leak-tight. Prevented by in-line degassing (rotary argon or nitrogen units) and hydrogen measurement before casting.

Non-metallic inclusions

Oxide films, spinel or refractory particles embedded in the metal; they appear as streaks, pits after etching or indications in ultrasonic tests. Controlled with ceramic foam or deep-bed filtration and careful launder practice.

Metallic inclusions

Foreign metal particles, for example from tools or contaminated scrap, rolled into the surface. They can cause pitting and anodising spots. Controlled by clean scrap segregation and housekeeping.

Laminations (layering)

Splits running parallel to the surface, usually near mid-thickness, revealed at a sawn edge or by ultrasonic testing. They come from inclusions, oxide layers or shrinkage at the slab centre that are flattened rather than welded during rolling. Always a rejection for structural use.

End and edge cracks in the slab

Cracks at the slab head, foot or edges from casting stresses, especially in high-strength 2xxx and 7xxx alloys. Must be cropped completely; any remnant opens up during hot rolling.

Many of these defects are invisible on the plate surface. For thick plate in aerospace, tooling and pressure applications, buyers therefore specify ultrasonic inspection, typically to ASTM B594 or AMS-STD-2154 with an acceptance class (for example class A or AA). The class sets the largest reflector allowed, so it is far more useful than a contractual phrase such as "free of internal defects".

Hot mill

Hot-rolling defects: cracks, peeling and black marks

Hot rolling breaks down the cast structure and reduces the slab to plate thickness at temperatures typically between about 300 °C and 500 °C, depending on alloy. Too much reduction per pass, a slab that is too hot or too cold, or poor slab preparation all show up as characteristic defects.

DefectAppearanceCause

Transverse surface cracksFine cracks running across the rolling directionPoor slab surface, overheating, or too heavy a pass reduction

Banding and streaks in the structureLight and dark bands visible after etching or anodisingSegregation in the slab, uneven scalping or non-uniform hot-working temperature

Hot-rolling defects matter most when the surface will be anodised or polished, because anodising exaggerates differences in grain structure and intermetallic distribution. If a plate will be decoratively anodised, say so on the order: the mill can then select anodising-quality material with tighter controls on scalping, rolling temperatures and surface.

Cold rolling & finishing

Surface defects from cold rolling, finishing and handling

Cold rolling, levelling and handling produce the widest variety of surface marks. Most are shallow and cosmetic, but they matter for anodised, polished, painted or reflective products, and some reduce corrosion resistance by disturbing the natural oxide.

Stretcher strain marks deserve a special mention because they are easily mistaken for a rolling defect. They are a property of the alloy, appearing in 5xxx alloys such as 5182, 5083 and 5754 when annealed (O temper) material yields unevenly during the first few per cent of strain. They are harmless structurally but visible through paint, which is why outer automotive panels use 6xxx alloys and why some fabricators order 5xxx in H111 or with a light levelling pass.

Furnace & quench

Heat-treatment and chemical-residue defects

Solution heat treatment and annealing bring their own risks. Heat-treatable plate is held close to its solidus temperature during solution treatment, so the margin for error is small, and any oil, water or salt left on the surface reacts with the hot metal.

Overheating (burning)

If solution-treatment temperature exceeds the lowest melting point of the alloy, grain boundaries partially melt. The plate may look normal, but metallography shows rosettes and grain-boundary melting, and ductility collapses. Overheated plate is scrap; it cannot be recovered.

High-temperature oxidation (blistering)

Moisture in the furnace atmosphere drives hydrogen into the surface during solution treatment, causing small blisters. Controlled with dry atmospheres and, in some furnaces, protective additives.

Copper diffusion on clad plate

In Alclad 2xxx plate, excessive time at temperature lets copper diffuse from the core into the cladding, seen as brownish spots and lowering corrosion protection. Prevented by limiting heat-treatment time and re-solution cycles.

Oil stains (annealing stains)

Residual rolling oil carbonises in the annealing furnace, leaving brown, grey or rainbow streaks. Prevented by controlled oil films, clean-burning lubricants and pre-cleaning.

Water stains

White or dark marks where quench water dried on the plate, or where condensation formed between stacked plates. Prevented by complete drying and dry storage.

Nitrate (salt bath) residues

Where salt-bath solution treatment is still used, incompletely washed nitrate leaves white patches that attack the metal. Prevented by thorough rinsing.

Quench distortion

Uneven quench rate warps the plate and leaves high residual stress. Corrected by stretching (T651, T7351) on thick plate.

Abrasion between plates in the quench

Curved rub marks where plates touch while hot. Prevented by spacing and racking.

Performance

Property and performance defects

Some of the most expensive defects are invisible: the plate looks perfect and is dimensionally correct, but its properties do not meet the specification. These are detected only by testing, which is why the mill test certificate matters.

For 5xxx marine plate there is an extra test. 5083-H116 and H321, 5086 and 5456 plate for hulls must pass the intergranular corrosion test ASTM G67 (nitric acid mass loss) and the exfoliation test ASTM G66, as required by ASTM B928. If a marine plate certificate does not show these results, ask for them before fabrication starts.

Practical checklist

How to inspect aluminium plate on delivery

A structured receiving inspection catches most problems before they become expensive. The steps below take very little time for an ordinary consignment and give you the evidence you need if something is wrong.

  1. Check the packing before openingPhotograph the cases or pallets as they arrive. Look for water damage, crushed corners and broken strapping. Note any damage on the delivery receipt before signing.
  2. Match documents to materialCompare heat or lot numbers, alloy, temper and dimensions on the plate markings with the mill test certificate (EN 10204 3.1) and packing list.
  3. Read the certificate properlyConfirm the chemistry and tensile results are within the specification you ordered, that the temper is exactly what you specified (T651, not T6), and that any required tests such as ultrasonic class or ASTM G66/G67 are reported.
  4. Measure dimensionsMeasure thickness at several positions away from the edge, plus width, length and diagonals. Compare with the tolerance tables of the order standard.
  5. Check flatnessLay the plate on a flat surface and measure the maximum gap under a straight edge of the length the standard specifies.
  6. Inspect the surfaceExamine both faces in good, angled light. Peel back protective film in a few areas. Look for corrosion, white staining between plates, scratches and dents on functional faces.
  7. Record and quarantinePhotograph any defect with a scale and the plate marking in view. Keep defective plates separate and do not cut them until the supplier has responded.

Specify these on the purchase order to avoid disputes

  • Alloy, temper and governing standard (for example 6061-T651 to ASTM B209 or 5083-H111 to EN 485)
  • Tolerance class, and any tighter flatness or thickness requirement
  • Surface requirement: mill finish, anodising quality, one or both faces protected by PVC film
  • Ultrasonic inspection standard and class for thick plate
  • Certificate type (EN 10204 3.1 or 3.2) and any third-party inspection
  • Packing: interleaving, desiccant, sea-worthy cases for export
Remedies

What to do if you receive defective plate

Not every defect means the plate is lost. The right response depends on whether the defect is within the standard, whether it affects your part, and whether it can be removed.

Confirm against the standard

First establish whether the condition actually breaches the order standard or contract. Many surface marks are permitted on mill-finish plate unless a special surface was ordered.

Notify promptly with evidence

Send photographs, measurements, the plate and lot numbers and the certificate reference. Prompt, documented notice is essential to any claim.

Machine or trim around it

Edge cracks, local dents or one-sided marks can often be removed by trimming, re-cutting into smaller blanks or machining a few tenths of a millimetre from one face.

Re-level or re-cut

Moderate bow on thinner plate can be corrected by levelling; out-of-square plate can be re-cut to a smaller size if your nesting allows.

Reject what cannot be used

Laminations, through-porosity, overheating, corrosion and properties below specification are rejection grounds. Do not machine such plate for structural parts.

Recycle the rest

Aluminium retains high scrap value and can be remelted indefinitely, so rejected plate should be segregated by alloy for recycling rather than discarded.

Prevention is cheaper than any claim. For critical orders, consider a sample plate or a pre-shipment inspection, define surface and inspection requirements clearly, and keep a record of quality performance by supplier and product. FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) plate defects: frequently asked questions

What is the most common defect in aluminium plate?

Surface handling marks such as scratches, scuffs and water staining are the most frequent, but they are usually cosmetic. The most common functional problem is flatness or distortion after machining, which is often caused by ordering or receiving T6 instead of a stress-relieved temper such as T651.

How can I tell a lamination from a surface scratch?

A lamination is a split parallel to the surface, normally visible as a fine line along a sawn edge, often near mid-thickness, and detectable by ultrasonic testing. A scratch is only on the surface and has no depth into the edge. Laminations are a rejection for structural use; scratches often are not.

Why does my aluminium plate warp when I machine it?

Quenching leaves residual stress in heat-treatable plate. Removing material unbalances those stresses and the plate moves. Order T651, T7351 or T7451 plate, which is stretched after quenching, machine symmetrically where possible, and take finishing cuts after roughing both sides.

Are white stains on aluminium plate a problem?

White staining usually comes from water trapped between plates during transport or storage. Light staining is cosmetic and can be removed, but heavy staining is a form of corrosion that pits the surface. Store plate dry, under cover and off the floor, and never let condensation form inside the packing.

Which standard sets the tolerances for aluminium plate?

For ASTM orders, ASTM B209 refers to ANSI H35.2 tolerance tables; for European orders, EN 485-3 covers hot-rolled and EN 485-4 cold-rolled products. Aerospace specifications may add tighter requirements. The order standard should always be named on the purchase order.

Can defective aluminium plate be repaired?

Some defects can be trimmed, re-cut or machined away, and moderate bow can sometimes be levelled. Overheating, through-porosity, laminations, corrosion and low mechanical properties cannot be repaired and the plate should be rejected and recycled.
