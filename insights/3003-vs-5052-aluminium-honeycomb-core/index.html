3003 vs 5052 Aluminium Honeycomb Core: How to Choose

Choose 3003 honeycomb core for dry, indoor and cost-sensitive panels, and 5052 core when the panel carries real shear load, sees vibration or lives in humid or salty air. The alloy decides foil strength, corrosion life and price, but cell size, foil gauge and density decide most of the panel's actual stiffness, so specify all four together.

The short answer

Which core should you specify?

Most honeycomb-core decisions are made too early on alloy and too late on geometry. Our view: pick the alloy from the service environment and the type of load, then tune density and cell size until the panel meets its shear, crush and flatness targets.

3003 and 5052 are both non-heat-treatable wrought alloys. Neither is strengthened by ageing; both get their foil strength from cold rolling to a hard temper. The difference is the main alloying element. Manganese in 3003 gives a modest strength gain over pure aluminium and keeps the foil very forgiving to roll, expand and form. Magnesium in 5052 dissolves in the aluminium matrix and raises strength considerably, while chromium refines the grain and helps resistance to chloride attack.

In a finished sandwich panel the core carries the transverse shear and keeps the two skins apart. When a panel fails in service, the usual causes are core shear failure, core crushing under a local load, or loss of bond between core and skin after corrosion. 5052 helps with the first and third; density and cell size matter most for the second.

Comparisons assume the same cell size, foil gauge and density. A heavy 3003 core can outperform a light 5052 core in flatwise compression, so always compare like for like.

Bulk mechanical data for the foil helps explain the strength gap. Aluminum Association typical values for full-hard sheet are about 200 MPa tensile and 186 MPa yield for 3003-H18, against about 290 MPa tensile and 255 MPa yield for 5052-H38. In the annealed state the gap is similar in proportion: roughly 42 MPa yield for 3003-O and 90 MPa for 5052-O. Very thin honeycomb foil will not always match these sheet figures exactly, which is why the core's own test data, not the foil datasheet, should drive design.

Core geometry

Cell size, foil gauge and density: the levers that matter most

Honeycomb is made by printing adhesive lines on stacked foil, curing the stack into a block and pulling it open into hexagonal cells (the expansion process), or by corrugating foil and bonding the corrugations for heavier grades. Three numbers then define the core:

Cell size

The distance across the flats of one hexagon, commonly 3.2, 4.8, 6.4, 9.5, 12.7 and 19 mm (1/8 to 3/4 in). Smaller cells support thin skins better and reduce the "telegraphing" of the cell pattern through the face.

Foil gauge

Typically 0.025 – 0.10 mm. Thicker foil raises density and delays the cell-wall buckling that governs crush strength.

Density

Usually quoted in kg/m³ or lb/ft³ (pcf). Common structural cores run from about 30 to 130 kg/m³. Density is the single best predictor of compressive and shear strength.

A useful habit is to write the core as alloy – cell size – foil gauge, for example "5052 – 1/4 in – 0.002 in". That one line fixes the density. Quoting density alone leaves the cell size open, and quoting cell size alone leaves the strength open.

Honeycomb is also directional. The ribbon (L) direction runs along the continuous foil sheets and is stiffer and stronger in shear than the transverse (W) direction. Shear strength in W is often only about 55 – 65 % of the L value for the same core. Lay the core so the main span of the panel bends along L.

Mechanical behaviour

Shear and compressive behaviour: where the alloy shows

Core properties are measured on bonded test specimens with standard methods: ASTM C365 for flatwise compression (bare and stabilised), ASTM C273 for plate shear in L and W, ASTM C297 for flatwise tension and ASTM D1781 (climbing drum) for skin-to-core peel. Ask for these values on the core supplied rather than relying on generic tables.

Compression. In thin-foil cores, crushing starts with elastic and then plastic buckling of the cell walls. Buckling depends heavily on wall thickness relative to cell size, and only partly on the yield strength of the metal. That is why, at equal density, the compressive gap between 3003 and 5052 is often modest.

Shear. Under panel bending, the cell walls carry shear as thin plates. Here the higher yield strength of 5052 foil delays wall wrinkling and permanent deformation, so 5052 cores typically post noticeably higher L and W shear strength at the same geometry. For floors, bulkheads and long-span panels, shear is usually the governing core check, which is why 5052 dominates structural work.

Fatigue and impact. Floors in trains, buses and trailers see millions of load cycles and local point loads from heels, trolleys and cargo. The stronger 5052 foil resists progressive cell-wall damage better, and the denser cores used there limit dents in the skin.

Common specification mistakes

  • Comparing a light 5052 core with a heavy 3003 core and concluding the alloy made no difference
  • Leaving the L direction unspecified, so the core is laid across the main span
  • Specifying a small cell for appearance but forgetting the density (and weight) rise that comes with it
  • Choosing 3003 for an exterior panel and relying on edge sealant alone for corrosion protection
Durability

Corrosion, bonding and long-term durability

Honeycomb foil is extremely thin, so a corrosion pit that would be harmless in a 3 mm sheet can perforate a cell wall. Corrosion at the foil-to-adhesive interface also weakens the skin bond, and debonding is far harder to detect and repair than a dent.

3003 resists ordinary indoor and rural atmospheres well. In coastal air, salt spray, washdown areas or wherever condensation sits inside the panel, it is more prone to pitting than 5052. The magnesium and chromium in 5052 give a more resilient oxide film in chloride environments, which is why marine, rail and aerospace core specifications are built around 5052 (and the stronger 5056).

Whichever alloy you choose, three measures add far more life than the alloy upgrade alone: a chemical conversion coating or primer on the foil before bonding, a compatible structural adhesive, and properly sealed panel edges and fixing holes. Isolate the core from carbon fibre or steel fixings with suitable barriers to prevent galvanic attack.

Processing

Forming, cutting, brazing and cost

Forming

3003 is the softer, more ductile foil, so the core follows curves and local crushing with less tearing. 5052 forms well too, but tight curvature may need an over-expanded or flexible cell configuration.

Cutting and machining

Both alloys slice cleanly on band knives and can be machined to contours with dedicated honeycomb cutters. Unexpanded blocks are easier to machine accurately before expansion.

Brazing

3003 is easily brazed, which suits brazed panels and airflow components. The magnesium in 5052 hampers flux action and wetting in conventional brazing, so 5052 panels are almost always adhesive bonded.

Perforated core

Perforated foil lets air escape during bonding and curing, which helps avoid voids. It is common in aerospace and vacuum-bag processes for both alloys.

Thermal role

Both conduct heat well; 3003 has the higher thermal conductivity, which is useful in heat-exchange and airflow straightener applications.

Cost

3003 is generally the lower-cost core at equal geometry. The premium for 5052 is usually small relative to the cost of skins, adhesive, coating and fabrication in a finished panel.

A sensible split on mixed projects is to use 5052 for exposed, wet or load-bearing zones and 3003 for protected interior panels. A typical ferry interior, for example, might use 5052 core for deck-adjacent bulkheads and 3003 core for cabin ceilings, keeping cost down without compromising the panels that need durability.

Ordering

How to specify and order honeycomb core

  1. Define serviceIndoor or outdoor, humidity and salt exposure, temperature range, fire requirement and expected loads.
  2. Choose alloy3003 for dry, moderate-load panels; 5052 for structural, humid or marine panels.
  3. Fix geometryCell size, foil gauge (or density), core height and the L-direction orientation on the drawing.
  4. State formUnexpanded blocks, expanded blocks, sliced sheets to thickness, perforated or non-perforated, and any surface treatment.
  5. Testing and documentsRequired test methods (ASTM C273, C365, C297), mill test certificate for the foil and export packing needs.

FAQ

3003 vs 5052 honeycomb core: frequently asked questions

Is 5052 honeycomb core stronger than 3003?

Yes in shear and fatigue, at the same cell size, foil gauge and density. In flatwise compression the gap is smaller, because thin cell walls fail by buckling, which depends mainly on wall thickness and cell size.

Which aluminum honeycomb core is best for marine use?

5052 (or 5056 for higher loads). It resists chloride pitting far better than 3003. Combine it with a pre-treated foil, a marine-grade adhesive and sealed edges.

What is the yield strength of 3003 compared with 5052?

Aluminum Association typical values are about 42 MPa for 3003-O and 90 MPa for 5052-O; in full-hard sheet, about 186 MPa for 3003-H18 and 255 MPa for 5052-H38. Honeycomb foil is supplied in hard tempers, and the core test data matter more than foil yield for design.

Can I replace 5052 core with 3003 to save cost?

Only for dry, indoor, lightly loaded panels, and only after checking shear strength at the chosen density. For floors, exterior panels or vibrating structures, keep 5052.

Why is my core density more important than the alloy?

Density reflects how much metal is in the cell walls. Crush and shear strength rise steeply with density, so a change of one density grade often moves strength more than switching alloy.

Can 3003 honeycomb be brazed?

Yes. 3003 brazes readily, which is why it is used for brazed panels and airflow parts. 5052 is difficult to braze because of its magnesium content and is normally bonded with adhesive.

Choosing between 3003 and 5052 core?

Tell us the panel use, loads, environment and size. We will suggest an alloy, cell size and density and quote core and skins with full documentation.
