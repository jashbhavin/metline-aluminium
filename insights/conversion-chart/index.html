Global Aluminium Alloy Conversion Chart

Most wrought aluminium alloys use the same four-digit number worldwide, so 6061 in the US is EN AW-6061 in Europe and A6061 in Japan. The confusion comes from older national systems (DIN Werkstoff numbers, Chinese LY/LF/LD codes, Indian IS numbers, Russian GOST names and British HE/NS codes) that are still printed on drawings. This chart maps the common grades across those systems and explains where an "equivalent" is only approximate.

Background

How aluminium alloy designations work

The International Alloy Designation System, administered by the Aluminum Association and published in its "Teal Sheets", is the closest thing aluminium has to a world language. National standards bodies registered their alloys into it over decades, so today most countries use the same four-digit numbers.

The first digit gives the main alloying element: 1xxx is unalloyed aluminium (99.00 % minimum), 2xxx copper, 3xxx manganese, 4xxx silicon, 5xxx magnesium, 6xxx magnesium plus silicon, 7xxx zinc and 8xxx other elements such as iron or lithium. The second digit records modifications of the original alloy, and the last two digits identify the specific alloy (or, in the 1xxx series, the minimum purity beyond 99 %, so 1050 is 99.50 % Al).

A letter suffix marks a national variation that is registered separately: 1050A, 2017A, 6005A and 6063A are closely related to, but not the same as, 1050, 2017, 6005 and 6063. Europe writes the same numbers with a prefix, EN AW-, under EN 573-1, and adds a chemical-symbol name such as EN AW-6061 [AlMg1SiCu]. The Unified Numbering System used in North America prefixes A9 to the wrought number, giving A96061.

Read this before using any conversion chart

  • Same number, same alloy: an AA, EN AW, JIS A or GB grade with the same four digits normally shares registered limits
  • Old national codes are often "nearest equivalents" with different chemistry windows
  • Equivalence of chemistry does not mean equivalence of temper, product standard or guaranteed properties
  • For critical parts, compare the composition limits and the mechanical requirements line by line

A common error, repeated on many charts, is listing British NS4 as 5052. NS4 corresponds to 5251, which has a lower magnesium content than 5052; the two are similar in character but not interchangeable on a certified order. Russian AMg grades are named by approximate magnesium content (AMg2, AMg3, AMg5, AMg6), so they map to families rather than to single Western alloys. AMg6, for instance, has more magnesium than any common EN alloy.

Temper codes

Practical advice

How to substitute grades safely

  1. Identify the original standardNote the full designation and the product standard it came from (for example GB/T 3880, JIS H4000, GOST 4784, IS 737). The product standard decides tolerances and properties, not just the alloy.
  2. Compare composition limitsPut both alloys side by side, element by element. If the windows overlap enough that one lot can meet both, dual certification is often possible.
  3. Compare mechanical requirementsCheck the guaranteed minimum tensile, yield and elongation for the temper and thickness. Equivalent alloys in different product standards can have different minimums.
  4. Check service-critical propertiesStress-corrosion, weldability, conductivity or anodising response may matter more than strength. A "near" equivalent can fail on these.
  5. Get approval and certifyFor regulated or safety-critical parts, get written approval from the designer, then order material with a mill certificate (EN 10204 3.1) stating the grade and standard that was agreed.

Common conversion mistakes

  • Treating 6061 and 6082 as identical: they are close substitutes, but 6082 has more silicon and manganese and slightly different strength
  • Assuming JIS or GB product standards guarantee the same minimum properties as ASTM B209 or EN 485-2
  • Copying a chart that pairs a pure grade with the wrong purity (1050 and 1060 are not the same)
  • Ignoring letter suffixes: 1050 vs 1050A, 2017 vs 2017A, 6005 vs 6005A

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) alloy equivalents: frequently asked questions

What is the European equivalent of 6061 aluminium?

EN AW-6061, also written EN AW-AlMg1SiCu, with the German Werkstoff number 3.3211. Many European projects use 6082 instead because it is more widely stocked there, but 6082 is a substitute, not an identical alloy.

Is 6063 the same as 6060?

No, although they are close. 6060 has slightly lower magnesium and silicon, extrudes faster and is a little weaker. Both were covered by the old German AlMgSi0.5 (3.3206) designation, which is why older charts link them.

What is the Chinese equivalent of 2024 aluminum?

2A12, formerly coded LY12, is the long-standing Chinese counterpart, and Russian D16 is equivalent in the same way. China also produces 2024 to its own registration under the international number.

What does UNS A97075 mean?

It is the Unified Numbering System code for wrought 7075. The letter A denotes aluminium, the 9 marks a wrought alloy and the last four digits repeat the Aluminum Association number.

Can I accept a JIS A5052 certificate for an ASTM 5052 order?

The alloy chemistry limits are aligned, but the product standards differ in tolerances and minimum properties. Accept it only if the certificate also shows compliance with ASTM B209 or your purchase order explicitly allows JIS H4000.

Why do conversion charts disagree with each other?

Many charts copy each other and list "nearest" national grades as if they were exact. Others confuse product standards with alloy standards. Always check composition limits from the source standard before substituting.
