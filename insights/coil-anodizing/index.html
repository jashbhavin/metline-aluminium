Coil Anodizing: How Continuous Anodising Works and When to Specify It

Coil anodizing passes an unwound aluminium coil continuously through cleaning, etching, anodising, colouring and sealing tanks, then rewinds it as a finished, pre-anodised coil. The result is a far more uniform colour and film thickness across very large surface areas than batch anodising of individual sheets, which is why architects, ceiling and lighting makers specify it. The trade-off is that the film must stay thin enough to survive later bending and forming.

The basics

What is coil anodizing?

Anodising is an electrochemical process, not a coating that is painted on. The aluminium is made the anode in an acid electrolyte, and current grows a layer of aluminium oxide out of the metal itself. Because the film is part of the substrate, it cannot peel the way paint can.

In conventional batch anodising, sheets, extrusions or parts are clamped onto jigs (racks), lowered into a series of tanks, held for a fixed time and lifted out. Each load sees slightly different current density, bath temperature and contact points, so colour and thickness vary from rack to rack and across a single sheet.

Coil anodizing turns the same chemistry into a continuous strip process. A coil, typically 0.3 – 2 mm thick, is unwound at the entry end, welded or stitched to the tail of the previous coil, and pulled through a line of tanks at a constant speed. Current is usually transferred to the strip without physical contact, through a liquid-contact (bipolar) cell, so there are no jig marks. At the exit the strip is dried, often interleaved or protected with film, and rewound. Every metre of strip has seen the same residence time and the same electrical conditions, which is the core advantage.

Why buyers specify pre-anodised coil

  • Colour and gloss stay consistent across thousands of square metres, important for façades and ceilings seen in one view
  • No rack or contact marks, and no edge "picture framing" common on batch-anodised sheet
  • Parts are cut, punched and formed from finished material, so there is no post-anodising step at the fabricator
  • Lower cost per square metre than batch anodising for large, flat or lightly formed parts
Line sequence

The coil anodizing process, step by step

Line layouts differ, but nearly every continuous anodising line follows the same sequence. Each stage is followed by one or more rinses so that one chemistry does not contaminate the next.

  1. Entry and joiningThe coil is uncoiled, levelled and joined to the previous coil so the line never has to stop. An accumulator stores strip so that joining does not interrupt the process tanks.
  2. Cleaning (degreasing)Alkaline or acid cleaners remove rolling oil, fines and loose oxide. Any residue left here shows later as streaks or patchy colour, so this stage sets the quality ceiling.
  3. Etching or brighteningA caustic etch dissolves a thin surface layer to give a uniform matt (satin) appearance and hide minor rolling lines. For specular reflector or "bright" finishes, chemical or electrochemical brightening is used instead.
  4. DesmuttingAn acid dip removes the dark smut of alloying elements and intermetallics left on the surface after etching.
  5. AnodisingThe strip passes through sulphuric acid electrolyte with controlled temperature, concentration and current density. Film thickness is set by current density multiplied by time in the cell, so line speed and current are linked by the control system.
  6. Colouring (optional)Organic dyes are absorbed into the open pores, or metal salts (commonly tin) are deposited at the pore base by AC electrolytic colouring to give champagne, bronze and black shades.
  7. SealingHot water, mid-temperature or cold (nickel fluoride type) sealing hydrates the oxide and closes the pores, locking in colour and giving corrosion and stain resistance.
  8. Drying, protection and rewindThe strip is dried, inspected online for thickness and colour, optionally laminated with protective film, and rewound or cut into sheets.
Film classes

Anodic film thickness: what coil can and cannot achieve

Film thickness is the most important line on an anodising specification because it governs durability. The Qualanod scheme and EN ISO 7599 use thickness classes expressed as the minimum average thickness in micrometres. Coil anodising covers the lower and middle classes well; the very thick films used for harsh exterior exposure are usually produced by batch anodising after fabrication.

Film classMinimum average thicknessTypical useSuitability for coil
Class 55 µmInterior decorative parts, trims, lighting componentsRoutine for coil
Class 1010 µmInterior ceilings and wall panels, appliance trimRoutine for coil
Class 1515 µmExterior use in rural and moderate urban atmospheresAchievable; check bend radius
Class 2020 µmExterior architectural work in urban or industrial airPossible on some lines; forming limited
Class 2525 µmAggressive marine or industrial exposureUsually batch-anodised after forming

Class definitions follow the Qualanod specifications and EN ISO 7599. Check the governing edition and the anodiser's approval scope for your project.

Why the limit? Anodic oxide is hard and brittle. When pre-anodised strip is bent, the outer surface stretches and the film crazes. A thin film shows fine, barely visible crazing; a thick film can crack visibly and lose its protective value at the bend. As a working rule, specify the thinnest film class that meets the exposure, use generous inside bend radii, and bend across the rolling direction where possible.

Two further points catch buyers out. First, film thickness is rarely identical on both faces: the reverse side of the strip often receives a thinner film unless a two-sided product is ordered. Second, strip edges tend to build a slightly thicker film than the centre. Both should be stated on the order if they matter for the application.

Substrate selection

Which aluminium alloys suit coil anodizing?

The anodised appearance depends heavily on the substrate. Alloying elements and intermetallic particles either dissolve, stay in the film or tint it, so alloy choice and metallurgical control at the rolling mill matter as much as line settings.

AlloyAnodised appearanceNotes
5005 (anodising quality)Clear, neutral silver; good colour match to 6063 extrusionsThe standard architectural choice. Order "anodising quality" (AQ) material to avoid streaks known as barcoding.
1050AVery clear, bright filmHigh purity gives clean colour; softer, so used for ceilings, signage and lighting.
5657 / high-purity Al-MgBrilliant, specular after brighteningUsed for reflectors and bright trim where gloss matters.
3003 / 3105Slightly grey or yellowish, less evenManganese particles cloud the film; acceptable for some interior and coloured uses.
5052 / 5754Good, slightly darker than 5005Chosen where higher strength is needed; colour match must be checked.
2xxx and high-Si alloysPoor, dark and unevenNot suitable for decorative coil anodising.

Temper matters too. Coil intended for forming after anodising is often supplied in a half-hard or quarter-hard temper (H14, H24, H22) so that it bends without excessive springback while still holding flat. Anodising does not change the temper of non-heat-treatable alloys, because the process runs well below annealing temperatures.

Colour options

Colouring options: dyed vs electrolytic colour

Clear (natural)

Unsealed or sealed natural film with no added colour. The most durable and the most consistent between batches, and the default for façades.

Electrolytic (two-step)

Metal salts are deposited at the base of the pores. Champagne, light to dark bronze and black are typical. Highly lightfast, so the normal choice for exterior architectural colours.

Organic dyes

Dyes fill the pores to give reds, blues, golds and many other shades. Very decorative, but many dyes fade under UV, so they suit interior work unless the dye is rated for exterior exposure.

Interference colours

Modified electrolytic processes that create blue, green or grey shades by light interference. Sensitive to process control and therefore produced by fewer lines.

Bright / specular

Brightening before anodising and a thin film produce mirror-like reflectors for lighting and solar uses. Reflectance depends on purity and film thickness.

Brushed or patterned

Mechanical brushing or embossing before anodising gives satin and textured looks that hide handling marks.

Whatever the colour, ask for approved colour limit samples (light and dark limits) before production, and keep all panels for one elevation from the same coil or production run. Even a continuous line drifts slightly over time, and a façade viewed from a distance shows small differences more than a sample board does.

Cut edges deserve attention. Pre-anodised sheet exposes bare aluminium wherever it is sheared, slit or punched. Indoors that rarely matters. On exterior cladding, design the panel so edges are folded back, hidden in the fixing system or sealed. For extrusions that must match coil-anodised panels, specify the same colour standard and request matched samples, since profiles are anodised in a different process. See our anodised extrusions page for that side of the project.

Applications

Where coil-anodised aluminium is used

  • Architecture: cladding cassettes, soffits, fascias, column covers and composite panel skins where a metallic, non-painted look is wanted
  • Interior ceilings and walls: linear and tile ceilings, lift interiors and wall linings that need consistent colour over large areas
  • Lighting: reflectors and louvres made from bright anodised high-purity strip
  • Appliances and electronics: trims, control panels and decorative front panels
  • Signage and nameplates: printed or engraved anodised sheet that resists wear and fading
  • Transport interiors: rail and bus wall panels and trim strips that must clean easily

In each case the buyer is purchasing a finish as much as a metal. A practical specification states the alloy and temper, thickness and width, film class, colour reference with limit samples, sealing method, single- or double-sided treatment, protective film type and the bend radius the coil must tolerate.

How coil-anodised material is tested

Common acceptance tests

Film thickness
Eddy-current measurement to EN ISO 2360, typically several readings across the width
Sealing quality
Dye spot test (ISO 2143), admittance (ISO 2931) or acid dissolution mass loss (ISO 3210)
Colour and gloss
Visual comparison with limit samples under standard light; instrumental colour measurement where agreed
Abrasion resistance
Where wear matters, abrasive wheel or jet tests per ISO 8251
Formability
Bend test at the agreed radius, checking for visible cracking or loss of adhesion
Documentation
Mill test certificate for the substrate (EN 10204 3.1) plus the anodiser's test report

FAQ

Coil anodizing and anodized aluminum coil: frequently asked questions

Can pre-anodised aluminium coil be bent?

Yes, within limits. Thin films (around 5 – 10 µm) tolerate moderate bends with only fine crazing. Thicker films crack more visibly, so use larger inside radii, bend across the rolling direction and confirm the radius with a bend test before production.

What film thickness should I specify for exterior cladding?

Most architectural specifications call for at least 15 µm in rural or moderate urban exposure and 20 µm or more in urban, industrial or coastal sites. If a heavily formed part needs 25 µm, batch anodising after fabrication is usually the safer route.

Which alloy gives the best anodised colour on coil?

5005 in anodising quality is the usual architectural choice and matches 6063 extrusions well. High-purity 1050A gives a very clear film, and specially processed Al-Mg alloys are used for bright reflectors.

Are the cut edges of anodised sheet protected?

No. Shearing, slitting and punching expose bare aluminium. Aluminium forms its own thin natural oxide, which is enough indoors, but exterior panels should be designed with folded, hidden or sealed edges.

Is coil-anodised aluminum cheaper than batch anodising?

For large, flat or lightly formed parts it is usually cheaper per square metre and far more uniform. For small runs, complex shapes or very thick films, batch anodising remains the practical choice.

Does anodising change the strength of the coil?

Not for the non-heat-treatable alloys normally used (1xxx, 3xxx, 5xxx), because process temperatures stay well below annealing temperatures. The film adds surface hardness but contributes little to the strength of the sheet.
