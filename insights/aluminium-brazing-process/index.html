The Aluminium Brazing Process Explained

Aluminium brazing joins parts with an aluminium-silicon filler that melts at about 577 – 610 °C, only a few tens of degrees below the base metal, so the parts never melt. Capillary action draws the molten filler into close-fitting joints, and a whole heat exchanger with thousands of joints can be brazed in one furnace cycle. This guide explains the metallurgy, compares the main brazing methods, and covers fillers, brazing sheet, flux, base-metal selection and joint design.

Principle

How aluminium brazing works

Brazing is defined by temperature: the filler melts above 450 °C but below the solidus of the parts being joined. For aluminium that window is narrow, because the Al-Si eutectic filler melts at 577 °C and common base alloys begin to melt between about 580 °C and 645 °C.

Aluminium is always covered by a tough oxide film that molten filler cannot wet. Every brazing method therefore needs a way to deal with it: a flux that dissolves or disrupts the oxide, or a vacuum process in which magnesium evaporates from the filler and breaks the film up. Once the oxide is breached, the molten Al-Si alloy wets the surface, flows along the joint by capillary action and forms a fillet. On cooling it solidifies into a continuous metallurgical bond.

  1. Clean and assembleParts are degreased and fitted with a controlled gap, with filler pre-placed as cladding, wire, rings, foil or paste.
  2. Remove the oxideFlux melts and attacks the oxide film (fluxed methods), or vacuum and magnesium do the job (vacuum brazing).
  3. Melt and flowThe filler melts, wets both surfaces and is drawn through the joint, displacing the molten flux.
  4. Diffuse and solidifySilicon diffuses into the base metal and a little base metal dissolves into the filler. The assembly is held briefly at temperature, then cooled.

CAB has become the dominant process for automotive and HVAC heat exchangers because it runs continuously and the residue of non-corrosive flux does not need washing off. Vacuum brazing remains the choice where flux residues are unacceptable, for example in aircraft and liquid-cooled electronics, or where internal passages could trap flux.

Materials

Aluminium-silicon filler alloys

Almost all aluminium brazing uses 4xxx alloys based on the Al-Si system. Silicon lowers the melting point towards the 577 °C eutectic; more silicon means lower and narrower melting, better fluidity and larger fillets. Fillers for vacuum brazing add magnesium, and some include bismuth to improve wetting.

Fillers are supplied as clad layers on sheet and strip, as wire, rod and flux-cored rod, as rings and preforms that sit on tube joints, as thin foil for laminated joints, and as powder or paste mixed with flux.

Materials

Clad brazing sheet: filler built into the part

Brazing sheet is the material that makes mass-produced heat exchangers possible. A core alloy, usually from the 3xxx family, is roll-bonded on one or both sides with a thin layer of Al-Si filler, typically 5 – 15 % of the total thickness on each clad side. Fins, tubes and header plates are formed from it and assembled; in the furnace the cladding melts and flows to every contact point, so no separate filler is needed.

Fin stock

Very thin clad or bare strip, often with zinc additions so the fins corrode sacrificially and protect the tubes.

Tube stock

Clad strip for folded or welded tubes; may carry a 7072 or zinc-bearing water-side liner for corrosion protection.

Header and side plates

Thicker clad sheet that holds the tube ends and gives the core its structure.

Long-life cores

Modern core alloys with controlled copper, manganese and titanium resist corrosion and sagging at brazing temperature.

Flux

The role of flux

For CAB, the industry standard is a non-corrosive potassium fluoroaluminate flux that melts at roughly 565 – 572 °C, just before the filler. It dissolves the oxide, protects the surface from re-oxidation and leaves a thin, inert residue. Its weakness is magnesium: above a few tenths of a per cent in the base metal, magnesium reacts with the flux and forms compounds that stop wetting. That is why CAB parts use low-magnesium alloys, and why caesium-modified fluxes exist for slightly higher magnesium contents.

Chloride-based fluxes are more aggressive and work at lower temperatures, which suits flame brazing, but their residues are hygroscopic and corrosive and must be completely washed off. Select flux by process, temperature, base alloy and whether residues can remain, and confirm it with a trial on representative parts.

Design

Joint design and common defects

  • Clearance: for fluxed brazing, gaps of about 0.05 – 0.20 mm are usual; vacuum brazing prefers tighter, near-zero clearances.
  • Lap joints over butt joints: lap length of about three times the thinner section gives a joint stronger than the annealed base metal.
  • Fixturing: parts must stay in contact as they expand; stainless or ceramic fixtures and clips are common.
  • Erosion: too much filler, too high a temperature or too long a hold lets silicon dissolve thin fins or tube walls.
  • Voids and poor fillets: usually caused by contamination, too little flux, incorrect gap or magnesium in the alloy.
  • Annealing: the brazing cycle anneals work-hardened alloys; design to O-temper strength, or select post-braze heat-treatable alloys.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) brazing: frequently asked questions

What temperature is aluminium brazed at?

Typically about 577 – 615 °C, depending on the filler. Furnace CAB cycles commonly peak around 600 °C, only slightly below the solidus of the base alloy, so temperature control is critical.

What is the difference between 4047 and 4343 filler?

4047 has about 12 % silicon and melts lowest, giving very fluid filler suited to wire, rings and torch or induction brazing. 4343 has about 7.5 % silicon and is mostly used as cladding on brazing sheet for furnace brazing.

Why can't 6061 be brazed easily?

Its solidus is around 580 °C, very close to the melting range of Al-Si fillers, and its magnesium content interferes with fluoride fluxes. It can be brazed with careful control or vacuum methods, but 3xxx and 6063 are far easier.

Does brazing weaken aluminium?

The brazing cycle anneals work-hardened alloys, so strength drops to roughly O-temper levels. Heat-treatable alloys can regain strength by post-braze ageing; designs normally use annealed properties.

Is flux needed for aluminium brazing?

For CAB, torch, induction and dip brazing, yes. Vacuum brazing uses magnesium-bearing filler and high vacuum instead, so no flux is required.

Can aluminium be brazed to copper?

Yes, with suitable fillers and fluxes and careful heat control, because brittle Al-Cu intermetallics form at the interface. It is common in HVAC and refrigeration tube joints.
