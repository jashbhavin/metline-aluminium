Gold vs Silver vs Copper vs Aluminium Bonding Wires

No bonding wire metal wins on every count. Gold is the stable, forgiving baseline for fine-wire ball bonding; copper and palladium-coated copper (PCC) cut material cost but need a tighter process; silver alloys sit between them; and aluminium owns wedge bonding and heavy-wire power interconnects. The right choice depends on bond method, pad metallisation, package environment and how much requalification you can afford.

The short answer

Can the four wire metals be compared directly?

Only partly. Gold, silver and copper wires compete for the same job: fine-wire ball bonding of integrated circuits and LEDs, usually at diameters of roughly 15 – 50 µm. Aluminium mostly does a different job: wedge bonding at room temperature with ultrasonic energy, either as fine wire or as heavy wire and ribbon carrying tens of amperes in power modules.

That distinction matters more than any property table. Swapping gold for copper is a change of material inside the same bonding architecture. Swapping gold ball bonds for aluminium wedge bonds is a change of architecture: different machines, tools, loop shapes and qualification plans. So the useful question is not "which metal is best" but "which wire route fits my bond method, pad and reliability target at the lowest total cost".

Difference 1

Electrical and thermal conductivity: why pure-metal data is not enough

Pure metal, room temperatureSilverCopperGoldAluminium
Electrical resistivity (µΩ·cm, lower is better)1.591.682.442.65
Thermal conductivity (W/m·K, higher is better)429401318237
Density (g/cm³)10.58.9619.32.70

On paper silver and copper are clearly ahead, and aluminium trails gold slightly. In a package the picture shifts. Bonding wires are not pure metals: gold wire carries trace dopants, silver wire is often alloyed with gold or palladium (which raises resistivity noticeably), PCC has a palladium skin, and fine aluminium wire usually contains 1 % silicon or about 1 % magnesium to make it drawable and strong enough to loop.

Geometry then dominates. A 25 µm wire a few millimetres long contributes very little resistance to most logic devices, so conductivity rarely decides fine-wire selection. In power modules the opposite is true, which is why designers use 300 – 500 µm aluminium wire, several wires in parallel, or flat ribbon. Because aluminium is about a third as dense as copper, an aluminium conductor of equal resistance weighs roughly half as much, and heavy aluminium wire is easy to wedge bond onto aluminium chip metallisation.

Selection point: use pure-metal data for screening only. Final decisions should rest on the datasheet of the actual wire and on measured interconnect resistance in your package.

Difference 2

Hardness, strength and the risk to the bond pad

Hardness controls how much force and ultrasonic energy are needed to deform the ball or wedge, and therefore how much stress reaches the pad and the fragile dielectric layers beneath it. Annealed copper is roughly twice as hard as annealed gold (about 50 HV against about 25 HV), and a copper free-air ball also work-hardens during bonding. That is the root cause of the best-known copper problems: aluminium splash around the ball, pad cratering and cracks in low-k dielectrics.

Palladium coating does not soften the ball; it mainly protects the surface from oxidation and improves the stitch bond. Silver alloys sit between gold and copper in hardness. Aluminium wire is soft in the annealed state, but fine Al-Si wire is supplied with a controlled breaking load and elongation so that it loops consistently and shears cleanly at the wedge.

Property to compareWhy it matters
Breaking load (cN or gf)Sets loop strength and resistance to wire sweep during moulding
Elongation (%)Controls loop shape, tail consistency and heel flexibility

Pad stack and thicknessThicker or harder pad metallisation tolerates harder wires

Selection point: evaluate hardness, breaking load, elongation and the pad structure together. A wire that loops beautifully can still crack the die under it.

Difference 3

Oxidation, corrosion, sulphidation and ion migration

Gold

Does not oxidise, which is why it tolerates storage, open spools and air-fired free-air balls. Its weaknesses sit at the interface with aluminium pads rather than on the wire surface.

Silver

Tarnishes in sulphur-bearing air and is attacked by chlorides from mould compounds and flux residues. Under bias and humidity, silver ions can migrate and form dendrites. Alloying with gold or palladium, or coating the wire, reduces these effects.

Copper and PCC

Bare copper oxidises quickly, so balls are formed under a nitrogen-hydrogen forming gas and spools have a short floor life once opened. In humid, biased tests copper ball bonds corrode by different mechanisms from gold; palladium coating and modern green mould compounds have made PCC far more robust.

Aluminium

Forms a thin, self-healing oxide film. The film protects the wire but has to be broken by ultrasonic scrubbing at every bond, so surface condition and storage affect bondability. Al wire on an Al pad avoids a dissimilar-metal interface entirely.

Selection point: confirm storage conditions, mould compound chemistry and any exposure to humid, sulphur-bearing or chloride-bearing environments before choosing silver or copper.

Difference 4

Bondability and process-window sensitivity

Gold has the widest and best-documented ball-bonding window. Copper and PCC need dedicated settings for electronic flame-off (EFO) current and time, shielding gas flow, capillary design, bond force and ultrasonic profile; gold parameters cannot simply be copied across. Silver wire behaviour depends heavily on the specific alloy and any coating, so two silver wires from different makers can need different recipes.

Aluminium wire runs on wedge bonders with a wedge tool and a wire-feed angle suited to the wire diameter. Because it bonds at room temperature with ultrasonic energy alone, it suits heat-sensitive substrates and large power modules that cannot be heated, but it is directional: the second bond must lie in line with the first, which slows fine-wire throughput compared with ball bonding.

Common mistakes when changing wire

  • Assuming the same diameter means a drop-in replacement
  • Copying gold EFO and bonding parameters to copper or silver wire
  • Ignoring spool floor life and storage for copper and silver
  • Qualifying one lot only, instead of several wire lots and wafer lots
Difference 5

Bond interfaces and long-term reliability

Most bonding wires land on an aluminium pad, so the interface chemistry is decisive. Gold and aluminium form several intermetallic phases; at high temperature they grow quickly, and unequal diffusion leaves Kirkendall voids that weaken the bond. The historical names "purple plague" (AuAl2) and "white plague" (Au5Al2) come from this system. Copper-aluminium intermetallics grow more slowly, which is one reason PCC can match or beat gold in high-temperature storage, while it is more sensitive to moisture-driven corrosion of those phases.

Aluminium wire on an aluminium pad forms no intermetallic at all. Its reliability limit in power electronics is mechanical: repeated power cycling makes the wire and chip expand at different rates (aluminium about 23 ppm/K, silicon about 3 ppm/K), which eventually cracks the wire heel or lifts the bond from the chip. That fatigue mechanism is why designers of high-temperature SiC modules evaluate copper wire, copper ribbon or aluminium-clad copper.

Wire on aluminium padMain interface or failure mechanismTypical tests
GoldAu-Al intermetallic growth and Kirkendall voiding at high temperatureHigh-temperature storage, ball shear, wire pull

Selection point: never rank reliability by base metal alone. Validate the complete wire, pad, mould compound and package system.

Difference 6

Wire price versus total conversion cost

Gold is by far the most expensive wire per metre, which is why copper and silver conversions began. But the price per spool is only one line in the business case. A conversion can require new capillaries or wedges, a forming-gas kit, process development time, pilot-run losses, a full reliability qualification on several lots, a product change notification (PCN) and approval from every customer who buys the device.

For high-volume devices those costs are recovered quickly. For low-volume, high-reliability or automotive parts with long qualification cycles, staying on gold or on an existing aluminium wedge process is often the cheaper answer.

Before you switch

What to re-evaluate when changing bonding wire material

  1. WireCurrent and candidate material, alloy or coating, diameter, breaking load, elongation, hardness and spool format.
  2. BondingBall or wedge process, tools and parameters; for ball bonding, FAB formation, EFO settings and shielding atmosphere.
  3. Pad and packagePad metallisation and thickness, underlying structure, mould compound, loop height and clearance.
  4. PerformanceYield, resistance, visual criteria, wire pull and ball or wedge shear baselines, environmental and cycling tests.
  5. Change controlPilot runs, multi-lot confirmation, customer approval, PCN and second-source introduction.

FAQ

Bonding wire materials: frequently asked questions

Which bonding wire material is best for semiconductor packaging?

There is no single best metal. Gold remains the most forgiving choice for fine-wire ball bonding, PCC copper dominates high-volume cost-sensitive IC packaging, silver alloys are common in LEDs, and aluminium is the standard for wedge bonding and heavy-wire power interconnects.

Can copper bonding wire directly replace gold?

Not directly. Copper is harder and oxidises, so it needs forming gas for ball formation, a new capillary and bond recipe, checks for pad cratering and a full reliability qualification. Many products have converted successfully, but always as a managed change.

Can silver alloy bonding wire replace gold?

Often, especially in LED and consumer packages. It runs in the same ball-bonding architecture, but ball formation depends on the alloy and coating, and the package must be protected from sulphur, chlorides and humidity-driven ion migration.

Why is aluminium bonding wire not compared one-to-one with gold, silver and copper?

Because it is usually used in a different process. Aluminium is wedge bonded at room temperature, as fine Al-1 % Si wire or as heavy wire and ribbon in power modules, whereas gold, silver and copper are mostly ball bonded at fine diameters.

What is the difference between bare copper and PCC wire?

PCC (palladium-coated copper) has a thin palladium layer on the copper core. The coating slows oxidation, extends spool floor life, improves stitch bonds and helps reliability in humid tests, at a modest extra cost over bare copper.

Why is heavy aluminum wire used in power modules?

Heavy aluminium wire of a few hundred microns carries high current, bonds directly to aluminium chip metallisation without a dissimilar interface, is light and costs little per ampere. Its limit is fatigue under power cycling, which is why some high-temperature designs move to copper or ribbon.
