Buyer’s guide

Everything About Marine Grade Aluminium, With a Purchase Guide

“Marine grade aluminium” is not a single alloy. It is a group of 5xxx aluminium-magnesium alloys, chiefly 5083, 5086, 5383, 5059 and 5456 in H116 or H321 temper, that resist seawater and keep most of their strength after welding, supported by 6xxx extrusions such as 6061 and 6082 for frames and fittings. This guide explains which alloy goes where, what the tempers and tests mean, and what to put on a purchase order so the material arrives fit for a classed vessel.

The case for aluminium

Why boatbuilders choose aluminium

An aluminium hull structure typically weighs much less than an equivalent steel one, which means more payload, higher speed or lower fuel burn for the same engine. Unlike steel, the right aluminium alloys need no paint below the waterline to survive in seawater, and unlike fibreglass they can be welded, repaired and recycled at end of life.

  • Weight: density of about 2.66 g/cm³ for 5083, roughly a third of steel
  • Corrosion resistance: 5xxx alloys form a stable oxide film and resist pitting, exfoliation and intergranular attack in seawater when correctly tempered
  • Weldability: 5xxx plate is MIG and TIG welded with 5183 or 5356 filler and keeps its annealed strength in the weld zone
  • Formability: plate rolls and bends to hull curves; 6xxx alloys extrude into large, thin-walled stiffeners and deck sections
  • Low-temperature toughness: aluminium does not become brittle in the cold, so 5083 is also used for LNG tanks

As a simple rule: 5083 is the default for hull plate, 5086 and 5052 cover lighter and smaller craft, 5383 and 5059 are chosen when welded strength must be pushed further, and 6xxx extrusions carry the framing. Copper-bearing 2xxx and zinc-bearing 7xxx alloys are generally avoided in seawater because of their poor corrosion resistance.

Tempers

H116 and H321: what the marine tempers mean

Aluminium-magnesium alloys with more than about 3 % magnesium can become sensitised: if held at moderately elevated temperature, a magnesium-rich β phase (Al3Mg2) forms along grain boundaries and makes the metal prone to intergranular corrosion, exfoliation and stress-corrosion cracking. The H116 and H321 tempers are produced with controlled strain hardening and thermal stabilisation so that the grain structure resists this attack.

H116

Strain-hardened with controlled processing for marine service. Must pass exfoliation and intergranular corrosion tests. Commonly preferred for hull plate in North America.

H321

Strain-hardened and thermally stabilised. Same strength minimums as H116 for 5083 and widely specified in Europe and Asia. Also corrosion-tested under ASTM B928.

H111 / O

Annealed or lightly worked. Used where forming is severe or where welded properties govern design anyway.

H32 / H34

Strain-hardened and stabilised tempers for 5052 and 5754 sheet; lower magnesium alloys are much less susceptible to sensitisation.

Sensitisation in practice

  • Avoid high-magnesium 5xxx alloys in service hotter than about 65 °C, such as near engines, exhausts or hot cargo
  • Choose 5454 or lower-magnesium alloys for warm tanks and exhaust-side structures
  • Do not use H32 or H34 tempers of 5083 or 5456 for hull plate; specify H116 or H321
  • Ask for ASTM G66 (exfoliation) and G67 (mass loss) results on the certificate

Classification rulesDNV, ABS, Lloyd’s Register, Bureau Veritas, ClassNK, KR and others set material approval, testing and certification for classed vessels

For a vessel built to class, material must come from a mill approved by the classification society for that alloy and product, with testing witnessed or certified to the society’s rules. The certificate then carries the society’s stamp or reference. If your project is classed, state the society and the rule edition on the enquiry so the material is ordered and certified correctly from the start; uncertified plate cannot be upgraded afterwards without full retesting.

Working with it

Welding, finishing and corrosion protection

Welding

MIG for most structure, TIG for thin and visible work, and friction stir welding for prefabricated deck panels. Use 5183 filler for 5083 and 5383, 5356 or 5183 for 5086 and 5052. Keep joints clean and dry to avoid porosity.

Galvanic isolation

Isolate stainless steel fasteners and fittings with sleeves and washers, keep copper and bronze away from bare aluminium, and never use copper-based antifouling paint on an aluminium hull.

Cathodic protection

Fit zinc or aluminium-alloy sacrificial anodes to protect underwater areas, and check shore-power wiring to prevent stray-current corrosion.

Coating

Bare 5xxx works above and below the waterline, but most yachts are painted. Abrade, clean and prime with an etch or epoxy primer, because paint does not adhere to the natural oxide film.

Anodising

Common on 6xxx extrusions, rails and fittings for appearance and wear; less used on hull plate.

Design details

Avoid crevices that trap seawater, drain all pockets, and keep aluminium out of contact with wet timber, concrete and treated wood.

Purchase guide

How to buy marine grade aluminium: step by step

  1. Define the partHull plate, deck, superstructure, frame or fitting; above or below the waterline; maximum service temperature.
  2. Choose alloy and temperFor example 5083-H116 hull plate, 5052-H32 sheet, 6082-T6 profiles. Avoid non-marine tempers of high-Mg alloys.
  3. State the standardASTM B928, EN 13195, EN 485 or EN 755, plus the classification society and rule edition if the vessel is classed.
  4. Give sizes and tolerancesThickness, width, length or profile drawing; plates as large as practical reduce welding.
  5. Specify tests and documentsEN 10204 3.1 or 3.2 certificate, G66 / G67 results for H116 / H321, class certificate, ultrasonic testing if required.
  6. Plan packing and deliveryInterleaved, film-protected plate in seaworthy cases; EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP terms to your yard.

Common purchasing mistakes

  • Buying “5083” without a temper and receiving H32 or H34 sheet
  • Ordering uncertified plate for a classed vessel
  • Using 6061 for permanently immersed structure without protection
  • Ignoring storage: wet, stacked plate develops water stain before it is ever welded

FAQ

Marine grade aluminium (aluminum): frequently asked questions

What is the best marine grade aluminum?

5083 in H116 or H321 temper is the standard choice for hull plate, balancing seawater resistance, weldability and strength. 5383 and 5059 offer more welded strength for performance craft, and 5086 or 5052 suit lighter boats.

Is 6061 a marine grade aluminium?

6061 is widely used on boats for extrusions, fittings and frames, but it is less corrosion-resistant in seawater than 5xxx alloys and loses much of its strength when welded. It is best used above the waterline or protected by anodising or paint.

What is the difference between 5083-H116 and 5083-H321?

Both have the same minimum strength and must pass ASTM B928 exfoliation and intergranular corrosion tests. H116 is achieved by controlled strain hardening, H321 by strain hardening plus thermal stabilisation; both are accepted for hull plate.

Can marine aluminium be left unpainted?

Yes. 5xxx hulls are often left bare above and below the waterline, especially on workboats. They still need sacrificial anodes, galvanic isolation from other metals and copper-free antifouling if painted.

Why should high-magnesium alloys avoid hot areas?

Above about 65 °C, alloys such as 5083 and 5456 can become sensitised, forming grain-boundary phases that cause intergranular and stress-corrosion cracking. Use 5454 or lower-magnesium alloys for warm areas.

What certificate do I need for a classed vessel?

Material from a mill approved by the classification society, with a certificate issued or endorsed under the society’s rules, normally to EN 10204 3.2 or the society’s own format. Specify this when ordering.
