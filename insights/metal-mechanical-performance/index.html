43 Mechanical Property Terms for Metals, with Aluminium Examples

Every mill test certificate and material datasheet is written in the language of mechanical testing: proof stress, tensile strength, elongation, hardness, creep and fracture toughness. This glossary explains 43 of those terms, groups them by test, and notes how each applies to aluminium alloys, which behave differently from steel in several important ways.

Context

Reading a certificate correctly

A certificate that says "Rm 310, Rp0.2 276, A 12" is reporting three of the terms below. Misreading them, for example treating a typical value as a guaranteed minimum or comparing elongations measured on different gauge lengths, is one of the commonest causes of rejected material.

Aluminium alloys differ from carbon steel in four ways that affect how these terms are used. They have no sharp yield point, so yield is always reported as 0.2 % proof stress. They have no true fatigue endurance limit, so fatigue strength is quoted at a stated number of cycles. Their face-centred cubic structure means they do not suffer a ductile-to-brittle transition at low temperature, so Charpy impact tests are rarely specified. And their elastic modulus is about one third that of steel, so stiffness rather than strength often governs design.

Group 1

Stress, strain and elastic behaviour (terms 1 – 9)

#TermMeaningAluminium note
1Elastic limitThe highest stress a material can carry and still return fully to its original shape when unloaded.Hard to measure precisely; proof stress is used instead.
2Elastic modulus (Young's modulus, E)The ratio of stress to strain in the linear elastic region; a measure of stiffness.About 69 – 72 GPa for wrought alloys (6061 ≈ 68.9 GPa, 7075 ≈ 71.7 GPa), barely affected by temper.
3Poisson's ratioThe ratio of lateral contraction to axial extension under uniaxial elastic load (taken as positive).About 0.33 for aluminium alloys.
4StrainThe relative change in dimension or shape of a body caused by load.Dimensionless, often given in % or microstrain.
5Engineering strainChange in length divided by the original length.Used for elongation values on certificates.
6Linear strainThe strain component along one specified direction.Measured with extensometers or strain gauges.
7True strainThe natural logarithm of instantaneous length over original length, valid up to necking.Used in forming simulation of sheet and extrusions.
8StressForce, or a component of force, acting on a plane divided by the area of that plane.Reported in MPa (N/mm²) or ksi.
9Engineering stressApplied force divided by the original cross-sectional area.All standard tensile values are engineering stresses.
Group 2

Tensile and compressive test terms (terms 13 – 25)

Tensile testing of aluminium follows ASTM B557 (which references ASTM E8/E8M) or ISO 6892-1. These are the values that appear on every EN 10204 3.1 certificate.

#TermMeaningAluminium note
13Elongation (A)Permanent increase in gauge length after fracture, as a percentage of the original gauge length.Always check the gauge length: A50mm (ASTM, EN thin products) and A (5.65√S0) give different numbers for the same material.
15Plastic extensionNon-proportional extension of the gauge length under load, as a percentage.The basis for defining the 0.2 % proof stress.
16Reduction of area (Z)Maximum reduction in cross-section at fracture, as a percentage of the original area.Reported mainly for bar and forgings; a good indicator of ductility in short-transverse tests.
17Tensile strength (Rm, UTS)Maximum force sustained during the test divided by the original area.6061-T6 typical 310 MPa; 7075-T6 typical 572 MPa; 1050-O around 75 MPa.
18Compressive strengthMaximum compressive stress a material sustains; for ductile metals, the stress at a defined deformation.For ductile aluminium, compressive yield is the more useful value.
19Shear strengthMaximum shear stress before failure, in direct shear or torsion.Roughly 55 – 65 % of tensile strength; key for rivets and bolted joints.
20Yield strengthStress at which plastic deformation occurs without an increase in force, for materials with a yield phenomenon.Aluminium shows no yield point, so Rp0.2 (0.2 % proof stress) is reported.
21Upper yield strength (ReH)Stress at the first peak before force drops at yielding.Applies to mild steel, not to aluminium.
22Lower yield strength (ReL)Lowest stress during yielding, ignoring initial transient effects.Not applicable to aluminium.
23Compressive stressCompressive force divided by the original cross-sectional area.Relevant to column and bearing design.
24Compressive yield strengthCompressive stress at a defined plastic strain (normally 0.2 %).Close to tensile proof stress for most tempers; lower in some stretched products along the stretch axis.
25Compressive elastic modulusStress-to-strain ratio in compression in the linear region.Slightly higher than the tensile modulus for aluminium.

Typical values describe what material usually achieves; standards such as ASTM B209, B221 and EN 485-2 give the specified minimums you should design to.

Group 3

Creep, stress-rupture and relaxation (terms 10 – 12, 14, 26 – 31)

Creep matters for aluminium at much lower temperatures than for steel. Above about 100 – 150 °C, most heat-treatable alloys begin to over-age and creep, which is why 2618 and 2219 are chosen for hot-running parts such as pistons and engine components.

#TermMeaningAluminium note
10Creep curveA plot of strain against time at constant load and temperature.Shows primary, secondary (steady) and tertiary creep.
11Creep-rupture timeTime to fracture under a defined stress at a defined temperature.Tested to ASTM E139 / ISO 204.
12Creep strengthStress that produces a defined creep strain in a defined time at a given temperature.Used for bolted joints and pressure parts above 100 °C.
14Creep-rupture elongationPermanent extension after creep rupture, as a percentage of the original gauge length.Indicates ductility at temperature.
26Stress-rupture (long-term) strengthThe highest stress a specimen can carry for a set time at a set temperature without fracturing.Not the same as fatigue endurance limit, which aluminium does not have.
27Stress-rupture elongationElongation after a stress-rupture test, measured at room temperature.
28Stress-rupture reduction of areaReduction of area after a stress-rupture test.
29Stress-rupture notch sensitivityRatio of rupture stress (or time) for notched and smooth specimens.A value below 1 warns that notches shorten life at temperature.
30Relaxation stressThe drop in stress in a specimen held at constant strain over time.Important for clamped joints and springs; tested to ASTM E328.
31Relaxation rateRate of stress loss per unit time at a given moment.Highest at the start of the test.

Fatigue: the term people look for

  • Aluminium has no endurance limit: given enough cycles, fatigue cracks can form at any alternating stress
  • Datasheets therefore quote fatigue strength at a stated life, commonly 5 × 108 cycles (6061-T6 about 96 MPa)
  • Surface finish, notches, welds and corrosion pits reduce fatigue life much more than alloy choice
Group 4

Hardness terms (terms 32 – 38)

#TermMeaningAluminium note
32Brinell hardness (HBW)Resistance to indentation by a hard-metal ball under a set force; the mean impression diameter gives the number.The usual hardness for aluminium plate, bar and forgings (ASTM E10, ISO 6506-1); 6061-T6 about 95 HB.
33Indentation hardnessThe general term for hardness measured by the size or depth of a permanent indentation.Covers Brinell, Vickers, Rockwell and Knoop.
34Vickers hardness (HV)Indentation hardness using a square-based diamond pyramid.Used for thin sheet, foil, coatings and weld traverses (ISO 6507, ASTM E92/E384).
35Rockwell hardness (HR)Hardness from the depth of indentation under a major load after a minor preload.Scales B, E, F and H suit aluminium; HRB is common for harder tempers.
36Shore hardnessRebound or durometer-type hardness; the Shore scleroscope uses a falling hammer.Rarely used for aluminium; durometer Shore scales are for polymers.
37Knoop hardness (HK)Micro-indentation hardness using an elongated rhombic diamond pyramid.Used on thin layers such as hard anodic coatings.
38Leeb hardness (HL)Portable rebound hardness: ratio of rebound to impact velocity of an impact body.Useful for large forgings and installed parts, with conversion caution.

For quick temper checks on sheet, extrusions and tube, the aluminium industry also uses the Webster hardness gauge (ASTM B647) and Barcol impressor (ASTM B648). These are sorting tools, not substitutes for a tensile test when a property is disputed. Hardness conversions between scales for aluminium are approximate; ASTM E140 gives the accepted tables.

Group 5

Impact and fracture terms (terms 39 – 43)

#TermMeaningAluminium note
39Impact absorbed energy (KV, KU)Energy absorbed in breaking a notched bar in a pendulum test, in joules, for V- or U-notch specimens.Charpy testing (ISO 148-1, ASTM E23) is seldom required for aluminium because there is no transition temperature.
40Brittle (crystalline) fracture percentageShare of the fracture surface with a flat, crystalline appearance.Relevant to steels; aluminium fractures are normally ductile.
41Ductile fracture percentageShare of the fracture surface that is fibrous and shows plastic tearing.
42Shear fracture percentageShare of the fracture surface that failed by shear (slant) fracture.Used with lateral expansion to judge toughness.
43Fracture toughness (KIC)Resistance to growth of an existing crack under quasi-static loading.Specified for aerospace plate and forgings in 2xxx and 7xxx alloys; tested to ASTM E399 and B645, and quoted by orientation (L-T, T-L, S-L).

For aerospace grades, fracture toughness and fatigue crack growth often limit design more than tensile strength. That is why alloys such as 7050 and 7475 were developed: similar strength to 7075, with higher toughness from tighter impurity limits and different tempers.

Practical advice

Five checks when comparing mechanical properties

  1. Typical or minimum?Design to specified minimums from the product standard; typical values are for comparison only.
  2. Same direction?Plate and forgings are weaker in the short-transverse direction. Compare like-for-like orientations.
  3. Same gauge length?Elongation on 50 mm and on 5.65√S0 differ. Check which the standard uses.
  4. Same thickness band?Minimum properties fall with thickness in heat-treatable alloys, so a value for 10 mm plate does not apply to 100 mm.
  5. Right temperature?Room-temperature values do not apply above about 100 °C for long exposures; use elevated-temperature data.

FAQ

Mechanical property terms: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between yield strength and tensile strength?

Yield (proof) strength is the stress at which permanent deformation begins, defined for aluminium as 0.2 % plastic strain. Tensile strength is the maximum stress reached before fracture. Structures are normally designed against yield, with tensile strength as a check.

Why does aluminium use 0.2 % proof stress instead of yield point?

Aluminium alloys yield gradually with no sudden drop in force, so there is no distinct yield point. The 0.2 % offset method gives a repeatable value that standards and certificates can specify.

Does aluminium have a fatigue endurance limit?

No. Unlike many steels, aluminium alloys can fail in fatigue at low stresses if the number of cycles is high enough, so fatigue strength is quoted at a specified life such as 5 × 10⁸ cycles.

Which hardness test is used for aluminium?

Brinell (HBW) is standard for plate, bar and forgings; Vickers for thin sheet, coatings and welds; Rockwell B, E or F for production checks; and Webster or Barcol gauges for quick temper sorting of sheet and extrusions.

Why is elongation on my certificate different from the datasheet?

Most often the gauge length differs (50 mm versus 5.65√S₀), or the datasheet shows a typical value while the certificate reports a test on your lot against a specified minimum.

Is aluminium brittle at low temperatures?

No. Aluminium keeps or improves its strength and ductility as temperature falls, which is why 5083 and 6061 are used in cryogenic tanks and LNG equipment.
