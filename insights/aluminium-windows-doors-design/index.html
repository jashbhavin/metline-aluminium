Designing Aluminium Windows and Doors: What the Profiles Must Deliver

A good aluminium window or door is a balance of appearance, structural stiffness, thermal performance, weather-tightness and buildability, and almost every one of those is decided by the extruded profile. This article is written for fabricators, system designers and project buyers. It explains how aluminium windows and doors are designed, which performance classes and tests matter, and what to specify when ordering profiles, thermal-break sections and finishes.

Context

Who designs what: system, fabricator and material supplier

A finished aluminium window is usually the work of three parties. A system company designs the profile cross-sections, gaskets, hardware interfaces and test evidence; a fabricator cuts, machines, assembles and glazes the units for a project; and a material supplier provides the extruded profiles, thermal-break sections, sheet and finishes to the system drawings.

Metline works at the material end of that chain. We supply 6063 and 6060 window and door profiles, thermally broken profiles, aluminium sheet for panels and flashings, and finishing to customer and system specifications. We do not fabricate or install windows or doors. The design guidance below explains what the finished product must achieve, because that is what determines how profiles should be specified.

Getting the profile specification right early avoids most downstream problems: frames that deflect too much under wind, condensation on cold internal faces, finishes that fail on the coast, or corner joints that open after a few seasons.

Appearance

Appearance design: proportion, sightlines, colour and shape

Architects judge windows first by their sightlines, the visible width of frame and sash. Aluminium's strength and stiffness allow much slimmer sightlines than PVC-U or timber of equal size, which is a large part of its appeal for modern buildings. But slimmer means less section to carry wind load and less space for a thermal break, so appearance and performance must be designed together.

Style and context

Minimal, flush profiles suit contemporary buildings; stepped or bevelled profiles and applied glazing bars suit traditional and heritage settings. Steel-look slim profiles mimic historic steel windows with better thermal performance.

Proportion and grid

The ratio of glass to wall and the grid of mullions and transoms shape the façade. Many designers use repeating modules or classical proportions such as the golden ratio to organise openings, while matching structural spans to profile capacity.

Size

Large openings bring daylight and views but raise wind load, glass weight and heat loss or gain. Window-to-wall ratio is often limited by energy codes; balance daylighting with solar control.

Colour

Anodised finishes give a metallic, durable look in natural, bronze and black tones; powder coatings offer any RAL or custom colour, textures and wood-grain effects. Dual-colour profiles (different inside and outside) are possible with thermally broken sections.

Shape

Arched, raked and curved units are produced by bending profiles. Tight radii and large sections increase cost and need suitable alloy and temper; discuss bend radii with the profile supplier.

Hardware visibility

Concealed hinges, flush handles and hidden drainage keep elevations clean but require profile chambers and grooves designed for them.

Structure

Structural design: wind load, deflection and glass weight

The frame must carry wind pressure and suction to the building structure without excessive deflection, and support the dead weight of the glass. Design wind pressures come from national codes (for example EN 1991-1-4, ASCE 7 or IS 875 Part 3) and increase with building height, exposure and position on the façade, with corners and edges carrying the highest suction.

Deflection rather than strength usually governs aluminium window profiles, because aluminium's modulus of elasticity (about 70 GPa) is roughly one third that of steel. The stiffness of a mullion therefore depends on its moment of inertia, which the profile designer increases with deeper sections, thicker flanges or steel or aluminium reinforcement inside hollow chambers. Insulated glass units have their own deflection limits, and the frame must stay within them to protect the edge seals.

Alloy and temper matter for strength. 6063-T5 and 6063-T6 are standard for windows and doors; 6060-T66 is common in Europe; 6061-T6 or 6005A-T6 are used where higher strength is needed, such as large mullions and heavy sliding door tracks. Typical wall thickness for structural window profiles is 1.4 – 2.0 mm, with door profiles often 2.0 mm or more, but the required thickness should come from calculation and the system test, not a rule of thumb.

Thermal expansion is a design load too

  • Aluminium expands about 23 µm per metre per °C, roughly twice as much as steel and glass
  • A 3 m mullion heated 50 °C by sun grows about 3.5 mm
  • Allow for movement at joints, glazing pockets and anchors; use slotted fixings and expansion joints in long runs
  • Dark colours run much hotter than light ones in sunlight, increasing movement
Glass

Glazing design: safety, weight and support

Glass is usually the heaviest part of a window and the most important for energy performance. The profile must provide a glazing pocket deep enough for the glass thickness plus gaskets, setting blocks to carry the glass weight to the frame without point loading, and drainage so that water entering the pocket escapes outside.

  • Use toughened or laminated safety glass where codes require it: doors, low-level glazing, balustrades and overhead glazing
  • Laminated glass also improves acoustic performance and security
  • Check sash and hinge capacity for the weight of triple or laminated glazing; a 1 × 2 m triple unit can exceed 60 kg
  • Allow edge clearance so that glass never touches aluminium, and specify compatible sealants and gaskets (EPDM is standard)
  • Confirm the glazing bead design for the glass thickness range; thicker units often need deeper profiles

Energy

Energy-efficient design: thermal breaks, glass and air-tightness

Heat moves through a window in three ways: conduction through frame and glass, convection through air leakage and inside cavities, and radiation through the glass. Good design addresses all three. Aluminium conducts heat very well, so an aluminium frame without a thermal break performs poorly; with a well-designed thermal break it can meet demanding energy codes.

Values are indicative. Window U-values (Uw) must be calculated to EN ISO 10077-1/-2 or measured, and in North America rated to NFRC procedures, for each specific system and size.

Polyamide strip thermal breaks

Glass-fibre reinforced polyamide (PA66 GF25) strips are rolled into knurled grooves in inner and outer aluminium profiles, creating a composite section. Strip width, typically from about 14 to over 30 mm, and foam inserts in the cavity set the thermal performance. Shear strength of the composite is verified to EN 14024.

Pour-and-debridge thermal breaks

A polyurethane resin is poured into a channel in the profile and cured, then the aluminium bridge beneath is cut away. Common in North America; compact and strong, with design attention to adhesion and shrinkage.

Warm-edge spacers

Replacing aluminium spacers in insulated glass units with stainless or polymer warm-edge spacers reduces edge heat loss and condensation at the glass perimeter.

Air-tightness

Multiple compression gaskets and a well-designed corner seal minimise air leakage, classified under EN 12207 (classes 1 – 4). Air leakage often costs more energy than frame conduction in windy locations.

Solar control

Solar-control low-E glass, external shading, louvres and brise-soleil reduce cooling load in hot climates. Aluminium louvre and fin profiles are a natural partner to aluminium window systems.

Water-tightness and drainage

Pressure-equalised designs with drained cavities and baffled outlets keep water out, classified under EN 12208. Drainage slots must be machined where the system drawing shows them.

Project specifications usually define classes for air, water and wind together, for example "air class 4, water class 9A, wind class C4", plus a maximum window U-value. Performance is proven by testing representative units of the system, so fabricators should build to the tested system details: changing a profile, gasket or drainage position can invalidate the test evidence.

Pretreatment is as important as the coating. In coastal locations specify "seaside class" pretreatment (deeper pre-etch under Qualicoat) to resist filiform corrosion. Use stainless steel fasteners with isolating washers where they contact aluminium, avoid direct contact with copper, and keep mortar and cleaning chemicals off finished profiles during construction.

Buildability

Fabrication and assembly: cutting, machining and corner joints

  1. CuttingDouble-mitre saws cut frames at 45° for mitred corners or 90° for butt joints. Accurate, burr-free cuts are essential for tight, sealed corners.
  2. MachiningCopy routers or CNC profile machining centres cut drainage slots, hardware pockets, lock cases and fixing holes to the system drawings.
  3. Corner joiningMitred corners are joined with aluminium corner cleats, crimped or pinned in a corner-crimping machine, or with screwed cleats; two-part corner adhesive seals and bonds the joint. Butt joints use screws into screw ports, often with end seals.
  4. Mullion and transom jointsMechanical T-joints with cleats, screws and sealing pads; profile ends are notched to fit.
  5. Hardware and gasketsFit hinges, locks, rollers and gaskets per the system; continuous gaskets with vulcanised or bonded corners give the best seal.
  6. Glazing and inspectionGlaze with setting blocks and beads; check diagonals, squareness, operation and sealing before dispatch.

Corner integrity is the most common long-term weakness. Use the cleats and adhesives the system specifies, apply enough adhesive to fill the joint, and protect anodised or coated faces during crimping. Fasteners should be stainless steel, and rivets or screws should be compatible with aluminium to avoid galvanic corrosion.

For buyers

Checklist for specifying window and door profiles

  • Profile drawings with die numbers or system references, and critical dimensions and tolerances (EN 12020-2 for precision 6060/6063 profiles)
  • Alloy and temper (e.g. 6063-T5, 6063-T6, 6060-T66) and any mechanical property minimums
  • Thermal-break type, strip width and composite shear values required
  • Finish: anodic film class and colour, or powder coating class, RAL colour and gloss; seaside pretreatment where needed
  • Lengths (commonly 5.8 or 6.0 m for container shipment) and cut-to-length requirements
  • Protective film and packing so that finished faces are not damaged in transit
  • Certificates: EN 10204 3.1 mill test certificate, finish thickness reports, and any third-party inspection

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) window and door design: FAQs

Which aluminium alloy is used for window and door profiles?

6063 in T5 or T6 temper is the most widely used, with 6060-T66 common in Europe. Both extrude well into complex thin sections and take excellent anodised and powder-coated finishes. 6061-T6 or 6005A-T6 is used for heavily loaded mullions and door tracks.

Are aluminium windows energy efficient?

With a thermal break, low-E double or triple glazing, warm-edge spacers and good gaskets, aluminium windows can meet modern energy codes. Without a thermal break, aluminium frames conduct heat readily and can cause condensation, so thermally broken profiles are standard in most climates.

What wall thickness should window profiles have?

Structural window profiles are commonly about 1.4 – 2.0 mm and door profiles 2.0 mm or more, but the right thickness comes from the wind-load calculation, the system test and any national minimums. Thinner walls also limit the strength of screw fixings.

What is the difference between polyamide and pour-and-debridge thermal breaks?

Polyamide breaks join inner and outer profiles with glass-fibre reinforced polyamide strips rolled into grooves; they allow wide breaks and dual colours. Pour-and-debridge breaks fill a channel with polyurethane and then cut the aluminium bridge away; they are compact and common in North America.

Anodising or powder coating for aluminium windows?

Powder coating offers any colour and is the most common choice. Anodising gives a metallic look and very hard, abrasion-resistant surface. For coastal sites, specify anodic film of 25 µm or powder coating with seaside-class pretreatment.

Does Metline make windows and doors?

No. Metline supplies the aluminium materials, such as window and door profiles, thermal-break profiles and sheet, to fabricators and system companies, who design, fabricate and install the finished windows and doors.
