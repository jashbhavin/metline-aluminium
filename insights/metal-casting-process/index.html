25 Terms for Metal Casting Properties and Processes

Casting turns molten metal into a shaped part or a semi-finished ingot in a single step, and almost every aluminium product starts life this way. This glossary explains 25 core casting terms, from mould types and melt refining to filling, solidification and shrinkage, with notes on what each one means in aluminium practice.

Context

Why casting terms matter to aluminium buyers

Aluminium is cast in two broad ways. Shape casting produces near-net parts such as wheels, housings and engine blocks from casting alloys like A356 or AlSi9Cu3. Ingot casting, mostly by direct-chill (DC) casting, produces the rolling slabs, extrusion billets and forging stock from which sheet, plate, profiles and forgings are made.

Defects created at this stage, such as gas porosity, oxide films, segregation and shrinkage cavities, can survive through rolling or extrusion and appear later as blisters, laminations or ultrasonic indications. Understanding the vocabulary helps when reading foundry specifications, ingot certificates and defect reports.

Terms 1 – 9

Casting processes, moulds and melt treatment

#TermMeaning, with the aluminium angle
1CastingPouring molten metal into a mould and letting it solidify into a part or ingot of defined shape, size and properties.
2Sand castingCasting in moulds of bonded sand. Suits large or low-volume aluminium parts; covered by standards such as ASTM B26.
3Special castingAny method other than conventional sand casting: permanent-mould (gravity die), high-pressure die, low-pressure, investment, lost-foam, squeeze and continuous casting.
4Casting mouldThe cavity-forming tool, expendable (sand, ceramic shell) or permanent (steel dies). Aluminium’s low melting point makes reusable steel dies practical.
5IngotMetal cast into a simple shape for remelting or further working: remelt ingot, foundry alloy ingot, rolling slab or extrusion billet.
6Duplex (tandem) meltingMelting in one furnace and holding or refining in a second, for example a melting furnace feeding a holding furnace on a DC casting line.
7RefiningRemoving dissolved hydrogen, inclusions and unwanted elements from the melt. In aluminium this means fluxing, rotary degassing with argon or nitrogen, and ceramic foam filtration.
8Vacuum refiningRefining under reduced pressure so dissolved gases escape. More common for specialty metals; aluminium usually relies on inert-gas degassing.
9External (in-line) refiningTreating the melt outside the furnace, typically in an in-line degassing unit and filter box between furnace and casting machine.
Terms 10 – 14

Filling and pouring

#TermMeaning, with the aluminium angle
10Fluidity (filling capacity)The ability of molten metal to fill the mould and reproduce fine detail. Al-Si alloys near the eutectic (about 12 % Si) have the best fluidity, which is why they dominate die casting.
11Filling velocityThe rate at which metal enters the cavity through the ingate. Aluminium is sensitive to turbulence, which folds oxide films into the casting, so gating keeps velocity low in gravity processes.
12Filling timeTime to fill the cavity completely. Too slow causes cold shuts and misruns; too fast causes turbulence and trapped air.
13Pouring temperatureMelt temperature at pouring. For most aluminium casting alloys it lies roughly between 680 and 760 °C, depending on alloy, section and process.
14Pouring timeTime from the start to the end of pouring. In gravity casting it is set together with filling velocity through gating design.
Terms 15 – 20

Solidification

#TermMeaning, with the aluminium angle
15SolidificationTransformation from liquid to solid as temperature falls through the liquidus and solidus. Most aluminium alloys solidify over a temperature range, forming dendrites.
16Solidification timeTime for a section to solidify completely; it rises roughly with the square of section modulus (Chvorinov’s rule). Fast solidification gives finer structure and better properties.
17Equilibrium solidificationTheoretical solidification slow enough for complete diffusion, so composition is uniform. Real castings cool too fast for this.
18Simultaneous solidificationDesign aim where all sections solidify at about the same time, used for thin, uniform castings to minimise stress and distortion.
19Directional (sequential) solidificationSolidification progressing from thin sections towards risers, so liquid feed metal can compensate shrinkage. Essential for sound aluminium sand and gravity castings.
20Solidification without solute redistributionVery rapid solidification in which solute is trapped in the solid, as in melt spinning or splat cooling; it produces supersaturated, fine structures.
Terms 21 – 25

Contraction and shrinkage

#TermMeaning, with the aluminium angle
21Contraction (shrinkage)Reduction in volume and dimensions as metal cools from pouring temperature to room temperature.
22Liquid contractionVolume reduction of the melt while still liquid; compensated by feeding from the pouring system.
23Solidification contractionVolume reduction on changing from liquid to solid. Pure aluminium shrinks by roughly 6 – 7 % by volume; high-silicon alloys shrink less because silicon expands as it solidifies.
24Solid contractionDimensional reduction of the solid casting as it cools. Pattern makers allow roughly 1.3 % linear shrinkage for aluminium.
25Liquid-solid contractionTotal of liquid and solidification contraction; if not fed, it forms shrinkage cavities and porosity.

Common aluminium casting defects and their roots

  • Gas porosity: hydrogen picked up from moisture; controlled by degassing and dry tools
  • Shrinkage porosity: poor feeding; fixed by risers, chills and directional solidification
  • Oxide films (bifilms): turbulent pouring; fixed by gating design and filtration
  • Hot tearing: restrained contraction in long-freezing-range alloys; fixed by alloy choice and mould design

FAQ

Metal casting: frequently asked questions

What is the most common way to cast aluminium?

By volume, high-pressure die casting dominates shape casting, while direct-chill casting produces the slabs and billets for almost all wrought products. Sand and permanent-mould casting cover larger or lower-volume parts.

What temperature is aluminum poured at?

Most aluminium casting alloys are poured between roughly 680 and 760 °C. The exact figure depends on the alloy, section thickness and process.

Why do aluminium castings have porosity?

Mainly from dissolved hydrogen coming out of solution as the metal solidifies, and from shrinkage where sections are not fed. Degassing, filtration and good gating and riser design reduce both.

How much does aluminium shrink when cast?

Pure aluminium contracts about 6 – 7 % by volume on solidification, and pattern allowances for solid contraction are about 1.3 % linear. High-silicon alloys shrink less.

What is the difference between an ingot and a billet?

An ingot is any simple cast shape for remelting or working; a billet is a round DC-cast log specifically for extrusion or forging, while a slab is a rectangular ingot for rolling.
