Busbar Terminology and Practical Selection Guide

Choosing a busbar comes down to five decisions: the conductor material and alloy, the cross-section that keeps temperature rise within limits, the shape (flat, tubular, laminated or flexible), how it is insulated and supported, and how its joints are made. This guide defines the terms you will meet in specifications and walks through each decision, with a focus on aluminium busbar systems.

Terminology

Busbar product types explained

Specifications often use the same word for different things: "busbar", "bus conductor", "busway" and "busduct" are not interchangeable. Getting the terminology right avoids quoting the wrong product.

TermWhat it isTypical use
Flat busbar (bus bar, bus conductor)Solid rectangular bar, often with rounded edges, bare or platedSwitchgear, panel boards, distribution boards, earthing
Tubular bus pipe (buspipe)Round or square hollow conductor; better heat dissipation and stiffness over long spans, less skin effectOutdoor and indoor substations, generator connections
Copper-clad aluminium (CCA) busbarAluminium core with a bonded copper skinWeight- and cost-sensitive panels needing a copper contact surface
Laminated busbarThin conductor layers separated by insulating film, bonded into one assemblyInverters, UPS, traction drives, EV power electronics (low inductance)
Insulated busbarBusbar with epoxy powder, sleeve, film or overmould insulationCompact high-voltage assemblies, EV packs, switchgear
Flexible busbarStacked thin foils or braided conductor, often with welded endsConnections that absorb vibration, thermal movement and tolerance
Coiled busbarLong lengths of busbar strip supplied in coils for cutting and forming on site or in productionHigh-volume panel building and cable-tray systems
Busway / busductEnclosed, prefabricated busbar trunking system with housings and tap-off pointsBuilding and data-centre power distribution

Accessories complete the system: busbar connectors and clamps join bars and take off branches; bimetallic connectors and washers join aluminium to copper without galvanic corrosion; expansion joints (flexible links) absorb thermal movement; supports and insulators hold bars at fixed spacing against short-circuit forces; and insulating sleeves and shrouds protect against accidental contact.

Material

Selecting the conductor material and aluminium alloy

Electrical-grade aluminium offers about 60 % of the conductivity of copper at about 30 % of the density, so for equal resistance an aluminium bar is larger but roughly half the weight. The alloy then trades conductivity against strength.

Electrical performance

Key electrical parameters and what they mean

Current-carrying capacity (ampacity)

The current a bar can carry continuously without exceeding its permitted temperature rise. It depends on cross-section, shape, orientation, number of bars in parallel, surface finish, enclosure and ambient temperature, not on material alone.

Resistance and voltage drop

R = ρL/A. For long runs, voltage drop (I × R) can govern sizing before temperature does. Aluminium at 61 % IACS has a resistivity of about 2.8 µΩ·cm at 20 °C, rising about 0.4 % per °C.

Power loss

I²R loss heats the bar and wastes energy. Over a 25-year life, a modest increase in cross-section often pays for itself in lower losses.

Skin effect

At AC, current crowds towards the conductor surface. The skin depth in aluminium is about 12 mm at 50 Hz (about 11 mm at 60 Hz), so bars thicker than about 2 × the skin depth use their centre poorly. Use several thinner bars in parallel or tubular conductors instead.

Proximity effect

Nearby conductors carrying AC distort each other's current distribution, increasing resistance. Phase arrangement and spacing matter in multi-bar systems.

Eddy currents and inductance

Steel supports or enclosures around high-current bars heat up from induced currents; use non-magnetic materials near bars. Laminated busbars reduce loop inductance for fast-switching power electronics.

A note on surface treatments: plating with tin or silver improves and stabilises contact resistance at joints, and dark or matte surfaces radiate heat slightly better than bright ones. Neither changes the bulk conductivity of the bar or the eddy-current behaviour of the system.

Sizing

A practical sizing procedure

  1. Define the dutyContinuous current, overloads, harmonic content, ambient temperature, enclosure and permitted temperature rise (assembly standards such as IEC 61439 set limits at terminals and conductors).
  2. Choose material and alloyHigh-conductivity 1350/1060 for flat bars; 6101 where the bar must be self-supporting; tubular 6063/6101 for long outdoor spans.
  3. Select a trial sectionUse manufacturer or handbook ampacity tables (for example the Aluminum Association conductor handbook) as a starting point, then correct for enclosure, orientation and parallel bars.
  4. Check voltage drop and lossesCalculate I × R and I²R for the actual length; increase the section if drop or loss is excessive.
  5. Check short-circuit withstandVerify thermal withstand for the fault duration and mechanical forces on supports, typically following IEC 60865 methods.
  6. Design the joints and supportsSpecify overlap length, bolt size, plating, washers and support spacing; joints should run no hotter than the bar.
  7. Allow for expansionAluminium expands about 23 µm per metre per °C: a 30 m run rising 50 °C grows by about 35 mm, so long runs need flexible expansion joints.

Joints

Making reliable aluminium busbar joints

Most busbar failures start at joints, not in the bar. Aluminium has a hard surface oxide and creeps slowly under sustained bolt load, so joints must be designed to keep contact pressure for decades.

  • Clean contact faces by wire brushing or abrading, then apply a suitable jointing compound immediately
  • Use tin- or silver-plated contact areas for stable contact resistance, especially where aluminium meets copper
  • Use high-strength bolts with Belleville (disc spring) washers and wide flat washers to maintain clamping force as aluminium creeps
  • Torque to a specified value and re-check where the procedure requires
  • Join aluminium to copper through bimetal washers or transition plates, or plate the aluminium, to control galvanic corrosion
  • Make overlap length at least comparable to the bar width so joint resistance stays below that of an equal length of bar

FAQ

Busbar selection: frequently asked questions

Is an aluminium busbar as good as copper?

For most distribution uses, yes, if it is sized correctly. An aluminium bar needs about 1.6 times the cross-section of copper for the same resistance but weighs about half as much and costs less. Joints need more care because of aluminium's oxide and creep.

Which aluminum alloy is best for busbars?

1350 or 1060 when conductivity is the priority and the bar is well supported; 6101 in T6 or an over-aged temper when the bar must carry mechanical load; 6063 or 6101 for tubular bus pipe.

How do I calculate busbar current capacity?

Start from ampacity tables for the material and section, then correct for enclosure, ambient temperature, orientation and parallel bars, and confirm temperature rise by calculation or test to the assembly standard. Check voltage drop and short-circuit withstand as well.

Why use several thin bars instead of one thick bar?

Because of skin effect. In aluminium at 50 Hz, current penetrates about 12 mm, so the centre of a very thick bar carries little current. Several thinner bars with gaps also cool better.

When should a laminated busbar be used?

In fast-switching power electronics such as inverters, UPS and traction drives, where low inductance reduces voltage spikes and electromagnetic interference.

How do you connect aluminium busbar to copper?

Use a bimetallic washer or transition plate, or tin-plate the aluminium contact area, apply jointing compound and use disc-spring washers to keep clamping force. Keep the joint dry.
