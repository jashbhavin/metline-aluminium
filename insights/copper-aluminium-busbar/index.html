Copper vs Aluminium vs Copper-Clad Aluminium Busbars

Copper gives the most current in the smallest cross-section; aluminium gives the same current at roughly half the weight and a fraction of the metal cost, provided the bar is about 1.6 times larger and the joints are designed for aluminium; copper-clad aluminium (CCA) sits in between, with a copper surface for easy connections over a lighter aluminium core. The right choice depends on whether space, weight, cost or connection simplicity limits your design.

Our view

The short answer

There is no universal winner. Each conductor is the economical answer for a different constraint, and many good switchboards combine them: aluminium for long runs, copper or CCA at crowded terminations.

If your main constraint is…Usually chooseWhy
Space in the enclosureCopperHighest conductivity per square millimetre, so the smallest bar for a given current
Weight (vehicles, aircraft, raised structures)AluminiumAbout half the weight of copper for equal resistance

CCA figures are rule-of-mixtures estimates for a bar with 20 % copper by volume; actual products vary with the cladding ratio, so use the supplier's measured values.

The two numbers that drive every comparison are conductivity and density. Aluminium conducts about 61 % as well as copper by volume, but it weighs only about 30 % as much. Per kilogram, aluminium therefore conducts about twice as well as copper. That single fact explains why overhead transmission lines are almost entirely aluminium, while compact switchgear, where space is scarce, still uses a lot of copper.

Engineering

How to size an aluminium busbar to replace copper

For the same DC resistance (and therefore the same I²R loss), an aluminium bar needs about 1.6 times the cross-sectional area of a copper bar: 100 ÷ 61 ≈ 1.64 for 1350, and a little more for 6101. Because aluminium is so light, that larger bar still weighs only about half as much.

Ampacity is not set by resistance alone. A busbar's rating is the current that holds its temperature rise within the limit of the assembly standard (IEC 61439 for low-voltage switchgear, for example), and that depends on surface area, orientation, spacing, enclosure ventilation and emissivity. Because the larger aluminium bar also has more surface to shed heat, the equal-temperature replacement is often slightly smaller than the equal-resistance one. As a rough rule, an aluminium bar of the same dimensions as a copper bar carries about 75 – 80 % of the copper bar's current. Always confirm ratings from tested data or the assembly manufacturer's design rules.

Short-circuit withstand also needs checking. Aluminium's lower melting point and heat capacity per volume mean its permissible short-time current density is lower than copper's; the larger cross-section usually compensates, but bracing and support spacing must be verified for electromagnetic forces.

Connections

Joints and terminations: where aluminium needs care

Most problems blamed on aluminium busbars are really joint problems. Three properties make an aluminium joint behave differently from a copper one, and each has a straightforward engineering answer.

Surface oxide

Aluminium forms a thin, hard, insulating oxide film within seconds of exposure. Remove it by wire-brushing or abrading just before assembly and immediately apply an oxide-inhibiting contact compound, or use plated contact faces.

Creep and relaxation

Soft aluminium (especially 1350) slowly yields under bolt pressure, particularly when warm, so clamping force can fall over time. Use 6101 or harder tempers at joints, large flat washers, and conical spring (Belleville) washers to maintain contact pressure.

Thermal expansion

Aluminium expands about 40 % more than copper and much more than steel bolts. Spring washers and correct torque accommodate the differential movement through daily load cycles.

Galvanic corrosion

Where aluminium touches copper in the presence of moisture, the aluminium corrodes. Use tin- or silver-plated contact faces, bimetallic copper-aluminium transition pieces or washers, and keep the joint dry or sealed.

Plating

Tin plating is the common choice for aluminium busbar contact areas; silver is used where higher temperature or lower contact resistance is required. Plating also simplifies connection to copper equipment.

Welding

Aluminium busbars can be joined permanently by TIG or MIG welding, or by friction-stir welding, which removes bolted-joint maintenance in long runs.

Common mistakes when switching from copper to aluminium

  • Reusing the copper drawing with the same bar size, which overloads the aluminium bar thermally
  • Bolting bare aluminium to bare copper without plating or a bimetallic transition piece
  • Using plain flat washers on soft 1350 bars, so clamping force relaxes after a few heating cycles
  • Skipping contact compound on freshly cleaned faces, letting the oxide film reform before assembly
  • Not re-checking support spacing and bracing for short-circuit forces on the larger, lighter bars

CCA reduces several of these issues at the contact face, because the surface is copper and can be bolted, crimped or plated like copper. It does not remove them inside the bar: the aluminium core still expands more than copper and the cladding bond must stay intact. At cut ends and drilled holes the aluminium core is exposed, so those surfaces should be protected, especially in humid environments.

Copper-clad aluminium

What copper-clad aluminium busbar is, and what to check

Copper-clad aluminium is a bimetal: an aluminium core metallurgically bonded to an outer layer of copper. For busbars, the cladding is usually applied by roll bonding or by co-extrusion/drawing of a copper sleeve over an aluminium bar, followed by heat treatment to create a diffusion bond. The copper share is usually specified by volume or by thickness of the cladding layer.

Because conductivity and density scale with the copper share, CCA is best thought of as a continuum between aluminium and copper. More copper gives higher conductivity and easier terminations but more weight and cost. Unlike CCA wire, which is covered by ASTM B566 with defined copper classes, there is no single widely adopted international standard for CCA busbar, so the purchase specification must define what you need.

  • Copper ratio: state the minimum copper share by volume or minimum cladding thickness on each face and edge
  • Measured conductivity: request resistance or % IACS test results for the actual bar, not a theoretical figure
  • Bond integrity: require bend, peel or shear tests showing no separation of copper from aluminium
  • Edge coverage: confirm the copper continues around edges, since thin edge cladding is a common weak point
  • Fabrication limits: agree minimum bend radius and hole-to-edge distances to avoid cracking the bond
  • End protection: specify how cut ends and holes will be sealed or plated

One point where we disagree with many published comparisons is sustainability. Aluminium and copper each recycle indefinitely in clean, single-metal scrap streams. CCA saves metal and transport energy during use, but at end of life it is a mixed-metal product, and separating the copper from the aluminium costs more than recycling either metal alone. Count that if your project has recycled-content or end-of-life targets.

Applications

Typical applications for each conductor

  • Aluminium: busway and rising mains, substation bus, smelter and electrolysis bus, data-centre power distribution, transformer connections, solar and battery storage combiner boxes, EV battery module busbars
  • CCA: switchgear and distribution boards where copper terminations are standard, inverter and UPS internal links, battery interconnects where weight and contact resistance both matter
  • Copper (reference): compact motor control centres, very high-density switchboards and short internal links where space is the binding constraint

FAQ

Copper, aluminium and CCA (aluminum) busbars: FAQ

How much bigger does an aluminium busbar need to be than copper?

For equal resistance, about 1.6 times the cross-sectional area for 1350 aluminium and slightly more for 6101. For equal temperature rise the factor can be a little lower because the larger bar sheds heat better; confirm with the assembly standard or tested ratings.

Is an aluminum busbar safe in switchgear?

Yes, when the assembly is designed and tested for it. Aluminium busbars are widely used in busway, switchboards and substations. The key is correct joint design: clean contact faces, contact compound or plating, spring washers and proper torque.

What is the conductivity of copper-clad aluminium busbar?

It depends on the copper share. As a guide, the DC conductivity lies between about 61 % IACS (all aluminium) and 100 % IACS (all copper) in proportion to the copper volume. Ask for measured values for the specific product.

Can aluminium and copper busbars be bolted together?

Yes, but not bare. Use tin-plated faces, a bimetallic copper-aluminium transition piece or washer, and an inhibiting compound, and keep the joint dry. Direct bare contact in moist air leads to galvanic corrosion of the aluminium.

Which is better for EV battery busbars, aluminium or CCA?

Aluminium is lighter and matches aluminium cell terminals, avoiding galvanic couples; CCA or copper is used where terminals are copper or contact resistance must be very low. See our battery busbar insight for the detailed trade-offs.

Does Metline supply copper busbars?

No. Metline supplies aluminium only, including 1350 and 6101 busbar, extruded bus sections, copper-clad aluminium and copper-aluminium bimetallic transition parts.
