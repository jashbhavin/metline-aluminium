4043 vs 5356: Choosing an Aluminium Filler Wire

Use ER4043 when crack resistance, a fluid weld pool and good results on 6xxx extrusions and castings matter most; use ER5356 when you need higher shear strength, ductility, a colour match after anodising or you are welding 5xxx alloys. Neither wire is "better": the base metal, service temperature and finish decide the choice.

These two alloys cover most aluminium MIG and TIG work worldwide. They are classified in AWS A5.10 as ER4043 and ER5356 (R4043 / R5356 for cut-length TIG rod) and in ISO 18273 as S Al 4043 (AlSi5) and S Al 5356 (AlMg5Cr(A)).

Silicon forms a low-melting aluminium-silicon eutectic. During solidification that liquid back-fills the shrinkage cracks that would otherwise open between grains, which is the real reason 4043 resists hot cracking so well. Magnesium in 5356 strengthens the deposit by solid solution, giving a tougher and more ductile weld, but it gives less eutectic liquid to heal cracks on crack-sensitive base metals.

The melting ranges of the two wires are close (roughly 575 – 630 °C for 4043 and 570 – 635 °C for 5356), so the practical difference at the arc is fluidity and freezing behaviour, not a markedly lower melting point.

Weld integrity

Crack sensitivity: why 6xxx alloys favour 4043

Heat-treatable 6xxx alloys such as 6061 and 6082 contain around 1 % of magnesium silicide formers. At that composition the weld metal is close to its peak crack sensitivity, and welding without filler, or with too little dilution, often cracks. Adding filler moves the weld chemistry away from the crack-sensitive range. 4043 does this by raising silicon; 5356 does it by raising magnesium well above the sensitive level.

Both work on 6xxx alloys in normal joints. 4043 is more forgiving of poor fit-up, high restraint, thin sections and high dilution (for example square butt welds and root passes), where 5356 can crack. 5356 is preferred on 5xxx base metals, because combining a 4xxx filler with high-magnesium base metal forms brittle magnesium silicide in the weld.

Rules that prevent most filler mistakes

  • Do not use 4043 on base metals with more than about 2.5 % magnesium, such as 5083, 5086 and 5456
  • Do not use 5356 on Al-Si castings such as A356; use 4043 (or 4047 for higher silicon)
  • Avoid 5356 where the part runs continuously above about 65 °C; use 5554 on 5454 or a 4xxx filler on 6xxx
  • If the welded part will be solution heat-treated and aged, consider 4643 instead of 4043
Joint strength

Weld strength: groove welds versus fillet welds

Strength comparisons between the two wires are often quoted without context. Two situations behave very differently:

Groove (butt) welds in 6061-T6. The weakest zone is usually the heat-affected base metal next to the weld, not the filler. Design codes such as the Aluminum Design Manual and AWS D1.2 assign a single minimum as-welded tensile strength (165 MPa / 24 ksi for 6061-T6) whichever of the two fillers is used. Choosing 5356 does not make a 6061 butt joint stronger on paper.

Fillet welds. Here the load passes through the weld metal in shear, so the filler matters a great deal. Minimum shear strengths commonly used in design are listed below.

The practical result: a fillet-welded bracket made with 5356 can be designed with considerably smaller welds than one made with 4043. In structures dominated by fillet welds, such as frames, trailers and marine outfitting, that is often the deciding factor.

Finishing

Bead appearance, anodising and coatings

As welded, both wires leave a bright bead. 4043 wets out to a flatter, smoother bead with less spatter, and produces little black smut. 5356 leaves a slightly more crowned bead and more smut, which wipes off but should be cleaned before coating.

The difference shows after anodising. Silicon particles in a 4043 weld do not anodise like the surrounding metal, so the seam turns grey, often dark charcoal. A 5356 weld anodises to a much closer match with 5xxx and 6xxx base metals. For anodised architectural, consumer and decorative parts, 5356 is the default; where 4043 has to be used for crack resistance, place welds where they will be hidden.

Under paint, powder coat or e-coat, the colour difference is invisible, so the filler can be chosen on strength and crack resistance alone.

FAQ

4043 vs 5356 aluminium filler: frequently asked questions

Is 5356 stronger than 4043?

In fillet welds, yes: its minimum shear strength is roughly 50 – 70 % higher. In butt welds on 6061-T6 the heat-affected base metal usually limits strength, and design codes give the same as-welded minimum for either filler.

Can I use 4043 on 5083 aluminum?

No. 5083 contains about 4 – 4.9 % magnesium, and a silicon-rich filler would form brittle magnesium silicide in the weld. Use 5183 or 5356.

Which wire should I use for 6061?

Both are accepted. Choose 4043 for crack-prone joints, thin sections and parts that will be painted; choose 5356 for fillet-weld strength, ductility and anodised parts.

Why does my 4043 weld turn black after anodising?

Silicon particles in the weld do not form the same clear oxide film as the base metal, so the seam looks grey to dark grey. Use 5356 when appearance after anodising matters.

Can 5356 be used for high-temperature service?

Not for sustained service above about 65 °C. Alloys with more than 3 % magnesium can become sensitised to stress-corrosion cracking. Use 5554 or a 4xxx filler, depending on the base metal.

Which wire feeds better through a MIG gun?

5356 is stiffer and pushes more reliably through long liners. 4043 is softer and benefits from a push-pull gun or spool gun, U-groove rolls and low drive-roll pressure.
