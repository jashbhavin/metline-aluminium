Aluminium Foam: How It Is Made and Where It Earns Its Place

Aluminium foam is solid aluminium filled with gas pores, typically 70 – 90 % of its volume, which makes it light, stiff for its weight, non-combustible and very good at absorbing impact energy. It is made either by foaming molten aluminium or by expanding a compacted powder precursor, and the route chosen largely fixes its cell structure, density and cost. This article explains each manufacturing method, gives realistic property ranges, and looks at where foam outperforms honeycomb, solid sheet and polymer cores, and where it does not.

The material

What is aluminium foam?

A metal foam is a cellular solid: a network of thin metal walls or struts enclosing gas-filled pores. In aluminium foam, those walls are aluminium or an aluminium alloy, and the pores are usually a few millimetres across. Because most of the volume is empty, a block of foam may weigh a fifth or less of the same block in solid aluminium, yet it still behaves as a metal: it conducts heat and electricity, does not burn, does not absorb water into its walls and can be cut, drilled and bonded.

Two families exist, and they should never be confused in a specification. Closed-cell foam has sealed pores, each surrounded by a continuous wall. It is the stiffer and stronger of the two for its weight and is the form used for structural cores, crash absorbers and most architectural panels. Open-cell foam (sometimes called metal sponge) consists of a connected skeleton of struts, so air and liquids can pass straight through it. It is used for heat exchangers, filters, flame arrestors and high-performance sound absorbers.

A third term, relative density, is the most useful single number for any foam. It is the foam density divided by the density of the solid alloy. Commercial closed-cell aluminium foams generally fall between about 0.07 and 0.25 relative density; in other words, 75 – 93 % of the volume is porosity. Almost every mechanical and thermal property scales with relative density, which is why two foams of the same alloy can behave very differently.

Why engineers consider aluminium foam

  • High stiffness-to-weight in bending when used as a sandwich core
  • A long, flat stress plateau in compression, ideal for absorbing crash and impact energy
  • Non-combustible, with no toxic smoke, unlike polymer foams
  • Can be shaped into near-net 3D parts and foamed inside tubes and hollow sections
  • Fully recyclable as aluminium at end of life
Preparation

How aluminium foam is made: the main routes

Researchers have demonstrated dozens of ways to make metal foam, but only a few are used at industrial scale for aluminium. They fall into three groups: foaming a liquid metal, expanding a solid powder precursor, and building foam around a pattern or space holder. The method determines whether the cells are open or closed, how uniform they are, which alloys can be used and what shapes are possible.

Liquid-metal foaming

Melt-based foaming in more detail

The central problem in foaming liquid aluminium is that bubbles in a clean melt rise and burst within seconds. Both melt routes solve it by raising the effective viscosity of the liquid so that films between bubbles drain slowly enough to freeze.

In the gas injection route, fine ceramic particles, typically a few to a few tens of percent by volume, sit at the gas-metal interfaces and stabilise the bubble walls. Gas is fed through a hollow rotating shaft so that bubbles are small and well dispersed. The foam collects on the surface of the bath and is pulled off on a conveyor as a continuous slab, where it solidifies. Densities are low and panels can be large, which suits architectural and acoustic products. The trade-offs are relatively large cells, some variation in density through the thickness and the abrasive ceramic content, which wears cutting tools.

In the blowing agent route, a small calcium addition is stirred into the melt in air. Calcium forms oxides and intermetallics that thicken the liquid. A powdered blowing agent, normally titanium hydride, is then dispersed; it decomposes and releases hydrogen, expanding the melt to fill a casting mould. After cooling, the block is sliced into sheets or panels. This route gives some of the most uniform closed-cell structures available and was the basis of the first commercially successful aluminium foam panels, used widely in Japan for noise barriers and building acoustics.

Both melt routes produce blocks or slabs that are cut to shape afterwards. They are the economical choice when flat panels or simple blocks are needed in quantity.

Powder metallurgy

The powder metallurgy route and foam sandwich panels

In the powder route, aluminium or aluminium alloy powder (commonly an Al-Si casting composition or a 6xxx alloy) is mixed with a fraction of a percent of titanium hydride. The mixture is consolidated into a fully dense "foamable precursor" by hot pressing, extrusion or rolling. At this stage it looks and behaves like ordinary metal and can be stored, cut and shaped.

Foaming happens later, when the precursor is placed in a mould or hollow part and heated just above the alloy's solidus. The softened metal expands as hydrogen is released and fills the cavity. Timing and temperature control are critical: stop too early and the foam is under-expanded and dense; heat too long and cells coarsen and collapse.

The great advantage of this route is shape freedom. A precursor can be placed inside an extruded tube to make a foam-filled crash member, or roll-clad between two aluminium sheets and then foamed to produce an aluminium foam sandwich (AFS) panel in which the skins and core are metallurgically bonded, with no adhesive line to fail in a fire or at high temperature. 3D-shaped sandwich parts can be made by forming the clad precursor before foaming. The cost is higher than for melt-route slabs, so the powder route is chosen for parts where geometry, bonding or energy absorption justifies it.

Two corrections to figures that often circulate in marketing material. First, aluminium foam is not a thermal insulator in the sense of mineral wool or polymer foam: those materials have conductivities of a few hundredths of a W/m·K, whereas aluminium foam is around a hundred times higher because the metal walls conduct heat. Foam reduces heat flow compared with solid aluminium, but it is not an insulation product. Second, closed-cell foam as cast is a modest sound absorber, because sound cannot enter sealed pores. High absorption requires opened cells: rolling, drilling, perforating or compressing the panel, or using open-cell foam, and mounting it with an air gap behind.

Mechanical behaviour

Why foam absorbs impact so well

When closed-cell foam is compressed, it first deforms elastically, then the weakest cells start to buckle and collapse. Collapse spreads through the foam band by band while the stress stays almost constant: this is the plateau. Only when the collapsed cells start touching each other, at around 50 – 70 % strain, does the stress rise steeply (densification).

For an energy absorber, that flat plateau is ideal. The force transmitted to a vehicle occupant, a payload or a structure is capped at the plateau stress multiplied by the loaded area, while a large amount of energy is dissipated per unit volume. By choosing density, a designer can tune the plateau stress to the force limit, which is not possible with a solid metal part.

Foam also works well as a filler. A thin-walled aluminium tube filled with foam folds in a more stable, regular pattern under axial crash than an empty tube, and absorbs more energy than the tube and foam would separately, because the foam supports the walls and the walls confine the foam. Crash boxes and sill reinforcements use this effect.

Compression behaviour of porous metals is measured to ISO 13314, which defines plateau stress, plateau end and energy absorption from a standard test. Specify it when comparing suppliers or densities.

Where it is used

Aluminium foam is rarely the cheapest option. Construction

Buildings, acoustics and noise barriers

In architecture, aluminium foam is valued first for its appearance: a metallic, irregular cellular surface that reads well in lobbies, retail interiors, exhibition stands and feature ceilings. Panels can be cut thin (from around 5 – 10 mm for decorative uses), backlit through perforated or very open structures, anodised, coloured or lacquered. Because the material is aluminium, it is non-combustible and does not support mould, which matters in public buildings and humid spaces.

For acoustic ceilings and wall linings, the panel must be designed to absorb rather than reflect. Surface-opened closed-cell foam, perforated panels with an air cavity behind, or open-cell foam can reach high absorption in the speech frequencies. Acoustic performance should be measured in a reverberation room to ISO 354 and reported as frequency-dependent absorption coefficients or a single-number rating, on the actual mounting arrangement proposed.

Highway and railway noise barriers were among the first large-volume uses. Foam panels resist UV, rain and frost, do not rot or absorb water like fibrous absorbers, and are light enough to reduce foundation and post sizes. In Europe, barrier acoustic performance is classified under the EN 1793 series and non-acoustic performance (wind load, self-weight, impact, fire) under EN 1794. Metline supplies foam panels for fabricators of barrier, ceiling and wall systems, along with the aluminium sheet, profiles and coil used to build them; the fabricator designs and installs the finished system.

Vehicles

Automotive, rail and marine uses

In cars and commercial vehicles, the main uses are crash management and stiffening. A foam-filled crash box or bumper beam can absorb a defined amount of energy within a short crush distance; foam-filled sills and pillars resist intrusion in side impacts; and foam inserts in hollow sections stiffen them and reduce booming noise. In electric vehicles, foam layers have been evaluated beneath battery packs to protect cells from ground impact. For further detail see our automotive aluminium foam guide.

Rail vehicles use foam sandwich panels for floors and partitions, and foam elements in crash energy absorbers at the car ends. Interior materials must meet the fire and smoke requirements of EN 45545-2 in Europe; non-combustible metal foams help, but bonded skins, adhesives and coatings must be tested as part of the panel.

In ships and offshore structures, aluminium foam sandwich panels are considered for decks, bulkheads and superstructure where weight is critical and fire behaviour matters. The closed cells do not take up water the way some core materials do, although cut edges should be sealed. Class society approval (DNV, ABS, LR, BV) is project specific and must be obtained for the panel system.

Special sectors

Defence, aerospace and nuclear applications

In defence, the attraction is energy absorption at high strain rates. Layers of foam in vehicle floors and seat mounts can spread and attenuate the pulse from a mine blast; foam behind armour can reduce back-face deformation. These are engineered systems tested to programme-specific requirements, and the foam is one component among several.

Aerospace and space uses include impact absorbers for landing systems, crushable pads, and sandwich elements that must tolerate temperature ranges where polymer cores soften. Aluminium honeycomb remains the dominant aerospace core because it gives higher stiffness for its weight in a known direction; foam is chosen where isotropic behaviour, complex shapes or multi-directional impact matter more.

In nuclear and other strategic equipment, aluminium foams have been studied for impact limiters on transport packages and for combined structural and shielding enclosures. Any such use is governed by the regulator's qualification process, and material data must be generated for the specific foam, density and temperature range.

Honeycomb gives more predictable properties and higher stiffness in flat sandwich panels, and is the right choice for most flat, lightweight panels. Foam is the better choice for crash and impact parts, curved or 3D shapes, filling hollow sections, and where fire behaviour and acoustics are combined. See aluminium honeycomb for the alternative.

Practical guidance

Working with foam panels, and what to put in an enquiry

Cutting

Band saws with fine teeth, waterjet and wire EDM give clean edges. Abrasive ceramic-stabilised foams wear tools quickly; carbide tooling helps.

Joining

Adhesive bonding is the most common method. Mechanical fixings need inserts or through-bolts with spreader plates, because foam crushes under point loads.

Finishing

Anodising, powder coating and lacquering are possible. Seal or edge-trim cut faces in exterior or wet locations.

Tolerances

Expect wider thickness and flatness tolerances than for sheet. Agree tolerances and acceptable surface variation with a sample.

  • Closed-cell or open-cell, and the purpose (structure, energy absorption, acoustic, decorative)
  • Target density or density range, with tolerance
  • Thickness, panel size and quantity; skins and skin alloy if a sandwich panel
  • Surface: natural, opened, perforated, anodised or coated
  • Test data required: ISO 13314 compression, ISO 354 acoustic, fire classification report
  • Delivery destination and packing requirements

Limitations to plan for

  • Property scatter between and within panels is higher than for solid metals or honeycomb
  • Low tensile and bending strength without skins; foam is best loaded in compression or as a sandwich core
  • Open-cell foam traps moisture and dirt; plan for cleaning and drainage
  • Panel sizes and densities differ by process, so not every combination is available

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) foam: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between closed-cell and open-cell aluminium foam?

Closed-cell foam has sealed pores and is used for structural cores, crash absorbers and most panels. Open-cell foam is a connected strut network that air and liquids pass through, used for heat exchangers, filters and high-absorption acoustic elements.

How strong is aluminium foam?

It depends mostly on density. Commercial closed-cell foams typically have compressive plateau stresses of about 1 – 15 MPa. Tensile and bending strength are low without skins, so foam is normally used in compression or as a sandwich core.

Is aluminium foam a good thermal insulator?

Not in the usual sense. Its conductivity is far lower than solid aluminium but roughly a hundred times higher than mineral wool or polymer foam insulation. Use it for fire and structural performance, not as the insulation layer.

Is aluminium foam fireproof?

It is a non-combustible metal that does not burn or produce smoke, though it melts at around 580 – 660 °C. For building or rail use, ask for the fire classification report of the complete panel, including any adhesive, skins or coating.

Does aluminium foam absorb sound?

Closed-cell foam as cast absorbs relatively little. Opening the cells by rolling, drilling or perforation, using open-cell foam, and mounting with an air gap can give high absorption. Ask for ISO 354 test data on the proposed mounting.

How is aluminium foam made?

Mainly by injecting gas into a particle-stabilised melt, by adding a blowing agent such as titanium hydride to a thickened melt, or by heating a compacted powder precursor containing a blowing agent. Open-cell foams are made by investment casting or salt space-holder methods.
