Aluminium Surface Treatment: Choosing a Finish for Performance, Not Just Looks

The right aluminium finish is chosen by what the surface must do: resist weather or salt, take wear, insulate or conduct, bond to paint or adhesive, reflect light, or simply look consistent across a façade. Anodising, organic coatings, conversion coatings, plasma electrolytic oxidation and mechanical finishes each solve a different problem, and the alloy you start with often decides which will work.

Purpose

What a surface treatment actually does

Bare aluminium already protects itself with a natural oxide film a few nanometres thick. That film is enough indoors and in many dry climates, but it is too thin to resist abrasion, too variable to look uniform and too weak against chlorides, acids and alkalis. Surface treatment replaces or builds on it.

  • Corrosion protection in coastal, industrial or de-icing-salt environments
  • Wear and friction resistance for sliding, rotating or handled parts
  • Appearance: consistent colour and gloss across batches and over decades
  • Electrical behaviour: insulation (anodic films) or retained conductivity (conversion coatings)
  • Adhesion: a stable base for paint, powder, adhesives and laminates
  • Optical properties: high reflectivity or low-glare matte surfaces
  • Cleanability and hygiene in food, medical and cleanroom equipment

Anodising

Anodising: the integral finish

In anodising, the part is the anode in an acid electrolyte (usually sulphuric acid). Current grows a porous aluminium-oxide layer out of the metal itself, so the film cannot flake off like paint. The pores can absorb dyes or be electrolytically coloured with metal salts (bronze, black, champagne), and are then sealed in hot water or a nickel-free cold seal to close them and lock in corrosion resistance.

For buildings, the European Qualanod label uses thickness classes of 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 µm, with 15 µm or more for exterior use and 20 – 25 µm for aggressive marine or industrial sites. North American practice under AAMA 611 calls for Class I (at least 18 µm) for exterior architectural work and Class II (10 – 18 µm) for less demanding exposures.

Hard anodising (MIL-A-8625 Type III, ISO 10074) runs in colder, stronger electrolyte at higher current density to grow thick, dense films. Roughly half the thickness grows outward and half inward, so allow for dimensional change on precision fits. More detail is in our anodising guide and anodising defects article.

Organic coatings

Powder coating, liquid paint and coil coating

Organic coatings give unlimited colour and are applied over a conversion-coated surface. Pretreatment is the single biggest factor in long-term performance: most filiform corrosion and peeling failures trace back to inadequate cleaning, etching or conversion coating rather than the paint itself.

Powder coating

Electrostatically sprayed polyester or polyurethane powder cured at about 180 – 200 °C. Architectural systems are qualified to Qualicoat, EN 12206-1 or AAMA 2603/2604/2605. Super-durable polyesters are the usual choice for façades; standard polyester for indoor use.

PVDF liquid coating

Fluoropolymer resin (typically 70 % PVDF) with outstanding UV and chemical resistance, used for landmark façades meeting AAMA 2605. Applied on profiles by spray or on coil by roller.

Coil coating (PE, HDPE, PVDF)

Paint applied to flat coil on continuous lines to EN 1396, then cured in seconds. Coated coil can be formed, so it suits composite panels, roofing and ceiling systems. Specify T-bend and impact requirements.

Sublimation and printed finishes

Wood, stone or image patterns transferred into a polyester coating by heat. Useful for interior décor and signage; check exterior UV durability.

Other treatments

Conversion coatings, e-coat, PEO and mechanical finishes

Conversion coatings

A chemical reaction forms a thin film that improves paint adhesion and corrosion resistance while keeping electrical contact. Hexavalent chromate (MIL-DTL-5541 Type I) is being replaced by trivalent chromium (Type II) and zirconium-titanium chemistries under REACH and RoHS.

Electrophoretic coating

After anodising, a water-based acrylic is deposited electrically and baked. The combined film is very uniform and resists mortar and alkaline cleaners well, which is why it is popular for window profiles in Asia.

Plasma electrolytic oxidation

High-voltage sparking in an alkaline electrolyte converts the surface into a hard ceramic of alumina phases. Wear resistance exceeds hard anodising, and the process works on high-silicon alloys that anodise poorly.

Brushing and polishing

Abrasive belts or wheels give a linear grain or mirror finish. Often followed by clear anodising to protect the texture.

Blasting

Glass bead or aluminium-oxide grit gives a uniform matte surface that hides machining marks and improves coating adhesion.

Chemical etch and bright dip

Caustic etching gives a soft satin matte; phosphoric-nitric bright dipping gives a near-mirror finish on suitable alloys before anodising.

Selection

Choosing the right finish: a practical sequence

  1. Define the environmentIndoor, rural, urban, coastal or industrial. Coastal and industrial sites need thicker anodising (20 – 25 µm) or PVDF / super-durable powder.
  2. Define the functionWear calls for hard anodising or PEO; electrical contact calls for conversion coating; colour freedom calls for organic coatings.
  3. Check the alloyChoose 6060, 6063 or 5005 for decorative anodising. If structural needs force 6082 or 7xxx, expect a less uniform anodised colour.
  4. Decide when to finishForm before anodising; coil-coated material can be formed after coating within its T-bend rating.
  5. Name the standardQuote ISO 7599, MIL-A-8625, Qualanod, Qualicoat, AAMA or EN 1396 so every supplier works to the same test methods.

A common mistake is specifying "anodised" without a thickness class or seal requirement, then finding that a 5 µm decorative film has failed on a seafront building. Another is hard anodising a thin-walled part without allowing for the fatigue reduction and dimensional growth.

FAQ

Aluminium surface treatment: frequently asked questions

Which is better for aluminium, anodising or powder coating?

Anodising gives a hard, integral metallic finish that cannot peel and suits wear and a natural look. Powder coating gives any colour and a thicker barrier but can chip. For façades both work when specified correctly: anodising at 20 – 25 µm on coasts, or super-durable powder to Qualicoat Class 2 or AAMA 2604/2605.

How thick should an anodised layer be for outdoor use?

At least 15 µm in benign exterior conditions and 20 – 25 µm in coastal or industrial atmospheres under Qualanod; AAMA 611 Class I requires 18 µm or more.

Can all aluminium alloys be anodised?

Almost all wrought alloys can be anodised, but appearance varies. 6060, 6063, 5005 and 1xxx give the best decorative result; copper-rich 2xxx and high-silicon castings give darker, less protective films.

What is the difference between Type II and Type III anodising?

Type II (sulphuric) produces a 5 – 25 µm film that can be dyed and is used for decoration and corrosion protection. Type III (hard) produces a thicker, denser film for wear resistance, typically dark grey.

Does anodising affect electrical conductivity?

Yes. Anodic oxide is an electrical insulator. If a part must stay conductive for grounding, mask the contact areas or use a conversion coating instead.
