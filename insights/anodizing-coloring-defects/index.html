14 Anodizing Colouring Defects and How to Prevent Them

Most anodizing colour defects trace back to one of four things: poor electrical contact on the rack, a bath that has drifted out of its window, contaminants or precipitates in the metal itself, or careless handling before sealing. This guide groups 14 common defects by root cause, explains what each looks like and gives the practical fix, so a finisher or buyer can diagnose a rejected batch quickly.

Quick diagnosis

A one-table diagnosis of anodizing colour defects

Our view: the anodizing line usually gets blamed first, but roughly half of colour problems start earlier, in the alloy chemistry, the extrusion or rolling route, or the way parts are racked. Diagnose before you adjust the bath.

Anodising grows a porous aluminium oxide layer by making the part the anode in an acid electrolyte, usually sulphuric acid. Colour is then added by absorbing an organic dye into the pores or by depositing metal (tin, nickel or cobalt salts) at the pore bottoms by electrolytic colouring, and the pores are finally sealed. A fault at any of these stages shows up as a colour defect, and the appearance of the defect is the best clue to which stage went wrong.

Defects 1, 2, 7, 8, 11, 12, 14

Defects caused by racking and electrical contact

Anodising is a current-driven process: film thickness is roughly proportional to the charge passed through each square decimetre. Anything that disturbs the current path gives uneven film, and uneven film gives uneven colour, because thinner film absorbs less dye or holds less metal deposit.

1. Thin film, light or no colour

Cause: binding wire or clamps loosen during alkaline etching as metal is removed, so contact with the flight bar becomes intermittent. Fix: use titanium or aluminium jigs with spring tension, and re-tighten binding after etching and before anodising.

2. Partial burning and blackening

Cause: too small a contact area, dirty or oxidised jig contacts, or a short circuit between anode and cathode. Fix: strip and clean jigs regularly, enlarge the contact area and check part-to-cathode spacing.

7. Overlapping

Cause: profiles packed too tightly touch or shield each other, leaving imprints or rainbow (interference) colours where film is very thin. Fix: keep consistent spacing, replace bent jigs, and never hang distorted or bowed profiles.

8. Accumulated gas pockets

Cause: hydrogen from the cathode reaction and agitation air become trapped in corners, slots and blind holes, so no film forms there. Fix: tilt parts so gas can escape upward, and orient channels and hollows to drain.

11. Short circuit and sparking

Cause: a part swings or falls into contact with the cathode, and the local current dissolves metal. Fix: secure loads against movement, remove fallen parts from the tank and keep electrode spacing fixed.

12. Poor electrification

Cause: rectifier fault, power interruption or degraded jig joints, so the set current is never reached. Fix: maintain joints, confirm the current set point at the start of every load and log rectifier output.

14. Film burns

Cause: local current density far above the average, typically at edges near the cathode or where contact area is small. The local heat makes the film thick, white and powdery. Fix: increase contact area, move cathodes back, improve agitation and ramp the current up gradually.

Burned or short-circuited parts usually cannot be rescued by stripping and re-anodising, because metal has been lost. Treat them as scrap and fix the rack.

Defects 3, 5, 10, 13

Defects caused by electrolyte chemistry and temperature

A sulphuric acid bath dissolves its own film slowly while it grows it. Higher acid concentration, higher temperature and higher dissolved aluminium all speed up that dissolution, which softens the outer film, makes it powdery and lowers sealing quality. The window below is a common starting point for decorative Type II work; follow your process specification or chemical supplier where they differ.

ParameterTypical working rangeWhat goes wrong outside it
Free sulphuric acid150 – 200 g/LToo high: soft, powdery film, weak corrosion resistance. Too low: higher voltage, darker film
Dissolved aluminiumAbout 5 – 15 g/L; act well before 20 g/LHigh: poor conductivity, dull and powdery film; bleed off and replenish part of the bath
TemperatureAbout 18 – 22 °C for decorative filmToo warm: powdering, milky film. Too cold: higher burning risk
Current densityAbout 1.2 – 1.8 A/dm²Too high: burns, especially at edges and contacts
AgitationUniform air or pumped flowPoor: local overheating and burns
Chloride and other contaminantsAs low as practical (deionised make-up water)Pitting and etched spots during anodising

3. Dark film layer

An over-dark batch usually points to alloy composition (higher Si, Fe or Mn), a current interruption mid-cycle, low acid concentration that forces voltage up, or inadequate pretreatment. Check the mill certificate and the rectifier log before changing dye time.

5. Poor corrosion resistance

High acid and dissolved aluminium above about 20 g/L give a porous, soft film that seals poorly. Hold acid in range and replace part of the bath (often half to three-quarters) when dissolved aluminium climbs too high. Verify sealing by test, not by eye.

10. Powdering

Long electrolysis in a warm, concentrated bath, or leaving parts sitting in the acid after the current stops, dissolves the outer film into a chalky layer. Lower temperature and concentration, shorten cycle times and rinse promptly.

13. Milky white film

Film loses transparency when it is grown hot, when the hot-water rinse is too short, when extrusion conditions vary, or when Si, Fe or Mn levels fluctuate between lots. Standardise bath and rinse conditions and buy metal to a consistent chemistry.

Defects 6, 9

Defects that start in the metal, not the tank

Some colour faults are already present in the aluminium before it reaches the anodiser. Intermetallic particles and precipitates do not anodise in the same way as the aluminium matrix, so they show as discoloured specks, streaks or a tint across the whole surface.

6. Yellowing

Iron, silicon and other impurities, either in the alloy or picked up in the electrolyte, are incorporated into the film and give a yellow cast that also shifts the tone of electrolytic colours. Specify an anodising-quality alloy with controlled Fe and Si, keep the bath clean and optimise anodising conditions.

9. Black or white spots

In 6xxx extrusions, slow or interrupted cooling after the press, or local reheating where the hot profile touches a conductive surface, precipitates coarse Mg2Si (the β′ and β phases). These appear as spots after etching and anodising. Use uniform fan or water quench at the press exit and insulating run-out table materials so the profile is not reheated at contact points.

Other metal-origin defects worth knowing: streaks and banding from die lines, weld seams in hollow profiles or uneven grain structure; coarse grain that etches unevenly into a sparkling, mottled look; and dark or blotchy film on copper-bearing alloys such as 2024 and 7075, where colour matching is always harder. For appearance parts, choosing the right alloy is the cheapest defect prevention available; see our guide on aluminium for anodised appearance parts.

Defect 4

Handling defects: fingerprints and contamination

4. Fingerprints are among the easiest defects to prevent and the most annoying to find. Skin oils and salts on the open-pored film block dye uptake or interfere with sealing, so an oval print appears after colouring. Operators should wear clean, lint-free gloves from the moment parts leave pretreatment until sealing is complete, and parts should be handled by edges or jigs only.

The same logic applies to anything that touches unsealed film: dirty rinse water, drips of cleaner from overhead rails, and airborne dust in the dye area. Unsealed anodic film is effectively a sponge for about 30 to 60 minutes after anodising, and every contamination it absorbs shows in the final colour.

Quality control

How to test anodised colour and film quality

Colour defects are often accompanied by hidden problems in thickness or sealing, so a sound inspection plan checks both. These are the tests most specifications refer to:

Common tests for anodised aluminium

Film thickness
Eddy-current measurement to ISO 2360; microscopical cross-section (ISO 1463) as the referee method
Thickness classes
ISO 7599 and QUALANOD classes 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 µm; AAMA 611 Class II (min 10 µm) and Class I (min 18 µm)
Sealing quality
Dye-spot test (ISO 2143), admittance test (ISO 2931) and phosphoric-chromic acid mass loss (ISO 3210)
Colour and appearance
Visual inspection against agreed limit samples under defined light; instrumental colour difference (ΔE) for critical work
Functional coatings
MIL-PRF-8625 Type II (sulphuric) and Type III (hardcoat), Class 2 for dyed finishes
  1. Agree limit samplesBefore production, agree a light and a dark limit sample on the actual alloy and temper.
  2. Control the incoming metalBuy to anodising-quality chemistry with a mill certificate; avoid mixing heats on one façade.
  3. Fix the rackMost burns, pale patches and interference colours are solved at the jig, not in the bath.
  4. Log the bathRecord acid, dissolved aluminium, temperature and current density every shift.
  5. Test the sealRun a dye-spot or mass-loss test on each batch destined for outdoor use.

FAQ

Anodizing colour defects: frequently asked questions

Why is my anodised part lighter in some areas than others?

Uneven film thickness, almost always. Loose contacts, parts shielding each other on the rack, trapped gas in recesses and distance from the cathode all reduce local film growth, and thinner film takes up less dye. Check the racking before changing dye concentration.

What causes burning during anodizing?

Local current density far higher than the average, typically at sharp edges close to the cathode or where the contact area is too small. Poor agitation and too-cold electrolyte make it worse. Ramp current gradually, enlarge contact areas and keep cathode spacing consistent.

Can defective anodised aluminium be stripped and redone?

Colour and thickness defects can usually be stripped in a caustic or phosphoric-chromic solution and re-anodised, though each strip removes some metal and dimensions change slightly. Burned, sparked or deeply pitted parts usually cannot be recovered.

When should the sulphuric acid bath be renewed?

When dissolved aluminium approaches about 20 g/L, film quality and sealing fall. Many shops bleed off and replace part of the bath on a schedule to hold aluminium in the 5 – 15 g/L range rather than waiting for problems.

Which aluminium alloys give the most uniform anodized colour?

Anodising-quality 6063 and 6060 for profiles and 5005 or 5657 for sheet give the most even colour. 6061 is good but slightly greyer, while copper-rich alloys such as 2024 give darker, less uniform results.

Do black spots after anodising come from the anodiser or the extruder?

Usually the extruder. Spots on 6xxx profiles are commonly caused by coarse Mg2Si precipitation from slow cooling or local reheating on the run-out table. Etching and anodising only reveal them.
