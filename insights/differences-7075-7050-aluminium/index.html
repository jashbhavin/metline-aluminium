7050 vs 7075 Aluminium: 7 Differences for Aerospace and High-Strength Parts

7075 is the stronger and cheaper choice for thin sections and small machined parts; 7050 is the better alloy for thick plate and large forgings, because it keeps its strength through the thickness and combines it with higher toughness and stress-corrosion resistance. The difference comes down to one alloying change, zirconium in place of chromium, plus tighter impurity limits.

Background

Two 7xxx aerospace alloys, two different design problems

7075 was introduced in the 1940s and became the standard high-strength aircraft alloy. As airframes grew and designers began machining large integral parts from thick plate, its weaknesses showed: strength fell towards the centre of thick sections, and peak-aged material was prone to stress-corrosion cracking. 7050 was developed in the 1970s to solve exactly those problems.

Both are Al-Zn-Mg-Cu alloys that are solution treated, quenched and aged. For a 10 mm bracket the two behave similarly and 7075 is usually the cheaper buy. For a 120 mm wing-spar plate or a large bulkhead forging, they are not comparable: 7050 keeps far more of its strength and toughness at the centre of the section.

Three changes matter. Zirconium forms very fine Al3Zr dispersoids that control grain structure without making the alloy quench-sensitive, unlike the coarser chromium-bearing particles in 7075. Higher copper and zinc raise strength in the over-aged tempers that 7050 is normally used in. And the much lower iron and silicon limits reduce coarse intermetallic particles, which is the main reason 7050 has better fracture toughness and fatigue-crack resistance.

Difference 2

Quench sensitivity and strength in thick sections

After solution treatment, the alloy must be cooled fast enough to keep zinc, magnesium and copper in solution. The centre of a thick plate cools more slowly than the surface. In 7075, slow cooling lets solute precipitate on the chromium dispersoids, where it is wasted, so the centre of a thick plate ends up weaker than the surface. 7050 is far less sensitive to cooling rate, so properties are more uniform through the thickness.

This is why 7075-T651 and T7351 plate is typically used up to moderate thicknesses, while 7050-T7451 is the standard choice for the thick plate used to machine wing spars, ribs, frames and bulkheads. For any part machined from plate thicker than about 75 mm, compare the specified minimum properties at that thickness, not the headline values.

The fairer comparison is between over-aged tempers, because thick 7075 parts in service should be T73 or T7351 anyway. On that basis 7050-T7451 is roughly 7 – 8 % stronger in yield than 7075-T7351 while offering similar stress-corrosion resistance, and the advantage grows with thickness. Peak-aged 7075-T651 remains the strongest of the three in thin and moderate sections.

Difference 4

Stress corrosion, exfoliation and fracture toughness

7075-T6 is known for poor short-transverse stress-corrosion resistance, the failure mode that cracked many early forged fittings. The T73 temper cures it but costs strength. 7050 was designed to deliver high strength in an over-aged condition, so a 7050-T7451 part does not force that trade-off as sharply.

Fracture toughness and fatigue-crack growth also favour 7050, mainly because of its cleaner chemistry. That matters for damage-tolerant aircraft design, where the structure must tolerate a crack for a set number of flights before inspection. For thin sheet skins, 7075 is commonly supplied clad with a thin layer of pure aluminium for corrosion protection; 7050 is used mostly as plate, forgings and extrusions, where cladding is not applicable.

Difference 5

Machining, welding and heat treatment

Both alloys machine very well, with short chips and fine finishes. Thick 7050-T7451 plate is stretched, so residual stress is low and large integral parts stay closer to shape after heavy pocketing. Neither alloy is suitable for conventional fusion welding; both are joined mechanically, by adhesive bonding, or by friction stir welding in specialist applications.

Heat treatment of 7050 is more involved: the two-step over-ageing that produces T74 or T7451 must be controlled closely, and electrical conductivity is commonly measured alongside hardness to confirm the correct temper. For 7050 rivets, the alloy is supplied as wire and the finished rivets are driven in the aged condition, which is one reason it replaced older rivet alloys that had to be refrigerated or driven soon after quenching.

7050 normally costs more than 7075 and is less widely stocked, so it is worth specifying only where its advantages are used. A simple rule: below about 50 mm and outside sustained stress in corrosive service, 7075 is usually adequate; for thicker sections, forgings, or damage-tolerant aerospace structure, 7050 is the safer engineering choice.

FAQ

7050 vs 7075 aluminum: frequently asked questions

Is 7050 stronger than 7075?

Not in thin peak-aged sections: 7075-T651 has the higher typical strength. In thick sections, and when both are compared in SCC-resistant over-aged tempers, 7050-T7451 is stronger than 7075-T7351.

Why is 7050 used for thick aircraft plate?

Zirconium replaces chromium for grain control, which makes 7050 much less quench-sensitive. Thick plate therefore keeps its strength and toughness at the centre of the section.

Can 7050 replace 7075 directly?

Often, but not automatically. Design allowables, temper, corrosion requirements and certification all differ, so substitution should be approved by the design authority.

Can either alloy be welded?

Neither is suitable for conventional MIG or TIG welding. They are joined mechanically, bonded, or friction stir welded in specialist cases.

What does T7451 mean?

Solution heat-treated, stress-relieved by stretching, then over-aged to improve stress-corrosion and exfoliation resistance. It is the most common 7050 plate temper.
