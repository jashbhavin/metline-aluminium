Brazing Aluminium to Copper: Fillers, Flux and Technique

Aluminium can be brazed to copper, but the process window is narrow: aluminium melts at about 660 °C, and where copper dissolves into aluminium a eutectic forms that melts at only 548 °C, producing brittle intermetallic compounds. Reliable joints come from a low-melting zinc-aluminium filler with a non-corrosive caesium-based flux, fast and controlled heating, good joint design and protection against galvanic corrosion afterwards.

The challenge

Why aluminium-to-copper brazing is difficult

Our view: most failed aluminium-copper joints are overheated, not underheated. The goal is to make the filler flow and wet both metals while giving copper and aluminium as little time and temperature as possible to react with each other.

The decisive factor is metallurgical. Copper and aluminium form a eutectic at about 33 wt % copper that melts at 548 °C, well below the melting point of either metal. If molten filler dissolves copper, the liquid at the interface becomes richer in copper and starts to eat into the aluminium, a defect called erosion. On cooling, copper-aluminium intermetallic phases such as CuAl2 and Cu9Al4 form at the interface. A thin layer is unavoidable and acceptable; a thick one is brittle, raises electrical resistance and cracks under vibration or thermal cycling.

Filler choice

Choosing the filler metal

The filler must melt below 548 °C if possible, wet both metals with the help of flux, and resist corrosion in service. That is why zinc-aluminium fillers dominate in production, with aluminium-silicon fillers used only with very fast, well-controlled heating.

Flux

Flux selection for Cu-Al brazing

Aluminium's oxide must be broken down by a fluoride-based flux. The flux must be active at or slightly below the filler's melting range, otherwise the filler melts on an oxidised surface and balls up instead of flowing.

Caesium fluoroaluminate (CsAlF4-based)

Melts at a lower temperature than standard potassium fluxes, so it matches Zn-Al fillers. Residues are non-corrosive in most cases, which is why it is the usual choice for HVAC Cu-Al joints. Check that the specific product is rated for copper-aluminium joints.

Potassium fluoroaluminate (KAlF4-based)

The standard non-corrosive flux for controlled-atmosphere aluminium brazing with Al-Si fillers, melting around 565 – 572 °C. Too high in temperature to suit Zn-Al fillers.

Chloride-containing fluxes

Very active and tolerant of poor cleaning, but residues are hygroscopic and corrosive. Must be removed completely by hot-water washing; generally avoided for enclosed systems.

Flux-cored and flux-coated rods

Flux inside or around the filler gives a consistent flux-to-filler ratio, less mess and easier automation; widely used for site and production brazing of tube joints.

Method

Step-by-step: brazing aluminium to copper

  1. Design the jointUse a lap or socket joint with a small, uniform clearance as recommended by the filler supplier, and enough overlap for strength. In tube work, the copper tube is commonly inserted into an expanded aluminium socket.
  2. Clean both surfacesDegrease, then remove oxide from the aluminium with stainless brushing or abrasive just before brazing. Clean copper bright. Do not touch cleaned surfaces.
  3. Apply fluxCoat both surfaces with flux paste, or use a flux-cored or flux-coated rod. Keep flux off areas you do not want filler to flow onto.
  4. Heat the copper firstCopper conducts heat away quickly and melts much higher, so direct the flame or induction field mainly at the copper and let heat reach the aluminium by conduction. Use a soft, slightly reducing flame.
  5. Add filler when flux is activeWhen the flux turns clear and fluid, feed filler into the joint. It should be drawn into the gap by capillary action.
  6. Stop as soon as it flowsRemove heat immediately once the filler has filled the joint. Extra time and temperature grow intermetallics and risk eroding the aluminium.
  7. Clean and protectRemove flux residue if required, inspect, then seal the joint against moisture with a coating, sleeve or heat-shrink.

In production, induction heating with fixed coils and timed cycles is far more repeatable than hand-torch work, because it controls exactly how long the interface spends above the critical temperatures. That is the main reason Al-Si fillers can be used successfully in automated cells but are risky with a hand torch.

Durability

Preventing galvanic corrosion of Cu-Al joints

Copper and aluminium are far apart in the galvanic series in seawater and similar electrolytes, so a wet joint becomes a battery in which aluminium (and any zinc-rich filler) is consumed. Dry, indoor joints may last indefinitely; condensing or outdoor joints need deliberate protection.

  • Keep moisture out with sealing compounds, paint, epoxy or heat-shrink sleeves over the finished joint
  • Use non-corrosive fluxes and remove any residue that could attract moisture
  • Make the aluminium area large relative to the copper so corrosion is spread thinly
  • Where possible, locate the joint where condensate drains away rather than pooling
  • For electrical joints, consider tin-plated or bimetal transition pieces instead of a direct joint
Alternatives

Alternatives to brazing for joining copper and aluminium

Friction welding

Solid-state rotary or inertia welding produces copper-aluminium transition pieces with a very thin intermetallic layer. Widely used for refrigeration tube adapters and electrical terminals.

Explosion and roll bonding

Large-area metallurgical bonds used to make copper-aluminium clad plate, strip and copper-clad aluminium busbar.

Ultrasonic welding

Low-heat solid-state joining for battery tabs, foils and thin busbars.

Magnetic pulse and laser welding

Fast, controlled processes used in automotive and battery production, often with process limits on intermetallic thickness.

Bimetal lugs and washers

Factory-made Cu-Al terminals and bimetallic washers move the dissimilar interface into a controlled component.

Mechanical crimp and bolted joints

Simple and serviceable, with contact paste and plating to control corrosion and contact resistance.

Safety

Safety when brazing aluminium to copper

Fluoride fluxes release irritating fumes when heated, and zinc-based fillers can release zinc oxide fume, which causes metal fume fever if inhaled. Work with local fume extraction or good ventilation, wear eye protection, heat-resistant gloves and flame-retardant clothing, and follow the safety data sheet for the specific filler and flux. Keep flammables away from the torch area and allow parts to cool on a non-combustible surface.

FAQ

Brazing aluminium (aluminum) to copper: frequently asked questions

Can you braze aluminium to copper with a torch?

Yes, with a zinc-aluminium filler, a caesium-based flux and careful heat control: heat mainly the copper, add filler when the flux is active and remove heat as soon as it flows. Overheating is the most common cause of failure.

What temperature is needed to braze copper to aluminum?

With Zn-Al fillers, roughly 400 – 480 °C at the joint, below the 548 °C Al-Cu eutectic. Al-Si fillers need about 580 – 600 °C, which is why they require very short, controlled heating cycles.

Why does the aluminium melt before the filler flows?

Heat is aimed at the aluminium, the copper is drawing heat away, or copper has dissolved into the joint and formed a low-melting eutectic. Heat the copper side and use a lower-melting filler.

Is a brazed Cu-Al joint strong enough for refrigeration systems?

Yes, properly made Zn-Al brazed joints are widely used in air-conditioning and refrigeration tube connections. They must be leak tested and protected from condensate to avoid galvanic corrosion.

Do brazed Cu-Al joints conduct electricity well?

Reasonably, but intermetallics and voids add resistance. For high-current busbar and terminal joints, friction-welded or clad bimetal parts give lower and more stable resistance.

Does Metline supply copper parts for these joints?

No. Metline supplies the aluminium side: Zn-Al and Al-Si brazing fillers, aluminium tube and sheet, copper-clad aluminium and Cu-Al bimetal products. The copper components come from your own supply chain.
