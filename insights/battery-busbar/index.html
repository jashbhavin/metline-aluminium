Battery Busbar Design for EV, Storage and Charging Systems

A battery busbar has three jobs: carry current with a small, predictable temperature rise, survive vibration and thermal cycling for the life of the pack, and join reliably to cells and terminals in high-volume production. Aluminium grades such as 1060, 1070, 1350 and 6101 now handle most of these jobs at about half the weight of an equivalent copper bar, with copper-clad aluminium used where contact resistance or space is critical. Here is how we would approach the design.

Why busbars

Why battery packs use busbars instead of cables

Our position: in a modern pack the busbar is a structural, thermal and electrical component at once, and treating it as "just a conductor" is the most common cause of late design changes.

Battery packs for electric vehicles, stationary energy storage and fast chargers concentrate hundreds of amperes in tight spaces. A rigid or semi-rigid busbar gives a fixed current path, a repeatable geometry that robots can weld or bolt, a large surface for heat rejection and a clean layout that is easy to insulate and inspect. Cables are still used where routing has to flex or cross between enclosures, but inside modules and between them, busbars have largely replaced wiring.

Because so many identical busbars go into each pack, small design choices multiply. A 0.5 mm reduction in thickness, a change from bolted to welded joints, or a switch from copper to aluminium can change pack weight by kilograms and cost noticeably, while also changing temperature rise, short-circuit withstand and assembly yield.

Prismatic cells usually present aluminium terminals for welding (the negative terminal is often an aluminium-copper composite precisely so that an aluminium busbar can be laser welded to both poles). Pouch cells use aluminium positive tabs and copper or nickel-plated copper negative tabs, which is where bimetal or nickel-plated connectors become necessary. Cylindrical cell packs commonly use thin aluminium or nickel-plated strip, or ultrasonic wire bonding with aluminium wire, to connect cells to a collector plate.

Material choice

Aluminium, copper-clad aluminium or copper?

Copper has the highest conductivity of the practical conductors, but aluminium wins on conductivity per kilogram and per unit cost. For the same resistance an aluminium bar needs about 1.6 times the cross-section of copper, yet weighs only about half as much because aluminium is roughly one-third as dense.

Pure aluminium (1060, 1070, 1350)

Highest conductivity among aluminium grades and very formable. The default for welded cell interconnects and flat distribution bars where mechanical load is low.

3003

Lower conductivity (roughly 40 – 50 % IACS depending on temper) but stiffer and stronger; used for some interconnect plates that also act as mechanical retainers.

6101 (T6, T61, T63, T64)

Heat-treatable electrical alloy with useful strength; over-aged tempers raise conductivity towards about 59 % IACS. Good for long self-supporting bars and extruded shapes.

Copper-clad aluminium (CCA)

Aluminium core with a metallurgically bonded copper skin. Offers a copper contact surface and better conductivity than pure aluminium at much lower weight than copper. Useful at main terminals and in tight junction boxes.

Sizing

Sizing a battery busbar: temperature rise first

There is no universal "amps per square millimetre" for battery busbars. The cross-section is set by the allowable temperature rise at the duty cycle (continuous, peak and fast-charge current), the cooling available, the joint resistance and the short-circuit current the bar must survive until the fuse or contactor opens. A simple resistance calculation is still the right first step.

Worked example: 200 mm cell-to-module bar, 20 × 3 mm (60 mm²)

1060 aluminium (ρ ≈ 2.8 µΩ·cm)
R ≈ 2.8×10⁻⁸ × 0.2 / 60×10⁻⁶ ≈ 93 µΩ
Loss at 300 A continuous
P = I²R ≈ 300² × 93×10⁻⁶ ≈ 8.4 W
Copper bar of the same size (reference)
R ≈ 57 µΩ, P ≈ 5.2 W, but about 3.3 times the mass
Aluminium bar sized for equal loss
About 20 × 5 mm (100 mm²): R ≈ 56 µΩ, still about half the mass of the copper bar

Joint resistance often adds as much loss as the bar itself, so specify and test the joints, not only the bar.

  • Define continuous, peak and fast-charge currents and their durations, not just a single rating
  • Set a temperature-rise limit that respects the cell, insulation and adjacent plastics
  • Check short-circuit heating: aluminium must stay well below softening temperature during the fault clearing time
  • Allow for thermal expansion: aluminium expands about 40 % more than copper per degree, so long bars need bends, slots or flexible sections
  • Model the joints: contact or weld resistance multiplied by current squared is localised heat

Joining

Joining aluminium busbars to cells and terminals

Laser welding

The dominant method for prismatic-cell interconnects. Aluminium reflects strongly and its oxide melts far above the metal, so beam wobble, green or blue lasers and clean, flat surfaces improve consistency. Control fit-up gap tightly.

Ultrasonic welding

Solid-state joining for thin aluminium busbars to tabs, and for stacked foils in flexible busbars. Avoids melting and brittle intermetallics when joining aluminium to copper.

Wire bonding

Ultrasonic bonding of aluminium wire between cylindrical cells and a collector busbar; each wire can act as a fuse link.

Bolted joints

Used at pack terminals and service points. Plate contact areas (tin or nickel), use Belleville washers to keep clamping force as aluminium creeps, and torque to a defined value.

Bimetal transitions

Where aluminium meets copper, use friction-welded, roll-bonded or clad bimetal pieces so the dissimilar interface is made once in a controlled way, not at every bolted joint.

Brazing

Rare inside packs because of heat and flux residue, but used for some cooling-plate assemblies; see our article on brazing aluminium to copper.

Insulation

Insulation, creepage and safety standards

High-voltage packs typically run at 400 V or 800 V, so busbars are insulated wherever they are not welded or bolted. Common methods are epoxy powder coating, PET or polyimide film lamination, extruded or heat-shrink sleeves, and overmoulded plastics. Each must keep its dielectric strength after thermal cycling, humidity and vibration.

Design for creepage and clearance distances appropriate to the working voltage and pollution degree, and verify the whole assembly against the vehicle or system standards the project follows, typically including ISO 6469-3 for electric-vehicle electrical safety, UL 2580 for EV batteries, IEC 62619 for industrial lithium batteries and UN 38.3 for transport testing.

Common battery busbar failure modes

  • Loosening bolted joints after creep and thermal cycling, raising contact resistance and heat
  • Cracked laser welds from poor fit-up or vibration fatigue at rigid connections
  • Insulation damage at sharp edges or bend radii that were not deburred
  • Galvanic corrosion where bare aluminium and copper meet in humid environments

FAQ

Battery busbars: frequently asked questions

Is aluminium good enough for EV battery busbars?

Yes. Aluminium needs about 1.6 times the cross-section of copper for the same resistance but weighs about half as much, and it laser welds directly to the aluminium terminals of most prismatic cells. It is now the default for many module interconnects and distribution bars.

Which aluminium grade is best for battery busbars?

1060, 1070 or 1350 for maximum conductivity and formability in welded interconnects; 6101 in T6 or an over-aged temper where the bar must be self-supporting; 3003 where extra stiffness matters more than conductivity.

When should copper-clad aluminium be used?

Where you need a copper contact surface or higher conductivity in limited space, such as main terminals and compact junction boxes, but want to save weight and cost compared with solid copper.

How do you join an aluminium busbar to a copper tab?

Avoid fusion welding the two directly, which forms brittle intermetallics. Use ultrasonic welding, a nickel-plated interface, or a friction-welded or clad bimetal transition piece, and protect the joint from moisture.

Do aluminium busbars need plating?

Welded joints are usually made to bare, clean aluminium. Bolted contact areas are normally tin- or nickel-plated to keep contact resistance low and stable over time.

Can Metline supply aluminum busbars cut and formed to drawing?

Yes. We supply aluminium and copper-clad aluminium strip and bar and can cut, punch, bend and plate to your drawing, with mill certificates showing chemistry and conductivity.
