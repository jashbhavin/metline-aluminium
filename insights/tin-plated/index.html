Tin-Plated Conductors in Power Systems: Why and How Aluminium Is Tinned

Tin plating does not make a conductor carry more current; it makes the contact surface stay predictable. On aluminium, a thin tin layer replaces the hard, insulating natural oxide with a soft, easily broken one, protects bolted and clamped joints from humidity and pollution, and reduces galvanic attack where aluminium meets copper. The catch is that aluminium must be pretreated properly or the coating will not stay on.

The problem tin solves

Why bare aluminium joints need help

A bolted electrical joint only conducts through the tiny points where the two surfaces actually touch. Anything that grows or changes at those points changes the joint resistance, and rising resistance means heat, which accelerates the process until the joint fails.

Aluminium forms an oxide film within seconds of exposure to air. The film is hard, adherent and electrically insulating. A well-made bare aluminium joint (wire-brushed, greased with contact compound and bolted promptly with the right hardware) works, but its performance depends heavily on workmanship and on the environment in which it lives.

Tin changes the chemistry at the interface. Tin oxide is thin and soft, so modest contact pressure breaks through it to clean metal. Tin is also ductile, so the contact spots flow and enlarge under load, and it sits between aluminium and copper in the galvanic series, which reduces the driving force for corrosion when aluminium busbar is bolted to copper terminals.

What tin plating achieves on aluminium

  • Lower and more stable contact resistance over the life of the joint
  • Protection of contact faces in humid, salty or sulphur-bearing atmospheres
  • Reduced galvanic corrosion in aluminium-to-copper connections
  • Less dependence on site surface preparation at installation
  • Solderability where joints are soldered rather than bolted
Process

How aluminium is tin plated

Tin will not plate directly onto aluminium: the oxide re-forms instantly and the aluminium would displace tin from solution unevenly. A multi-stage sequence is required, broadly following the preparation route described in ASTM B253.

  1. CleaningNon-etching alkaline cleaning removes oils from rolling, extrusion or machining.
  2. Etching and desmuttingA mild etch removes the original oxide and surface layer; an acid desmut removes the alloying-element residue it leaves.
  3. Zincate immersionThe part is dipped in an alkaline zincate solution, which removes oxide and deposits a thin zinc film. A double zincate (strip and repeat) gives a finer, more adherent film and is preferred for critical work.
  4. Strike layerA thin copper or nickel strike is plated over the zinc to form a stable base and a diffusion barrier. Nickel underlayers also help suppress tin whiskers.
  5. Tin platingTin is electrodeposited from an acid (sulphate or methanesulphonate) or alkaline bath to the specified thickness, in matte or bright finish.
  6. Rinse, dry and inspectThickness, adhesion and appearance are checked against the coating specification.

Each step depends on the one before it. Most adhesion failures on tinned aluminium (blistering after heating, peeling at bends) trace back to poor zincating or to an alloy whose surface was not desmutted properly, not to the tin itself.

For aluminium busbar and strip, electrolytic tin over a zincate and strike layer is the normal route. Coatings are commonly specified to ASTM B545 (electrodeposited tin), which groups coatings into six service classes by minimum thickness, or to MIL-T-10727 for defence work. Matte tin is usually preferred for electrical contacts because it has lower internal stress and lower whisker tendency than bright tin.

Specifying

What to put on a tin-plated aluminium specification

Key specification items

Substrate
Alloy and temper, e.g. 1350-H111, 1060-O, 6101-T6 or 6063-T6 busbar
Coating standard
ASTM B545 service class, MIL-T-10727, or a customer specification
Thickness
Minimum local thickness on significant surfaces; contact faces of busbar are often specified in the region of 5 – 15 µm
Underlayer
Zincate plus copper or nickel strike; nickel where whisker mitigation or high temperature matters
Finish
Matte, bright or flow-brightened
Coverage
Full plating, contact areas only, or ends only
Tests
Thickness (XRF or coulometric), adhesion (bend, heat-quench), appearance, solderability where relevant

Thickness values here are common practice, not a standard requirement; confirm against your design code or customer specification.

Temperature limit

Switchgear standards such as IEC 62271-1 permit a higher maximum temperature for tin-coated bolted joints in air than for bare aluminium or copper joints (105 °C against 90 °C), one of the main reasons designers specify tinning.

Creep and bolting

Tin does not stop aluminium creeping under bolt load. Use correctly torqued bolts with spring (Belleville) washers or wide flat washers, and avoid clamping over thin tin on soft tempers.

Forming after plating

Bend before plating where possible. If plated material must be bent, use soft tempers and generous radii, and specify an adhesion test that includes bending.

Our view: tin is the sensible default for aluminium busbar in switchgear, busway and battery systems, because it buys a large reliability margin at modest cost. Silver is justified only for sliding or high-temperature contacts, and bare aluminium suits dry indoor installations where joints are assembled by trained crews.

Applications

Where tin-plated aluminium conductors are used

  • Switchgear and panel boards: main and distribution busbars, especially where aluminium bars meet copper breaker terminals
  • Busway (bus duct): joint areas of aluminium conductors in sandwich busway
  • EV and battery energy storage: cell and module busbars, where low, stable resistance controls heating
  • Transformers and rectifiers: terminal pads and connection flags
  • Rail, marine and offshore: humid and salt-laden atmospheres where bare joints degrade
  • Flexible connections: laminated aluminium foil links with tinned contact ends

FAQ

Tin-plated aluminium (aluminum) conductors: FAQ

Does tin plating improve the conductivity of aluminium busbar?

No. Tin is a poorer conductor than aluminium. It improves the joint, not the bar, by keeping contact resistance low and stable over time.

Why is aluminium harder to tin plate than copper?

Aluminium forms an insulating oxide instantly and is more reactive than tin. It needs cleaning, etching, a zincate immersion and a copper or nickel strike before tin can be plated with good adhesion.

How thick should tin plating be on busbar?

It depends on the specification and environment. Contact faces are commonly specified in the region of 5 – 15 µm; ASTM B545 defines service classes by minimum thickness. Thicker coatings suit harsher environments.

Can tin-plated aluminium be bolted to copper?

Yes, and it is one of the main reasons for tinning. Tin reduces galvanic attack and gives a stable interface. Use correct bolting hardware with spring washers and contact compound where specified.

What are tin whiskers and do they matter for busbar?

They are thin tin filaments that can grow from stressed coatings. In power busbar they are rarely critical, but matte tin and nickel underlayers reduce the risk, which matters near fine-pitch electronics.

Does Metline supply tinned copper?

No. Metline supplies aluminium only. We supply aluminium busbar, strip and flexible links, bare or tin plated to your specification; copper is discussed here only as a comparison.
