How Aluminium Alloy Wheels Are Made, from Ingot to Packing

An aluminium alloy wheel starts as an A356 casting alloy ingot or a 6061 forging billet and ends as a precision-machined, coated and tested safety part. Between those points sit melting and degassing, casting or forging, T6 heat treatment, CNC machining, X-ray and leak testing, multi-layer finishing and protective packing. This article walks through each stage, explains why it is done, and shows where quality is won or lost.

Before we start

Wheel terminology and typical specifications

A wheel is defined by a handful of dimensions that must match the vehicle exactly. Getting any of them wrong means the wheel will not fit, will foul the brakes or suspension, or will alter the steering geometry.

TermMeaningTypical passenger car range
DiameterBead-seat diameter of the rim, in inches14 – 22 in (larger on SUVs and performance cars)
Width (J)Rim width between bead seats, in inches5.5 – 11 in

Commercial vehicle wheels (for example 22.5 × 8.25 in, 10-stud, 335 mm PCD in the ISO pattern) follow their own standards and are mostly forged. The process described below applies to both, with the differences noted.

Raw material

Which aluminium alloys are used, and why

RouteAlloyKey compositionWhy it is chosen
Cast wheelsA356.2 (EN AC-42100 is a close European equivalent)Al with about 6.5 – 7.5 % Si and 0.25 – 0.45 % Mg, low FeExcellent fluidity for thin spokes; heat-treatable to T6; good ductility when iron is kept low
Forged wheels6061Al with about 0.8 – 1.2 % Mg, 0.4 – 0.8 % Si, 0.15 – 0.4 % CuForgeable, heat-treatable to high strength, tough and corrosion-resistant
Some forged commercial wheels6082 or proprietary 6xxx variantsAl-Mg-Si with MnSimilar to 6061 with higher strength in some tempers

In casting, the melt is usually modified with strontium to turn the silicon eutectic from coarse plates into fine fibres, and grain-refined with titanium-boron master alloy. Both steps improve elongation and fatigue resistance. In forging, the starting stock is a homogenised 6061 billet or cast-and-extruded bar cut to precise weight, because forging accuracy depends on consistent slug mass.

Iron is the enemy of ductility in A356. It forms brittle needle-shaped intermetallics, so wheel foundries specify A356.2 with iron below about 0.12 % and control recycled content carefully.

Stage 1

Melting, degassing and melt cleanliness

Ingots and clean in-house returns are melted in gas-fired or electric furnaces, then the melt is treated before casting. This stage decides how much porosity and how many inclusions the wheels will contain, and porosity is the main cause of leaks and fatigue failure in cast wheels.

  1. Melt and alloyCharge ingot and returns, melt, and adjust chemistry to the A356.2 window; check with an optical emission spectrometer.
  2. DegasA rotary impeller injects fine bubbles of argon or nitrogen to strip dissolved hydrogen. Density-index or reduced-pressure tests verify the result.
  3. Modify and refineAdd strontium for eutectic modification and Ti-B for grain refinement.
  4. Clean and filterSkim dross; filter through ceramic foam on the way to the casting machine to catch oxide films.
Stage 2a

Casting methods: gravity, low pressure and flow forming

Gravity die casting

Metal is poured into a steel die and fills by gravity. Risers feed shrinkage, so more metal is used per wheel. Simple and flexible, it is still used for smaller volumes and some designs, often with robotic ladling.

Low-pressure die casting

The dominant method for car wheels. A sealed furnace beneath the die is pressurised with dry air, typically below about 1 bar, pushing metal up a riser tube into the die. Filling is calm and controlled, solidification proceeds from rim to hub, and the riser feeds the hub, giving dense castings and high metal yield.

Counter-pressure and squeeze variants

Pressure is applied on both the furnace and die side, or the metal is squeezed during solidification, reducing porosity further for high-performance wheels.

Flow forming (rim rolling)

A cast blank with a thick, short rim is heated and spun on a mandrel while rollers stretch the rim to its final width. The rim becomes thinner, its grain is refined and its strength approaches forged levels, saving weight at moderate cost.

Die design is decisive. Cooling channels in the die control the order in which sections solidify so that the hub, which solidifies last, is fed by the riser. Die coatings control heat flow and surface finish. A poorly balanced die leaves shrinkage porosity in the spoke-to-rim junction, which is exactly where the highest fatigue stresses occur.

Stage 2b

The forging route

Forged wheels follow a different path. A 6061 billet is preheated to forging temperature and pressed in several stages, typically upsetting, blocking and finishing, with press loads of many thousands of tonnes. The rim is then formed by spinning or flow forming, and the centre may be further shaped by machining.

  • Forging closes porosity and aligns grain flow with the wheel contour, giving high fatigue strength
  • A forged wheel of the same strength can be significantly lighter than a cast one
  • Two-piece and three-piece forged wheels bolt or weld a forged centre to spun rim halves for custom offsets
  • Commercial truck and bus wheels are almost all forged for weight and durability

Because forging relies on the quality of the starting stock, wheel forgers specify homogenised billet with controlled grain size, clean surface and ultrasonic inspection. Metline supplies aluminium forging stock and 6061 forgings to such specifications.

Stage 3

T6 heat treatment

Both cast A356 and forged 6061 wheels are heat-treated to T6. The wheels are solution-treated (for A356, typically around 535 – 545 °C for several hours) so the magnesium and silicon go into solid solution, quenched rapidly in water, and then artificially aged at a lower temperature, commonly in the region of 150 – 180 °C, to precipitate fine Mg2Si strengthening particles.

StepPurposeWhat can go wrong
Solution treatmentDissolve Mg and Si; spheroidise the eutectic silicon in castingsToo hot: incipient melting. Too short: low strength
QuenchFreeze the solid solutionToo slow: low strength. Too severe: distortion and residual stress
Artificial ageingPrecipitate strengthening particlesUnder- or over-ageing gives the wrong balance of strength and elongation

Hardness and, on sample wheels, tensile tests cut from spokes and rim confirm the result. Heat treatment is also why a cracked wheel should not be welded: local heat destroys the T6 condition around the weld.

Stage 4

CNC machining and drilling

After heat treatment, wheels are machined on CNC lathes and machining centres. The first operation turns the inboard side and rim profile; the second turns the face and bead seat; a third drills the bolt holes, valve hole and centre bore. Typical requirements include radial and lateral run-out within a few tenths of a millimetre and tight control of PCD and centre-bore diameter.

Precision

Run-out and balance determine vibration at speed. Wheels are checked on gauges, and many lines measure every wheel automatically.

Diamond cutting

Bi-colour wheels have their painted face machined with a single-point diamond tool to expose bright metal, then lacquered.

Deburring and cleaning

Edges are deburred and wheels washed to remove coolant and chips before finishing.

Replacement wheels sold in many markets must also meet approval schemes such as UNECE Regulation 124 in Europe or JWL/VIA in Japan, which require type testing and production conformity checks.

Stage 6

Pretreatment, coating and decorative finishes

  1. PretreatmentAlkaline cleaning, etching and a conversion coating (chromium-free systems are now normal) to give the paint something to grip and stop filiform corrosion.
  2. PrimerPowder primer, sometimes electrophoretic (e-coat) primer, applied and cured.
  3. ColourLiquid or powder colour coat, metallic silver, black, gunmetal or custom.
  4. Clear coatAcrylic powder or liquid clear coat for gloss and chemical resistance.
  5. CureEach layer baked at controlled temperature and time; over-baking can affect T6 properties, so cure cycles are kept within limits.

Alternatives include polishing, chrome or PVD plating, and anodising for some forged wheels. Each finish has trade-offs between appearance, durability in road salt and ease of repair.

Stage 7

Packing for shipment

Finished wheels are easily scratched, so packing matters as much as any machining step. Common practice is to fit a plastic face protector, wrap each wheel, and place it in an individual carton or stack wheels in layers separated by honeycomb board and corrugated dividers on a pallet. Export packing adds moisture protection and secure strapping so that loads cannot shift in containers. Labels carry size, PCD, offset, finish and batch number for traceability.

Outlook

Where wheel manufacturing is heading

  • Lightweighting for EVs: more flow-formed and forged wheels, and aerodynamic inserts to extend range
  • Low-carbon aluminium: carmakers ask for recycled content and lower-carbon primary metal, with documented footprints
  • Larger diameters: 20 – 22 in wheels are common on SUVs, raising the demand for strength and stiffness
  • Sensors: tyre-pressure monitoring is standard; more integrated sensing is under development
  • Process efficiency: shorter heat-treatment cycles, automated X-ray and inline measurement reduce cost and scrap

FAQ

How aluminium (aluminum) alloy wheels are made: FAQs

What aluminium alloy are car wheels made from?

Most cast car wheels are A356 (typically the low-iron A356.2 version), heat-treated to T6. Forged wheels are mostly 6061-T6.

What is the difference between cast, flow-formed and forged wheels?

Cast wheels are poured into a die and are the most economical. Flow-formed wheels are cast, then the rim is spun and stretched to make it thinner and stronger. Forged wheels are pressed from a solid billet, giving the highest strength-to-weight ratio at the highest cost.

Why are aluminium wheels X-rayed?

Casting can leave internal porosity or shrinkage that cannot be seen from outside. X-ray inspection finds these defects in high-stress areas such as the spoke-to-rim junction before the wheel is machined and painted.

Why can a cracked alloy wheel not just be welded?

Welding heat destroys the T6 heat treatment around the weld, reducing strength and fatigue life in exactly the area that failed. Reputable repairers will not weld structural cracks in road wheels.

Does Metline make wheels?

No. Metline supplies aluminium materials, such as 6061 forging stock, bar, sheet and extrusions, to manufacturers. Wheel production itself is carried out by specialist wheel makers.
