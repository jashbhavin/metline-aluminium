5052 vs 6061 Aluminium: 10 Differences That Decide Which One You Buy

5052 is a work-hardened aluminium-magnesium alloy that bends easily and shrugs off seawater; 6061 is a heat-treated aluminium-magnesium-silicon alloy that is about 40 % stronger and far better to machine. If the part is formed from sheet or lives near salt water, start with 5052-H32. If it is machined, carries structural load or is extruded, start with 6061-T6. The ten points below explain why, with the numbers behind each one.

The short version

5052 vs 6061 at a glance

Both alloys are everyday engineering aluminium, both are widely stocked, and both are often quoted for the same enclosure, bracket or tank. They are not interchangeable, though. They get their strength from completely different mechanisms, and that one fact explains most of the differences in forming, welding, machining and cost.

Difference 1

Chemical composition: magnesium alone vs magnesium plus silicon

5052 contains roughly 2.5 % magnesium in solid solution with a little chromium. Magnesium atoms distort the aluminium lattice and make dislocations harder to move, which is why the alloy is stronger than 1xxx and 3xxx grades even when annealed. There is nothing in it that can form precipitates, so heat treatment cannot strengthen it.

6061 has less magnesium (about 1 %) but adds 0.4 – 0.8 % silicon and a little copper. Magnesium and silicon combine as Mg2Si, which dissolves at about 530 °C and re-precipitates as very fine particles during ageing. Those particles are what give T6 its strength.

The low copper content of 5052 matters for corrosion: copper is the element most likely to create local galvanic cells inside an aluminium alloy, and 5052 allows only a trace of it.

Difference 2

Tempers: H codes for 5052, T codes for 6061

Because the strengthening routes differ, the temper systems differ too. Ordering "5052-T6" or "6061-H32" is a sign the specification needs correcting.

5052-O

Fully annealed, softest condition. Used for deep drawing, spinning and severe forming.

5052-H32

Strain hardened and stabilised to quarter-hard. The most common sheet temper: strong enough for panels and enclosures, still easy to bend.

5052-H34 / H36 / H38

Half, three-quarter and full hard. Stronger and stiffer to the touch, with progressively larger minimum bend radii.

6061-O

Annealed. Formable, but rarely used in service; parts are formed, then heat-treated.

6061-T4

Solution treated and naturally aged. Moderate strength and better formability than T6.

6061-T6 / T651 / T6511

Solution treated and artificially aged to peak strength. The "51" suffixes mean stretched for stress relief (plate and extrusions).

The "stabilised" part of 5052-H32 is easy to overlook. Al-Mg alloys can soften slowly at room temperature after cold work (age softening). The H3x tempers receive a low-temperature thermal treatment that completes this change at the mill, so the properties stay put in service. For an introduction to the full designation system see our guide to aluminium tempers.

The headline: 6061-T6 has about 43 % more yield strength than 5052-H32 and even beats fully hard 5052-H38. If the part is sized by yield, a 6061 version can be thinner and lighter.

There are two places where 5052 wins on strength. Its fatigue strength (about 117 MPa at 5×10⁸ cycles for H32, against 96.5 MPa for 6061-T6) is higher, which suits vibrating panels, fan blades and vehicle body parts. And annealed 5052-O is stronger than annealed 6061-O, so for parts that must be formed in the soft condition and then used without heat treatment, 5052 is the stronger result.

5052 is under 1 % lighter, which is negligible for most designs; a 1 m² sheet 3 mm thick weighs 8.04 kg in 5052 and 8.10 kg in 6061. Stiffness is effectively the same, so switching alloy will not cure a panel that deflects too much; only thickness or geometry will.

Conductivity is a real difference. 6061 conducts heat about 20 % better, which is why it is preferred for heat spreaders, electronics chassis and cold plates where the part is also machined.

Difference 5

Bending and forming

This is the most common reason to choose 5052. In H32 temper it bends to an inside radius of about one sheet thickness or less without cracking, takes press-brake work and roll forming well, and can be deep drawn in the O temper. Sheet-metal enclosures, chassis, ducts, tanks, boat hulls and trailer panels are built from it for that reason.

6061-T6 has much less ductility. Sharp bends crack on the outside, especially along the rolling direction. Typical guidance is an inside radius of 1.5 – 3 times thickness depending on gauge, bending across the grain. When 6061 has to be formed hard, the usual route is to form in O or freshly solution-treated (W/T4) condition and then age to T6, which adds a heat-treatment step and some distortion risk.

A practical rule

  • Laser-cut and bent sheet metal part: 5052-H32
  • Machined block, plate or bar part: 6061-T651 or T6
  • Extruded profile: 6061 (or 6063/6082); 5052 is seldom extruded
Difference 6

Corrosion resistance and marine use

5052 is one of the most corrosion-resistant aluminium alloys available as sheet. With very little copper and magnesium kept in solid solution at a level that does not sensitise, it resists seawater, salt spray and many industrial atmospheres without coating. Fuel tanks, boat hulls and fittings, marine enclosures and coastal cladding use it routinely.

6061 is still a good corrosion performer, much better than 2xxx or 7xxx alloys, but its copper content and Mg2Si precipitates make it less tolerant of chloride exposure. Above the waterline it is widely used; for permanent immersion, anodise or paint it, or choose a 5xxx grade. Neither alloy is prone to stress-corrosion cracking in normal tempers.

For heavier marine plate, where weldability and strength both matter, 5083 and 5086 outperform 5052. See our marine aluminium plate guide.

Difference 7

Welding and joining

Both alloys weld well by MIG and TIG, but the outcome differs. In 5052 the weld and heat-affected zone return to roughly the annealed strength, which for 5052 is still a respectable 193 MPa tensile, so welded joints keep a large share of the parent strength. ER5356 filler is the usual choice and gives a sound, ductile joint.

In 6061-T6 the heat of welding dissolves or coarsens the strengthening precipitates. Without post-weld heat treatment, the HAZ falls towards T4-level strength, and design codes use a welded tensile strength around 165 MPa. 6061 is also more crack-sensitive if welded without filler; use ER4043 or ER5356 and never fuse it autogenously.

For a welded tank, hull or enclosure, the parent-metal strength advantage of 6061 largely disappears at the weld line. That is why many welded structures that look like "6061 jobs" are in fact built from 5052, 5083 or 5086. More detail is in our 6061 welding guide.

Difference 8

Machinability and finishing

Soft, ductile alloys smear on cutting tools, and 5052 is in that group: long stringy chips, built-up edge and a duller surface unless tooling is very sharp and highly polished. It can be machined, but cycle times and finish suffer. 6061-T6 breaks chips cleanly and produces a bright finish at high spindle speeds, which is why it dominates CNC work.

For anodising, both give a good protective film. 6061 gives a slightly more uniform clear and dyed finish on machined faces; 5052 anodises clean and bright on sheet, though a faint yellow tint can appear with heavier films. Both powder-coat and paint well with proper pretreatment.

In practice 5052 is a flat-rolled alloy: most of it is sold as sheet, coil and light plate. 6061 is the generalist, found in almost every wrought form, and is the obvious choice when one alloy must cover a plate base, an extruded frame and a machined bar fitting in the same assembly.

Difference 10

Cost, availability and how to decide

Thin 5052 sheet is usually a little cheaper per kilogram than 6061 sheet of the same gauge, because it skips solution heat treatment and ageing. In thick plate the position can reverse, since 6061-T651 is produced in very high volumes. The real cost difference is in fabrication: forming 6061-T6 may need a heat-treat step, and machining 5052 takes longer. Price the finished part, not the kilogram.

Common specification mistakes

  • Specifying 6061-T6 for a bent sheet-metal enclosure, then fighting cracked bends
  • Choosing 6061 for strength and then welding it, losing most of the advantage at the joint
  • Calling up 5052 for a heavily machined part, where 6061 would cut faster and finish better
  • Assuming 5052 can be heat-treated to higher strength; only cold work raises it

FAQ

5052 vs 6061 aluminum: frequently asked questions

Is 5052 or 6061 aluminum stronger?

6061-T6 is stronger: about 310 MPa tensile and 276 MPa yield, against about 228 MPa and 193 MPa for 5052-H32. 5052 has the higher fatigue strength, and after welding the gap narrows considerably.

Can 5052 be heat-treated to T6?

No. 5052 has no precipitation-hardening system, so it can only be strengthened by cold work (H tempers). Heating it simply anneals it back towards the O temper.

Which is better for bending, 5052 or 6061?

5052-H32. It takes bends of about one thickness inside radius or tighter. 6061-T6 needs roughly 1.5 – 3 times thickness and tends to crack on tight bends, especially parallel to the rolling direction.

Which alloy is better for marine use?

5052 resists seawater and salt spray better because it contains almost no copper. For heavier welded marine structures, 5083 or 5086 plate is usually specified.

Can I substitute 6061 for 5052 in a sheet-metal design?

Only after checking bend radii and corrosion exposure. 6061-T6 may crack in bends designed for 5052, and it is less tolerant of chlorides. The reverse substitution reduces strength by about 30 – 40 %.

Which is easier to machine?

6061-T6. It forms short chips and a bright finish. 5052 is gummy, builds up on tools and gives a poorer surface unless tools are very sharp and well lubricated.
