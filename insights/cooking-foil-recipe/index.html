Foil Packet Recipes: How to Cook in Aluminium Foil Bags and Parcels

A foil packet is a small sealed oven: food steams in its own juices, flavours stay concentrated and there is almost nothing to wash up. Use heavy-duty foil or a ready-made cooking foil bag, seal with tight double folds, leave space for steam and keep very acidic or salty ingredients away from bare foil. Below are 12 tested combinations with times, followed by the material facts behind good foil cooking.

The method

Why cook in foil packets?

Cooking "en papillote" (in a parcel) is centuries old; foil simply made it practical for grills and campfires. The Metline Technical Team works with foil as a material every day, so this article explains both the cooking and the metal.

Aluminium conducts heat about four times better than stainless steel and reflects radiant heat strongly, so a sealed packet heats evenly on all sides. Moisture released by the food cannot escape, so the inside behaves like a steamer at close to 100 °C while the outer surfaces in contact with the foil can brown slightly. That combination suits fish, chicken, prawns and vegetables that dry out under direct heat.

The method also scales well. You can prepare individual packets in advance, keep them chilled and cook them together, each with different seasoning. On a campsite or barbecue there are no pans to scrub. The only real skills are cutting ingredients to similar cooking times and sealing the packet properly.

Foil packet rules of thumb

  • Use heavy-duty foil (about 18 – 25 µm) or a double layer of standard foil for grills and campfires
  • Cut ingredients so they cook in the same time; par-boil dense potatoes and root vegetables
  • Add a little fat or liquid so the packet steams rather than scorches
  • Seal with at least two tight folds, but leave headroom for steam to circulate
  • Keep lemon, tomato, vinegar and heavy salting away from bare foil; use a parchment liner or add them after cooking
Technique

How to fold and seal a foil packet

  1. Cut generouslyTear a sheet about 30 × 45 cm per portion (larger for whole fish). Too small a sheet forces a tight wrap with no steam space.
  2. Grease or lineBrush the centre with oil, or lay a sheet of baking parchment on the foil if the recipe contains acidic ingredients.
  3. Build a flat layerPlace food in the centre in one layer: firm vegetables on the bottom, protein on top, sauce last.
  4. Make the tentBring the long edges together above the food and fold them down twice in 1 cm folds, leaving an air gap above the food.
  5. Close the endsFold each short end in twice and press firmly. The packet should be sealed but loose, like a pillow.
  6. Cook and restCook on a tray in the oven or directly on the grill grate, seam side up. Rest one minute, then open away from your face; the steam is hot.

Ready-made cooking foil bags shortcut the folding. They are usually formed from heavy foil with a crimped or heat-sealed seam and an open end that is folded shut after filling. The same rules apply: do not overfill, keep the seam upwards on the grill, and open carefully.

Recipes

12 foil packet recipes for oven, grill and campfire

All times assume an oven at about 200 – 220 °C (390 – 430 °F) or a covered grill over medium-high heat, with one portion per packet. Grills and campfires vary widely, so check one packet before serving: chicken should reach 74 °C (165 °F) in the centre and fish should flake easily.

Chicken fajita packets

Strips of chicken thigh, sliced peppers and onion, oil, cumin, smoked paprika, garlic and salt. Seal flat and cook about 20 – 25 minutes. Add lime juice at the table, not in the packet.

Garlic butter prawns and corn

Peeled prawns, short lengths of corn on the cob, par-boiled baby potatoes, butter, garlic and a Cajun-style spice mix. Potatoes must be par-cooked; prawns need only about 12 – 15 minutes.

Maple-mustard salmon

Salmon fillet on a bed of sliced courgette, brushed with maple syrup, wholegrain mustard and black pepper. About 15 minutes, then open the top and grill 2 minutes to glaze.

Paneer tikka parcels

Paneer cubes, onion and capsicum in a thick yoghurt-spice marinade. Line the foil with parchment because the yoghurt is acidic. About 15 – 18 minutes on a hot grill.

Herbed baby potatoes

Halved baby potatoes, olive oil, rosemary, garlic and coarse salt (sprinkled at serving). Raw potatoes need 30 – 40 minutes; cut them small and keep the layer single.

Sausage, pepper and onion packets

Sliced cooked sausages, peppers, red onion and Italian herbs with a little oil. Because the sausage is already cooked, 15 – 20 minutes is enough to soften the vegetables.

Garlic mushrooms

Whole button mushrooms, butter, garlic, thyme and a splash of stock. About 15 minutes; serve on toast with the juices from the packet.

Whole fish with herbs

A small cleaned fish stuffed with herbs and sliced ginger, brushed with oil. Lay it on parchment if you add lemon slices. About 20 – 25 minutes depending on thickness.

Mixed vegetable medley

Carrot batons, broccoli, cauliflower and green beans with oil, garlic and chilli flakes. Cut dense vegetables thinner so everything finishes together, about 20 minutes.

Campfire banana boats

Split bananas in their skins and fill with chocolate chips and a few marshmallows. Wrap loosely and heat 5 – 8 minutes over embers.

Berry crumble pouches

Mixed berries with sugar under an oat, butter and flour crumble. Berries are acidic, so line the foil with parchment. About 15 – 20 minutes.

Cheesy garlic bread

A split loaf spread with garlic butter and grated cheese, wrapped loosely so the steam does not soften the crust. About 10 – 15 minutes, turning once.

Season salty components such as soy sauce, brine-cured meat or heavy salt sparingly inside the packet, or add them at the table. This keeps flavour balanced and limits contact between salt and bare foil.

Packets on a campfire cook faster on the side facing the embers. Place them on a grate or on a bed of embers raked thin, not in flames, and turn once halfway. Flames can burn through standard foil; heavy-duty foil or a double wrap prevents leaks.

The material

What foil to use: alloys, thickness and bags

Food foil is not pure aluminium. Household and cooking foil is commonly rolled from 8011 or 8079 (aluminium with small additions of iron and silicon) or from 1235 (99.35 % aluminium). The iron-bearing 8xxx alloys give better strength and puncture resistance at the same thickness, which is why heavy-duty and cooking-bag foil is usually 8011 or 8079. Foil for cooking is supplied in the fully annealed O temper so it is soft, folds tightly and does not spring back open.

Thickness ranges are typical of the market rather than a standard; check the pack or specification. EN 546 covers foil dimensions and tolerances, and EN 602 lists the alloys permitted for food contact.

Is there a "right side"? No. The shiny and matt faces come from the final rolling pass, where two foils are rolled together and the faces touching each other come out matt. The difference in heat reflection is negligible for cooking, so either side can face the food unless a coated or non-stick foil says otherwise.

Health facts

Is cooking with aluminium foil safe?

Aluminium foil is approved for food contact in Europe, the United States, India and most other markets, and normal use is considered safe. The nuance concerns acids and salt. Bare aluminium is protected by a thin natural oxide film that is stable at near-neutral pH. Strongly acidic foods (lemon juice, tomatoes, vinegar, fruit) and concentrated salt can attack that film and increase the amount of aluminium that migrates into the food.

The European Food Safety Authority set a tolerable weekly intake for aluminium of 1 mg per kg of body weight from all sources, most of which comes from food naturally. Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) has long advised that foil, trays and uncoated aluminium utensils should not be used for acidic or salty foods, and its tests found much higher release from acidic purées in uncoated trays. The practical answer is simple and costs nothing:

  • Line the foil with baking parchment when a recipe contains citrus, tomato, vinegar, yoghurt or fruit
  • Add lemon wedges, salsa and salty sauces after cooking rather than inside the packet
  • Do not store acidic leftovers in bare foil for days; use a glass or plastic container
  • Discard foil that has been pitted or eaten through by food
  • Coated or lacquered foil products are designed to reduce direct metal contact

Never use foil in a microwave unless the oven manufacturer allows it, and do not line the floor of an oven with foil, where it can trap heat and damage the enamel. For air fryers, see our separate note on using aluminium foil in an air fryer.

FAQ

Cooking with aluminium (aluminum) foil packets: FAQ

What temperature should I cook foil packets at?

An oven at 200 – 220 °C (390 – 430 °F) or a covered grill at medium-high heat suits most packets. Lower temperatures work but add time; very high direct heat tends to scorch the bottom before the top is cooked.

Should the shiny side of aluminium foil face in or out?

It makes no practical difference. The matt side is simply the face that touched another foil in the last rolling pass. Only non-stick or coated foils have a specific side, which is marked on the pack.

Can I put lemon or tomato in a foil packet?

Yes, but put a sheet of baking parchment between the food and the foil, or add the lemon or tomato after cooking. Acids attack bare aluminium and increase the amount that migrates into food.

Is heavy-duty aluminum foil necessary?

For grills and campfires it is strongly recommended because it resists tearing when packets are turned and does not burn through as easily. In an oven on a tray, a double layer of standard foil works.

How do I know a foil packet is cooked without opening it?

You cannot be certain, so open one packet carefully to check. Chicken should reach 74 °C (165 °F) in the centre and fish should flake. Reseal and continue cooking if needed.

Can foil packets be prepared in advance?

Yes. Assemble them, keep them refrigerated for up to a day and cook straight from the fridge, adding a few minutes. Leave acidic dressings out until serving.
