Wire Bonding vs Ribbon Bonding: Choosing Interconnects for Power Modules

Round wire and flat ribbon are both ultrasonically wedge-bonded interconnects; the difference is the cross-section, and that shape changes current capacity, inductance, loop height, bond count and reliability. Ribbon usually wins on high-current paths in compact IGBT, SiC and battery packages, while round wire remains the better choice for fine pitch, complex routing and lines already optimised for heavy wire. This article sets out the trade-offs and how to qualify a change.

The short answer

Wire or ribbon: the decision in brief

If a power path needs several thick wires in parallel, a single ribbon can usually replace them with fewer bonds, a lower loop and lower inductance. If the job is signal routing at fine pitch, in many directions, or on a line whose heavy-wire process is already qualified and cheap, round wire remains the sensible choice.

Key points

  • Wire and ribbon differ in shape (round versus flat), not in bonding principle; ribbon is always wedge bonded
  • One ribbon can replace several heavy round wires, cutting the number of bond interfaces
  • A flat conductor has more surface for its area, lower self-inductance and a lower loop profile
  • Ribbon bonds need more ultrasonic energy and bond force, so pads, die attach and the die itself must tolerate it
  • Round wire routes in any direction; ribbon must be bonded with its flat face down and bends easily only one way

Scope: this article covers die-to-package bonding wire and ribbon. It does not cover photovoltaic tabbing ribbon, earthing straps or braided grounding conductors, which share the word “ribbon” but are different products.

Background

What wire bonding does in a semiconductor package

Wire bonding makes the electrical connection between the pads on a semiconductor die and the leadframe, substrate or busbar of its package. It remains the most widely used interconnect method because it is flexible, mature and inexpensive per connection. Two families exist.

Ball bonding melts the tip of a fine wire into a ball with an electric spark, presses it onto the pad with heat and ultrasound, then forms a loop and finishes with a stitch bond on the lead. It is fast, works in any direction and dominates integrated-circuit and LED packaging using gold, copper, palladium-coated copper or silver wire, mostly at diameters of roughly 15 – 50 µm.

Wedge bonding presses the wire sideways under a wedge-shaped tool and joins it with ultrasonic energy, usually at room temperature for aluminium. Fine aluminium wire (around 18 – 75 µm) is used in hybrids, sensors and chip-on-board work; heavy wire of about 100 – 500 µm carries the large currents of power modules. Ribbon is the flat cousin of heavy wire and uses the same wedge principle with dedicated tools.

Our separate article on ball vs wedge bonding compares the two processes in depth, and the bonding wire materials comparison explains why aluminium dominates the wedge-bonded power segment.

Geometry

Round wire vs flat ribbon: why the cross-section changes everything

For the same cross-sectional area, and therefore the same DC resistance per unit length, a flat ribbon has a much larger perimeter than a round wire. That one geometric fact drives most of ribbon’s advantages.

Surface area and cooling

A 1000 × 100 µm ribbon has about 2.3 times the perimeter of a 380 µm wire of similar area, so it sheds more heat by convection and radiation and runs cooler at the same current.

Self-inductance

Inductance falls as a conductor gets wider and flatter. In fast-switching SiC and GaN modules, lower loop inductance reduces voltage overshoot and ringing at turn-off.

High-frequency current

At high frequencies current crowds into the outer skin of a conductor. A flat ribbon offers more skin for its area, which is why gold ribbon has long been used in microwave circuits.

Loop height

Ribbon loops are typically flatter, freeing headroom inside low-profile packages and reducing the volume of gel or mould compound needed.

Bond footprint

A ribbon wedge bond covers a wide rectangle, spreading current over more of the die metallisation and reducing local hot spots.

Directional stiffness

Ribbon bends easily about its thin axis but resists bending sideways, so loops must run roughly in line with the bond head; this is the main routing limitation.

Process

How the bonding process ties to the material choice

Ribbon bonding is a wedge process, so it inherits wedge bonding’s constraints and adds a few of its own. Before considering ribbon, check whether the process side of your line can support it.

  1. Bonder and bond headHeavy-wire bonders need a ribbon-capable bond head, wedge, guide and cutter. Many current machines handle both, but tooling and the ribbon feed are specific to the ribbon size.
  2. Ultrasonic energy and forceA wide ribbon needs more ultrasonic power and bond force than a single heavy wire. That energy passes into the die, so thin dies, fragile passivation or weak die attach can crack or delaminate.
  3. Top-side metallisationAluminium ribbon bonds well to the standard aluminium top metal of IGBT and diode chips. A thicker metal layer, or a copper or nickel-based top-side system, gives more process margin and longer cycling life.
  4. Die attachSoft solder, sintered silver and silver-filled epoxy react differently to ultrasonic load. Sintered silver is stiff and robust; some soft solders and adhesives absorb energy and reduce bond quality.
  5. Loop and cutRibbon is cut in front of or behind the wedge; loop shape and cut quality must be stable to avoid heel damage and stray tails.

Material is linked to the process too. Aluminium ribbon and heavy wire bond to aluminium pads at room temperature with no dissimilar-metal interface. Copper ribbon carries more current and resists fatigue better, but it is harder: it needs much higher bond energy and usually a copper-based or thickened top-side metallisation to avoid damaging the die. Aluminium-clad copper conductors aim to combine copper’s conductivity with aluminium’s gentle bonding.

Advantages

Where ribbon wins: current, heat, inductance and reliability

Ribbon’s benefit in high-current work is not simply “more metal”. Several mechanisms add together.

  • Fewer bond interfaces. Every bond foot is a potential failure site. Replacing three or four heavy wires with one ribbon removes most of those interfaces and the stitch bonds that go with them.
  • Larger continuous contact. A wide wedge bond spreads current across the pad rather than concentrating it under several small feet, lowering current density and local heating.
  • Better heat dissipation. The greater surface area and the wider bond help pull heat out of the die top side, which matters in modules that run continuously at high current.
  • Lower parasitic inductance. Important in fast-switching wide-bandgap devices and in RF paths.
  • Lower profile. Flatter loops suit compact discrete packages such as TO-type and surface-mount power packages, where loop height is constrained.
  • Shorter cycle time. One bond pair instead of several can shorten bonding time per device, provided the ribbon process runs at comparable speed.

The reliability case is supported by published research. Laboratory power-cycling and temperature-cycling studies comparing aluminium ribbon with aluminium heavy wire of similar current capacity have generally found longer lifetimes for ribbon, with reported gains in the region of 1.5 to 2 times depending on the test and package. The usual failure mechanism in both is fatigue cracking near the bond interface or heel driven by thermal expansion mismatch between aluminium and silicon or silicon carbide. Ribbon spreads that strain over a larger, flatter joint.

There is a caveat: in modules with very effective cooling, where the conductors run cool anyway, the electrical and thermal differences shrink, and the main benefit becomes temperature-cycling life and bond count rather than current capacity.

Sizing

One ribbon, how many wires? The equivalence logic

The first-pass sizing method is to match cross-sectional area, because that sets DC resistance and, roughly, heating. The table shows how many round aluminium wires of each diameter have the same area as common ribbon sizes.

Ribbon size (width × thickness)Area (mm²)≈ Ø 200 µm wires≈ Ø 300 µm wires≈ Ø 380 µm wires≈ Ø 500 µm wires
1000 × 100 µm0.103.21.40.90.5
1500 × 150 µm0.2257.23.22.01.1
1500 × 200 µm0.309.54.22.61.5
2000 × 200 µm0.4012.75.73.52.0
2000 × 300 µm0.6019.18.55.33.1

Calculated purely from cross-sectional geometry. A published simulation of IGBT modules, for example, found that a 2000 × 200 µm ribbon could replace three 400 µm wires with a similar temperature distribution.

Area matching is only the starting point. Because ribbon cools better, it may carry slightly more current than the equivalent bundle of wires at the same temperature rise; because the parallel wires are spaced apart, they may share heat less evenly than the table implies. Final sizing should account for:

  • Maximum steady and transient current, including short-circuit and surge events
  • Conductor temperature limit set by the surrounding gel or mould compound
  • Pad dimensions: the ribbon width plus wedge footprint must fit the available metallisation
  • Bond process window for the chosen thickness on your die and die attach
  • Thermal and power-cycling targets from the application specification
Economics

Does ribbon bonding actually lower cost?

Switching to ribbon can save money, but not automatically. The answer depends on four cost elements weighed together.

Material

Aluminium is by far the cheapest bonding metal; aluminium ribbon and heavy wire of the same cross-section cost a similar amount per metre of conductor. Material is rarely the deciding factor between wire and ribbon in aluminium.

Bond count and throughput

Fewer bonds per device can raise units per hour, but only if the ribbon process does not need much longer bond times or slower loop motions.

Reliability and yield

Fewer interfaces and longer cycling life can reduce field returns, warranty cost and the need for over-design, which often outweighs direct process cost in automotive work.

One-off investment

Ribbon bond heads, wedges, cutters and feeders, new recipes and a full qualification programme. On a mature, high-yield heavy-wire line with modest volumes, this can cancel the savings.

A useful way to frame it: ribbon pays back fastest on new module designs, where pads and layouts can be drawn for ribbon from the start and the qualification cost has to be spent anyway.

Selection

How to choose, and where round wire still wins

Ribbon is not better at everything. Round wire keeps clear advantages in several situations:

  • Fine pitch and small pads: round wire fits where a ribbon foot cannot.
  • Complex routing: wire loops can turn in any direction; ribbon must run broadly straight from its first bond.
  • Mature, qualified lines: a stable heavy-wire process with good yields may not justify requalification.
  • Sensitive dies: thin or fragile chips may not tolerate ribbon bonding energy.
  • Designed-in fusing: where the wire is meant to act as a fuse, as in many battery modules.

Both interconnects share the same family of failure modes, which any change must address: heel cracking from repeated flexing, bond lift-off from fatigue at the interface, reconstruction of the aluminium top metal under thermal cycling, and cratering or cracking of the die under bond energy.

Testing

Qualifying a switch to ribbon: tests and checklist

Bond strength is verified by two main destructive tests. The pull test hooks the loop and pulls until it breaks, recording force and failure location (heel, span, bond lift); MIL-STD-883 Method 2011 is the classic reference. The shear test pushes a tool against the bond foot to measure the strength of the welded interface. Ribbon needs wider hooks and shear tools and its own acceptance limits; do not reuse round-wire limits.

These tests show bond quality, but they complement rather than replace incoming checks of the ribbon itself (breaking load, elongation, dimensions, surface condition) and long-term reliability testing. For automotive power modules, power cycling and thermal shock to programmes such as ECPE AQG 324 are the usual benchmark.

  • Pad size and layout accommodate the ribbon width plus wedge footprint, with clearance to neighbouring structures
  • The bonder has a ribbon-capable bond head, matching wedge, guide and cutter
  • Die attach (solder, sintered silver or adhesive) withstands the higher ultrasonic energy
  • Loop height and clearance fit inside the package and encapsulation
  • Reliability plan includes temperature cycling and power cycling with pull and shear sampling before and after
  • Ribbon dimensions, temper and spool format match your feeding system and are available from more than one source

FAQ

Wire bonding vs ribbon bonding: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between wire bonding and ribbon bonding?

Both join a die to its package with ultrasonically bonded conductors. Wire bonding uses round wire, which can be ball or wedge bonded; ribbon bonding uses a flat rectangular conductor and is always wedge bonded. Ribbon offers more current per bond, lower inductance and a lower loop; wire offers finer pitch and free routing.

How many wire bonds can one ribbon replace?

Match cross-sectional area first: a 2000 × 200 µm ribbon has the area of about 3.5 wires of 380 µm or two of 500 µm. Then confirm by thermal analysis and testing, because ribbon cools better and pad space, process window and cycling targets also affect the result.

Is ribbon bonding more reliable than heavy aluminum wire bonding?

Usually, for comparable current capacity. Published power and temperature cycling studies have reported longer lifetimes for aluminium ribbon, partly because there are fewer bond interfaces and the strain is spread over a wider joint. The gain depends on die metallisation, die attach and cooling, so it must be confirmed for each design.

What is the difference between ball bonding and wedge bonding?

Ball bonding melts the wire tip into a ball and bonds it with heat and ultrasound; it is fast and routes in any direction, typically with gold or copper fine wire. Wedge bonding presses the wire sideways under a wedge tool with ultrasonic energy, usually at room temperature for aluminium, and is used for heavy wire and all ribbon.

What is the difference between wire bonding and flip chip?

Wire bonding connects the face-up die to the package with loops of wire or ribbon. Flip chip turns the die face down and connects it through solder bumps or copper pillars across its whole area, giving shorter paths and more connections but requiring a bumped die and a matching substrate.

Is bonding ribbon the same as PV ribbon?

No. Semiconductor bonding ribbon is a precision conductor, typically high-purity aluminium or copper, ultrasonically bonded inside a package. Photovoltaic ribbon is coated copper strip soldered between solar cells, and grounding straps or braids are different products again.
