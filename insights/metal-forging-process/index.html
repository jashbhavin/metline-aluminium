26 Forging and Forming Terms, Explained for Aluminium Buyers

Forging drawings, purchase specifications and supplier quotes use a compact vocabulary: hot or cold forging, open or closed die, grain flow, forging ratio, blanking, flanging. This glossary defines 26 of the most common terms in plain language and adds what each one means in practice when the metal is aluminium, so you can read a quote correctly and specify the right route for your part.

Context

Why forging vocabulary matters when you buy aluminium

Two quotes for the "same" forged bracket can differ in price by a factor of two because one assumes open-die forging and machining, and the other assumes a closed-die forging close to final shape. Knowing the terms lets you compare like with like.

The terms below fall into four groups: how hot the metal is when it is shaped, what kind of tooling shapes it, what happens inside the metal, and the related sheet-forming (stamping) operations that are often listed under the wider heading of "forging and pressing". Each definition is followed by the aluminium-specific point that matters most.

Aluminium forges very differently from steel. Its working window is narrow (roughly 60 – 80 °C wide for most alloys), dies must be preheated so the workpiece does not chill, and strong 7xxx alloys need higher forging pressures than carbon steel even though they are only a third as dense. Those facts come up again and again in the definitions.

Group 1 · Temperature

Hot, warm, cold and isothermal forging

#TermDefinitionWhat it means for aluminium
1ForgingShaping metal in the solid state by compressive force from a hammer, press or rolling tool, so that it flows into a new shape.Produces a wrought structure with no casting porosity; used for wheels, suspension arms, aircraft fittings, rings and flanges.
2Hot forgingForging above the metal's recrystallisation temperature, so the grains reform as it deforms and the metal does not work-harden.Most aluminium forgings are made hot, typically between about 380 °C and 480 °C depending on alloy.
3Warm forgingForging above room temperature but below the recrystallisation temperature.Used for some precision aluminium parts to reduce forming load while keeping tighter tolerances than hot forging.
4Isothermal forgingForging with the dies held at the same temperature as the workpiece, so the metal does not cool against the tooling.Allows thin ribs and webs in aerospace parts; slow and costly, reserved for complex, high-value forgings.
5Cold forgingForging at room temperature, including cold extrusion, cold heading and cold pressing.Common for 1xxx, 3xxx and 6xxx slugs (impact-extruded cans, fasteners, connectors); gives excellent finish and tolerance.

Typical hot-forging windows quoted in the ASM Handbook are approximately 430 – 480 °C for 6061, 420 – 460 °C for 2014, 420 – 450 °C for 2024, 405 – 460 °C for 5083 and 380 – 440 °C for 7075. The forging house's qualified procedure always takes precedence over handbook ranges.

Going above the window risks incipient melting at grain boundaries; going below it causes cracking and coarse recrystallised grains after heat treatment. Both are why aluminium forging needs close temperature control of billet and dies.

Group 2 · Tooling

Open die, closed die and other forging methods

#TermDefinitionWhat it means for aluminium
6Die forgingAny forging in which shaped dies deform a billet into a component.The general term for impression-die work; tooling cost is justified by volume.
7Open-die forgingForging between flat or simple dies that do not enclose the metal; the gap closes progressively and the part is shaped by repeated blows and manipulation.Used for hand forgings, blocks, discs, shafts and large rings in small quantities; final shape comes from machining.
8Closed-die (impression-die) forgingForging between dies that contain cavities matching the part shape; excess metal escapes as flash around the parting line.The standard route for volume aluminium parts such as wheels, control arms and fittings; flash is trimmed afterwards.
9Multi-directional forgingForging in which punches act from several directions in one cycle, often in a split die.Makes hollow or branched parts (valve bodies, manifolds) with less machining and continuous grain flow.
10Precision forgingForging to tolerances tight enough that little or no machining is needed (net or near-net shape).Aluminium is well suited because it forges at low temperature with little scale; ribs can be very thin.
11Closed-impression (flashless) forgingForging in a fully enclosed die with no flash gutter, so billet volume must be controlled precisely.Saves material and trimming; requires accurately cut and weighed billets.
12High-speed (high-energy-rate) forgingForging in which a ram is driven at very high velocity, for example by compressed gas, to shape the part in a single blow.Can fill fine detail, but die life and process control are demanding; less common than press forging.
13Composite forgingA route that combines hot, warm and cold forging steps on one part.For example, hot pre-forming followed by cold sizing to bring a 6xxx part to final tolerance.

Dies for aluminium are preheated, usually to a few hundred degrees Celsius; for hydraulic-press work they can run close to the billet temperature. Press forging is generally preferred over hammer forging for aluminium because the slower strain rate suits the alloys and gives better control of grain structure.

Group 3 · Metallurgy

Grain flow and forging ratio

#TermDefinitionWhat it means for aluminium
14Forging grain flowThe directional fibre structure created when grains and inclusions are elongated and follow the contour of the part during forging.Grain flow that follows the load path improves fatigue strength and resistance to stress-corrosion cracking, especially in 7xxx and 2xxx parts.
15Forging ratioA measure of the amount of deformation, normally the ratio of cross-sectional areas (or lengths or heights) before and after forging.Enough reduction is needed to break up the cast ingot structure; many specifications set a minimum ratio for this reason.

Grain flow is the main reason designers choose a forging over a machined plate. A part machined from plate cuts straight through the rolled grain, exposing end grain at fillets and holes; the same part forged to shape keeps the fibres wrapped around those features. Specifications for aerospace forgings often require a macro-etched section to show acceptable grain flow on first-article parts.

Directionality also matters for properties. Forgings are tested in longitudinal, long-transverse and short-transverse directions, and short-transverse properties are usually the lowest. When you order a forging, state which direction the main load runs so the supplier can orient the billet and test pieces correctly.

Group 4 · Sheet forming

Blanking, bending, deep drawing and other pressing terms

#TermDefinitionWhat it means for aluminium
16BlankingShearing a flat shape out of sheet; the piece cut out is the part (or a blank for further forming).Circles for spinning and deep drawing are blanked from 1xxx, 3xxx and 5xxx sheet.
17PunchingShearing holes along a closed contour; the removed slug is scrap and the sheet is the part.Punch-die clearance is set as a percentage of thickness per side and tuned to temper; too much leaves burrs, too little causes galling and fast tool wear.
18Cutting (parting)Fully or partly separating a blank, internally or along an edge.Includes notching, slitting and lancing; burr height is a common inspection point.
19Deformation (plastic forming)Permanently changing shape without separating material.The umbrella term for bending, drawing, flanging and bulging.
20BendingForming sheet, profiles or tube to a set angle or curvature by a bending moment.Minimum bend radius depends on alloy and temper; O and H32 bend tightly, T6 needs generous radii.
21Deep drawingPulling a flat blank into a die with a punch to make a cup or box whose depth can exceed its diameter.Uses high-formability 1050, 3003, 5052 or 8011 in O or H1x/H2x tempers; earing is controlled by texture.
22FlangingForming an upright edge along a straight or curved line at the border of a part or around a hole.Stretch flanges on aluminium split earlier than on steel; allow larger radii.
23BulgingExpanding a sheet or hollow blank by internal pressure or tooling so it stretches into a new shape.Hydroforming of aluminium tube for automotive frames is a form of bulging.
24Expanding and reducingEnlarging or reducing the diameter at the end of a tube or hollow part with a die.Used to join heat-exchanger tube to headers and to make tube fittings.
25TwistingRotating part of a stamped or extruded component about its axis by torque to set an angle.Applied to fan blades, brackets and decorative profiles.
26EmbossingPressing raised and recessed patterns into the surface by local thinning.Stucco-embossed and diamond tread sheet are produced this way, often by embossing rolls.
Practical advice

Which route suits your aluminium part?

Under about 500 pieces, large or simple

Open-die forging or a forged block or disc, then machining. No dedicated tooling, and the grain is still refined compared with cast stock.

Thousands of pieces, 3D shape

Closed-die forging. Tooling cost is spread over the run and machining is limited to datum faces and holes.

Small, symmetrical, very high volume

Cold forging or impact extrusion from slugs, which gives the best finish and tolerance at low unit cost.

Thin-walled sheet parts

Blanking, drawing and flanging from coil or sheet; choose an O or H2x temper with good elongation.

Rings and flanges

Seamless ring rolling from a pierced pre-form; far less waste than cutting rings from plate.

Critical fatigue parts

Specify grain-flow requirements and short-transverse properties on the drawing, and ask for first-article macro-etch evidence.

Common specification mistakes

  • Writing "forged" on a drawing without a standard (for example ASTM B247 or EN 586) or temper
  • Ignoring short-transverse properties in thick 7xxx forgings
  • Asking for tight tolerances on as-forged surfaces that will be machined anyway
  • Specifying a temper for a sheet-formed part that cannot achieve the bend radius on the drawing

FAQ

Forging and forming terms: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between open-die and closed-die forging?

Open-die forging shapes metal between flat or simple dies that do not enclose it, so the final shape comes mostly from machining. Closed-die forging uses cavities that match the part, giving near-final shape and better repeatability, but needs dedicated tooling that pays off only at volume.

At what temperature is aluminium forged?

Most aluminium alloys are hot forged between about 380 °C and 480 °C, depending on the grade; for example roughly 430 – 480 °C for 6061 and 380 – 440 °C for 7075. Dies are preheated so the workpiece does not chill.

Is forged aluminium stronger than cast aluminium?

Generally yes. Forging closes porosity and gives a fine, directional grain structure, so forgings have higher ductility and fatigue strength than castings of similar chemistry. Wrought forging alloys such as 6061, 2014 and 7075 also reach higher strengths than most casting alloys.

What does forging ratio mean on a specification?

It is the reduction in cross-section (or height) from the starting billet to the forged shape. A minimum ratio ensures the cast ingot structure is fully broken down so the forging has uniform, wrought properties.

Why are stamping terms included in a forging glossary?

Forging and sheet pressing are both plastic-forming processes and are often grouped together in standards and factory terminology. Blanking, bending and deep drawing apply to sheet, while forging applies to bulk metal.

Can Metline supply forged aluminium?

Yes. Metline supplies aluminium forgings, forged blocks, discs, rings and forging stock in common grades such as 6061, 6082, 2014 and 7075, with mill test certificates, to customers worldwide.
