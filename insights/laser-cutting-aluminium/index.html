Laser Cutting Aluminium: A Practical Guide for Sheet, Plate, Tube and Profiles

Modern fibre lasers cut aluminium quickly and accurately, from thin foil-like sheet to plate of 20 mm and more, with a narrow kerf and little distortion. Good results depend on matching laser power to thickness, cutting with high-pressure nitrogen, choosing an alloy that cuts cleanly and designing parts with laser-friendly features. This guide explains each of those choices and when waterjet, plasma or machining is the better option.

The physics

Why aluminium is harder to laser cut than steel

Aluminium reflects most infrared light, conducts heat about four times faster than carbon steel and melts at only about 660 °C, but its oxide skin melts above 2,000 °C. Those properties pull in different directions: the beam must deliver energy fast enough to melt a narrow channel before the heat spreads away, and the molten metal must be blown out before it re-solidifies as dross on the bottom edge.

Older CO2 lasers, at a wavelength of 10.6 µm, struggled because aluminium absorbs very little of that light and reflected beams could damage optics. Fibre lasers at about 1.07 µm are absorbed several times better, and modern sources include back-reflection protection, which is why fibre has become the standard technology for cutting aluminium. Higher available power, now commonly 6 – 20 kW and above on production machines, has steadily extended the thickness range.

What laser cutting does well on aluminium

  • Fast, programmable cutting of complex 2D shapes with no tooling cost
  • Narrow kerf (roughly 0.1 – 0.4 mm depending on thickness) and tight nesting for good yield
  • Small heat-affected zone compared with plasma
  • Bright, oxide-free edges with nitrogen assist that can be welded or anodised directly

For almost every aluminium sheet, plate or tube job the realistic choice is a fibre laser. The machine questions that matter are source power, cutting head quality, nitrogen supply capacity, bed size and, for tubes, the number of axes for bevel cutting.

Capability

How thick can a fibre laser cut aluminium?

Maximum thickness depends on power, beam quality, gas pressure and the edge quality you will accept. The ranges below are indicative for clean production cutting with nitrogen; machines can pierce and separate thicker material, but edges become rougher, tapered and drossy.

Laser powerClean production cutting (approx.)Typical use
1 – 2 kWup to about 3 – 4 mmEnclosures, panels, signage, thin brackets
3 – 4 kWup to about 6 – 8 mmGeneral sheet-metal work
6 kWup to about 10 – 12 mmMachine parts, battery and EV components
10 – 12 kWup to about 16 – 20 mmStructural plate, marine and rail parts
20 kW and above25 mm and beyond, with reduced edge qualityThick plate where waterjet is too slow

Published figures vary widely between machine builders. Ask for test cuts in your alloy and thickness, and agree the required edge quality before ordering.

Above about 20 – 25 mm, waterjet or CNC machining usually gives better edges and avoids any heat-affected zone, and for very thick blocks a saw cut followed by milling is often cheapest.

Process settings

Assist gas: why nitrogen is standard for aluminium

GasEffect on aluminiumWhen to use
Nitrogen (high pressure, roughly 10 – 25 bar)Inert; shears molten metal out of the kerf, leaving a bright, oxide-free edgeDefault for almost all aluminium cutting, especially parts to be welded, anodised or painted
Compressed airMostly nitrogen with some oxygen; cheaper but leaves a slightly oxidised edgeThin sheet where edge colour and weldability are less critical
OxygenReacts with the melt, producing a rough, heavily oxidised edge and more drossRarely recommended for aluminium
Nitrogen-oxygen mixesSome machine builders offer blends to improve speed or reduce drossSpecial cases; validate on your alloy

Gas pressure rises with thickness. Too little pressure leaves dross hanging from the bottom edge; too much can make the cut unstable. Nozzle condition, standoff distance and focus position are equally important, and they drift as nozzles wear, so consistent quality needs regular nozzle checks.

For fatigue-critical aerospace and structural parts in 2xxx and 7xxx alloys, many specifications prohibit thermal cutting on final edges or require a machining allowance to remove the heat-affected layer. Check the drawing and specification before choosing laser over waterjet or milling.

Quality

Two defects account for most rejects: dross, a bead of re-solidified metal on the bottom edge caused by low gas pressure, wrong focus or worn nozzles; and burn marks or rounded corners where the beam slows down at sharp corners. Good programs use corner loops, power ramping and lead-ins placed in scrap areas.

Design for laser

Design rules for laser-cut aluminium parts

  • Hole size: keep hole diameters at least equal to material thickness; smaller holes are possible on thin sheet but lose roundness and may need drilling on thick plate
  • Webs and slots: keep bridges between cuts at least about one material thickness wide to avoid heat distortion and burn-through
  • Inside corners: allow small radii rather than perfectly sharp corners where possible
  • Bending after cutting: keep holes and slots at least about two to three thicknesses from bend lines, and orient bends across the rolling direction in 5xxx and 6xxx sheet
  • Tabs and micro-joints: allow small tabs to keep parts in the sheet during cutting; specify whether tab marks must be removed
  • Protective film: use laser-grade PVC film on decorative or brushed surfaces so the film does not lift or burn at the edge
  • Finishing sequence: laser cut before anodising, powder coating or painting, so the finish covers the cut edge
  • Tolerances: do not specify machining tolerances on laser edges; add machining operations for precision fits
3D cutting

Laser cutting aluminium tube and profiles

Tube lasers rotate the tube while the head cuts, producing holes, slots, notches, mitres and bevelled ends in one set-up. They replace sawing, drilling and milling for frames, railings, furniture, solar structures, bicycle and vehicle parts. Round, square and rectangular tube, angles, channels and many extruded profiles can be processed, provided the section can be clamped and its geometry is programmed accurately.

Key points for tube work are the wall thickness range of the machine, the maximum section size and length, spatter on the inside wall opposite the cut, and tolerances on self-locating tab-and-slot joints that simplify welding. For extrusions, provide the exact profile drawing, because extrusion tolerances (to EN 755-9 or ANSI H35.2) affect where the laser lands.

Commercial

What drives the cost of laser-cut aluminium parts

  1. MaterialAlloy, temper, thickness and sheet size; nesting efficiency decides how much of the sheet you pay for as scrap.
  2. Cutting timeTotal cut length, number of pierces and thickness. Many small holes in thick plate are slow and expensive.
  3. GasHigh-pressure nitrogen is a real cost on thick aluminium; air cutting is cheaper on thin sheet if the edge is acceptable.
  4. Secondary operationsDeburring, tapping, countersinking, bending, inserting hardware and finishing add cost and handling.
  5. Quantity and inspectionSet-up and programming are spread over the batch; first-article reports and certificates add documentation time.

The cheapest part is usually the one designed around the process: standard sheet thicknesses, generous hole sizes, shared cut lines between nested parts and tolerances that a laser edge can hold without machining. Sending a clean DXF with closed contours, the alloy and temper, and a note of which edges are functional saves a round of questions and avoids paying for precision where it is not needed.

Common mistakes on laser-cut aluminium orders

  • Specifying an alloy by name only, without temper, so bending or strength behaviour differs from the prototype
  • Expecting machining tolerances on laser-cut edges of thick plate
  • Ordering 7075 or 2024 laser-cut parts without checking whether the specification allows thermal cutting
  • Forgetting protective film on decorative or brushed sheet
  • Cutting after anodising, leaving bare edges

FAQ

Laser cutting aluminium (aluminum): frequently asked questions

Can you laser cut aluminum?

Yes. Fibre lasers cut aluminium routinely. The material reflects and conducts heat strongly, so it needs adequate power, high-pressure nitrogen and good focus control, but results on sheet and medium plate are fast and precise.

What is the maximum aluminium thickness for laser cutting?

It depends on power and accepted edge quality. As a guide, 3 kW lasers cut about 6 – 8 mm cleanly, 6 kW about 10 – 12 mm and 12 kW about 16 – 20 mm. Higher-power machines go further, with rougher edges.

Which aluminium alloy is best for laser cutting?

5052 and 5083 give some of the cleanest edges, and 6061 and 6082 cut well. Pure 1xxx grades are harder because of their reflectivity and fluid melt; 2024 and 7075 cut but can develop micro-cracks at the edge.

Do laser-cut aluminium edges need deburring?

With correct settings, edges on thin sheet are usually clean enough for assembly. Thick plate often has a small bottom-edge burr or dross, and parts for handling or coating are commonly deburred or tumbled.

Can laser-cut aluminium be welded and anodised?

Yes, when cut with nitrogen. The edge is bright and oxide-free, so it welds and anodises well. Cut before anodising so the coating covers the edge.

Is laser or waterjet better for aluminium?

Laser is faster and more precise on sheet and plate up to roughly 20 mm. Waterjet is better for thick plate, heat-sensitive or fatigue-critical 2xxx and 7xxx parts, and mixed-material stacks.
