Brazing vs Soldering vs Welding: Differences and How to Choose

Welding melts the parts being joined; brazing and soldering melt only a filler metal that flows into the joint. Brazing and soldering are separated by a convention: fillers that melt above 450 °C (840 °F) are brazing, below it soldering. For aluminium the choice matters more than for most metals, because the gap between filler and base-metal melting points is small and welding heat softens strong tempers.

The basics

What separates welding, brazing and soldering

The simplest way to remember the difference: welding fuses the parts, brazing and soldering glue them with molten metal. Everything else, from strength to distortion to which metals can be joined, follows from that.

In welding, the edges of the parts are melted, usually with a filler of similar composition, and solidify into one continuous piece of metal. The weld is a small casting, and the metal around it (the heat-affected zone) has been heated close to melting.

In brazing, the parts are heated but stay solid. A filler metal with a liquidus above 450 °C but below the solidus of the parts melts and is drawn into a closely fitted gap by capillary action, wetting both surfaces and forming a metallurgical bond as it solidifies. In soldering the principle is the same, but the filler melts below 450 °C, so joints are weaker and are used mainly for electrical continuity and sealing.

The 450 °C boundary is a definition used by the American Welding Society and international terminology, not a physical law. Some zinc-aluminium fillers melt across it, which is why you will see them called both solders and brazing alloys.

Welding

Welding aluminium: processes, fillers and the heat-affected zone

Aluminium welds well with the right process, but it behaves differently from steel. It conducts heat about four times faster, so it needs more heat input; it has a refractory oxide that must be broken up by AC current or cathodic cleaning; it absorbs hydrogen readily, causing porosity; and it gives no colour warning before melting.

GTAW (TIG)

AC TIG with argon shielding gives clean, precise welds on thin sheet, tube and critical joints. Slower than MIG but easier to control.

GMAW (MIG)

Spray or pulsed-spray transfer with argon or argon-helium is the production workhorse for structures, trailers, boats and tanks. Push-pull or spool guns help feed soft aluminium wire.

Friction stir welding

A solid-state process: a rotating tool stirs the metal without melting it. Very low distortion and high joint efficiency, widely used for rail car panels, ship decks and EV battery trays.

Laser and hybrid welding

High-speed, low heat input welding for automotive bodies and battery parts; needs precise fit-up.

Resistance spot welding

Used in automotive body construction; aluminium needs higher currents and more electrode maintenance than steel.

Stick (SMAW)

Possible with aluminium electrodes for repairs, but spatter, flux residue and porosity make it a last resort.

A key point for designers: welding does not leave heat-treated or work-hardened aluminium at its original strength. For 6061-T6, design codes use a welded tensile strength of about 165 MPa (24 ksi), roughly half the parent metal, unless the whole assembly is heat-treated again. Non-heat-treatable 5083 in the O or H111 condition loses very little, which is why it is the standard welded marine alloy. Welding is therefore strongest in absolute terms but not always strongest relative to what the parent metal could carry.

Brazing

Brazing aluminium: processes and the narrow temperature window

Aluminium brazing fillers are aluminium-silicon alloys that melt at about 577 – 615 °C, only a few tens of degrees below the solidus of the alloys being joined. Brazing aluminium is therefore a matter of precise temperature control and of choosing base alloys with a high enough solidus.

Controlled atmosphere brazing (CAB)

Parts clad with Al-Si are fluxed with non-corrosive potassium fluoroaluminate and brazed in a nitrogen furnace at about 600 °C. The dominant process for automotive radiators, condensers and HVAC coils.

Vacuum brazing

Flux-free brazing in vacuum using Mg-bearing clad layers. Used for aerospace heat exchangers, cold plates and parts that must be completely free of flux residue.

Dip (salt-bath) brazing

Assemblies are immersed in molten flux salt that heats them evenly. Good for complex parts but requires thorough cleaning afterwards.

Flame and induction brazing

Local heating with rods, rings or flux-cored filler for tube joints, repairs and lower-volume work. Induction gives repeatable, timed cycles.

Magnesium above about 0.5 % in the base alloy interferes with CAB fluxes, which is why many 5xxx and some 6xxx alloys are brazed in vacuum or with caesium-modified fluxes. For dissimilar joints, see our article on brazing aluminium to copper.

Soldering

Soldering aluminium

Soldering steel, copper and brass is routine, but aluminium is harder to solder because its oxide reforms instantly and ordinary rosin fluxes cannot remove it. It can be done with reactive or organic fluxes designed for aluminium, with ultrasonic soldering irons that break up the oxide mechanically under molten solder, or on plated surfaces (nickel or tin) that behave like copper.

Solders that work on aluminium are usually tin-zinc or zinc-aluminium based, because zinc wets aluminium well. Tin-lead solders wet aluminium poorly and create a strong galvanic couple. Soldered aluminium joints are suited to electrical connections, sealing and light-duty repairs, not to load-bearing structures.

  • Lowest heat input of the three: little distortion and no softening of the parent metal
  • Joint strength is low and drops further at elevated temperature
  • Galvanic corrosion is a risk if moisture reaches the joint, so seal it
  • Plating aluminium with nickel or tin often makes soldering far easier and more reliable

Pitfalls

Common mistakes when joining aluminium

Assuming welds match parent strength

Heat-treated tempers lose up to about half their strength in the heat-affected zone. Design to welded allowables.

Skipping oxide removal

Brush with stainless steel and clean just before welding or brazing; aluminium reoxidises within hours.

Wrong filler for the base metal

Using 4043 on high-Mg 5xxx alloys or 5356 for hot service causes cracking or corrosion. Check a filler selection chart.

Brazing too close to solidus

Brazing 6061 with a standard 4047 filler leaves almost no margin; parts sag or melt.

Ignoring galvanic corrosion

Dissimilar joints and zinc-rich fillers need sealing in wet service.

Leaving corrosive flux residue

Chloride fluxes must be washed off completely or the joint will corrode from within.

FAQ

Brazing, soldering and welding: frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between brazing and soldering?

Only the filler melting temperature. By convention, fillers that melt above 450 °C (840 °F) are brazing alloys and those below are solders. Brazed joints are stronger and tolerate higher service temperatures.

Is brazing stronger than welding?

Usually not in absolute terms; a well-made weld is the strongest joint. But a brazed joint with enough overlap area can be stronger than the surrounding metal in shear, and brazing avoids the local softening and distortion that welding causes.

Can aluminum be brazed?

Yes. Aluminium is brazed every day in very large volumes for heat exchangers, using Al-Si fillers at about 580 – 620 °C with fluoride flux or in vacuum. The base alloy must have a solidus comfortably above the filler.

Can aluminium be soldered?

Yes, with special aluminium fluxes, zinc-bearing solders or ultrasonic soldering, or after plating with nickel or tin. Soldered aluminium joints are for electrical and sealing duties rather than load.

Which is best for joining aluminium to copper?

Brazing with a zinc-aluminium filler and caesium-based flux, or a friction-welded transition piece. Direct fusion welding forms brittle copper-aluminium intermetallics.

Does welding weaken 6061-T6 aluminium?

Yes, locally. The heat-affected zone drops to roughly half of T6 strength; design codes use about 165 MPa welded tensile strength unless the part is solution treated and aged again after welding.
