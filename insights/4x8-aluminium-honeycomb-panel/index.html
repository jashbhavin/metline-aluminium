4x8 Aluminium Honeycomb Panel: What Drives the Cost

There is no single price for a 4x8 ft (1220 × 2440 mm) aluminium honeycomb panel, because a thin powder-coated ceiling panel and a thick, PVDF-coated, fire-classified façade panel are different products. Six choices set the cost: skin alloy and thickness, core alloy and density, panel thickness, coating, fire class and fabrication. This guide shows how each one moves the price, so you can compare quotations on equal terms.

Context

Why 4x8 panel quotations differ so much

The 4x8 format is popular because it matches standard coil widths, board-handling equipment and building modules. But the format fixes only the area. Everything that determines performance and cost sits in the build-up.

When two quotations for "4x8 honeycomb panels" differ widely, the cheaper one usually has thinner skins, a lighter core, a lower-grade coating, a lower fire class or less fabrication. None of these is visible on a sample corner, so ask every supplier to quote against the same written specification. Aluminium price movements on the LME also pass straight through to skin and core cost, so quotations are normally valid for a limited period.

Our advice: write the specification first, then ask for prices. The checklist at the end of this page is enough to get comparable offers.

Cost driver 1

Skin alloy and thickness

The two aluminium skins usually make up the largest share of the metal cost. Each 0.1 mm of skin adds 0.27 kg per square metre per face, or about 0.8 kg on one face of a 4x8 panel.

Skin optionTypical thicknessWhere it fits
3003-H24 / 31050.5 – 1.0 mmInterior panels, ceilings, furniture; economical and easy to coat
5005-H14 / H240.8 – 1.5 mmArchitectural panels, especially anodised finishes
5052-H320.8 – 2.0 mmCoastal façades, marine and transport panels needing strength and corrosion resistance
Brazing sheet (clad)0.5 – 1.5 mmBrazed panels without organic adhesive

Façade panels commonly use 1.0 mm outer and 1.0 mm or 0.7 mm inner skins. Going thinner saves money but increases the risk of visible dimpling over the cells ("telegraphing"), dents from handling and loss of flatness on large panels. A thicker outer skin is the cheapest way to improve impact resistance; a taller core is the cheapest way to improve stiffness.

Cost driver 2

Core alloy, cell size, density and panel thickness

The honeycomb core sets panel thickness, carries shear and contributes a small but meaningful share of the weight. Three core choices affect cost:

  • Alloy: 3003 core for dry interiors; 5052 for façades, marine and transport; 5056 where higher shear strength is needed. See our 3003 vs 5052 comparison.
  • Density: higher density means more metal, higher crush and shear strength and higher cost. Floors and transport panels need far denser cores than ceilings.
  • Cell size: smaller cells support thin skins better and give a flatter face, but raise density and cost.
  • Panel thickness: bending stiffness rises roughly with the square of the distance between skins, so going from 15 to 20 mm adds a lot of stiffness for little extra metal.

Cost driver 3

Coating system and finish

Polyester / powder coat

Lowest cost; suited to interiors and sheltered exteriors. Look for AAMA 2603/2604 or Qualicoat class approval where specified.

PVDF (fluorocarbon)

The standard for exterior façades: high colour and gloss retention. Usually specified to AAMA 2605 for long-life façades. Two- and three-coat systems cost progressively more.

FEVE and special finishes

High-durability alternatives with good gloss options; also wood-grain and stone-grain transfer prints for decorative work.

Anodised skins

A metallic, premium look. Needs anodising-quality skin alloys such as 5005 and careful handling, so it costs more than painted skins.

Mill finish / primer only

For panels that will be laminated, painted on site or hidden in an assembly.

Protective film

A peelable film on the coated face is standard and inexpensive; specify UV-resistant film if panels will sit outdoors before installation.

Cost driver 4

Fire classification: often the biggest cost step

Fire class can change cost more than any other single choice. In Europe, reaction to fire is classified under EN 13501-1, with A1 and A2-s1,d0 at the top, followed by B, C and lower classes. Many countries now require A2-s1,d0 (or A1) for façade materials on high-rise and public buildings. Other markets refer to national classes such as B1 under DIN 4102 or GB 8624; these are not directly equivalent to EN classes, so quote the exact standard and class from your project specification.

A standard adhesive-bonded panel contains polymer adhesive film, which adds combustible content. Reaching A2 with an adhesive system requires low-organic adhesives, controlled glue weight and testing of the complete build-up, all of which raise cost. Brazed panels join skins and core metallurgically with an aluminium-silicon braze alloy, removing most of the organic content. They are more expensive to make but suit projects with strict fire, high-temperature or long-term durability requirements.

Cost driver 5

Fabrication and fixing details

Few panels go to site as plain flat sheets. Every operation below adds machine and labour time and tightens the tolerance chain:

  • Routing and folding the edges into returns or trays
  • Grooves and V-cuts for folded corners
  • Inserts and back-bolt holes for concealed fixings
  • Stiffening ribs or extrusions bonded to the back face for large panels
  • Hanging lugs, tongue-and-groove or hook-on edge profiles to match a façade or ceiling system
  • Edge sealing to keep moisture out of the core

Ask suppliers to price fabrication as separate line items. It makes offers comparable and shows where a change of detail, such as a standard edge profile instead of a bespoke one, can save money.

Getting comparable quotes

Specification checklist for 4x8 honeycomb panels

  1. PanelSize (1220 × 2440 mm or other), overall thickness and quantity.
  2. SkinsAlloy, temper and thickness of the outer and inner faces.
  3. CoreAlloy, cell size, foil gauge or density, and L-direction orientation.
  4. FinishCoating type, colour code, gloss, coating standard and film.
  5. PerformanceFire class with the exact standard, wind load, flatness tolerance and any peel or shear tests.
  6. FabricationDrawings showing folds, grooves, inserts and fixings.
  7. LogisticsDelivery port, Incoterm (EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP) and packing.

FAQ

4x8 aluminium honeycomb panels: frequently asked questions

What size is a 4x8 aluminum honeycomb panel in metric?

1220 × 2440 mm, an area of about 2.98 m². Many suppliers also offer 1250 × 2500 mm and custom sizes cut from larger panels.

How much does a 4x8 honeycomb panel weigh?

It depends on the build-up. A 20 mm panel with two 1.0 mm skins and a 55 kg/m³ core weighs about 19 kg before adhesive and coating; a 10 mm interior panel with 0.7 + 0.5 mm skins is roughly 11 kg.

Why is an A2 fire-rated panel more expensive?

It needs low-organic adhesives with controlled glue weight, or a brazed construction, plus testing of the full build-up to EN 13501-1. These add material and process cost.

Which core should I use for a façade panel?

5052 core in most cases, because the panel faces moisture and wind load for decades. 3003 core is better kept for dry interiors.

Can 4x8 honeycomb panels be cut on site?

They can be cut with suitable saws, but cut edges expose the core and must be sealed. Ordering panels cut and edge-finished to size gives cleaner, more durable results.

What should I send to get an accurate quotation?

Panel size, thickness and quantity, skin alloy and thickness, core alloy and density or cell size, coating and colour, fire class, drawings of folds and fixings, and the delivery port.
