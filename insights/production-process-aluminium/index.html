How to Choose the Right Aluminium Production Process

Every aluminium part starts as molten metal and reaches its final shape by one of five main routes: rolling, extrusion, drawing, forging or casting. The route decides which shapes are possible, how strong the part can be, what tolerances are realistic and how cost changes with volume. This guide explains each process, the products it makes, and a practical way to choose between them.

Overview

Wrought and cast: the two families

Aluminium processing divides into wrought processing, where solid metal is plastically deformed, and casting, where molten metal is poured or injected into a mould. Wrought products have finer, denser structures and higher ductility; castings allow complex shapes in one step.

Primary producers cast molten aluminium into slabs, billets, wire rod or ingot. Direct-chill (DC) casting produces the large rectangular slabs used for rolling and the round billets used for extrusion and forging; continuous casters produce strip and wire rod directly.

Process 1

Rolling: plate, sheet, coil and foil

Rolling reduces thickness by passing metal between rotating rolls. A scalped and homogenised slab is hot rolled, typically starting around 500 °C, through a reversing mill and often a tandem finishing mill into hot band of a few millimetres, or into plate if rolling stops earlier. Cold rolling then reduces the band to sheet, strip and foil stock while increasing strength through work hardening; intermediate and final annealing set the temper.

Foil is the extreme case: the thinnest gauges, around 6 – 7 µm, are made by rolling two sheets together (pack or double rolling), which is why household foil has one bright and one matt side. Plate in heat-treatable alloys is solution heat-treated, quenched and stretched (T651, T7351) to relieve residual stress.

Strengths

Very wide flat products at low cost per kilogram; excellent surface and thickness control; tempers from O to full hard.

Limits

Only flat products; shapes require further forming, stamping or machining.

Choose it for

Panels, skins, enclosures, packaging, heat-exchanger fins, machined plate parts.

Process 2

Extrusion: profiles, bars and tubes

In extrusion, a billet preheated to roughly 400 – 500 °C is pushed through a steel die by a hydraulic ram, emerging as a continuous length with the die's cross-section. Solid dies make bars and open profiles; porthole and bridge dies split the metal around a mandrel and reweld it to make hollow profiles and tube. The profile is quenched at the press (or later solution treated), stretched to straighten it and aged to temper.

Extrusion is uniquely flexible: almost any constant cross-section can be produced, and die costs are low compared with forging or casting tooling, so even modest volumes justify a custom profile. 6060 and 6063 extrude fastest and finish best; 6061 and 6082 give structural strength; 2xxx and 7xxx alloys extrude slowly and cost more.

Strengths

Complex constant sections, integrated features (screw bosses, snap fits, heat-sink fins), low tooling cost, good tolerances.

Limits

Section must be constant along the length; very thin walls and large sections are constrained by press size and alloy.

Choose it for

Window and façade systems, frames, rails, heat sinks, tube, bar for machining, vehicle structures.

Process 3

Drawing: precision tube, bar and wire

Drawing pulls an extruded or rolled product through a die at room temperature to reduce its size. Tube is drawn over a plug or mandrel to control both outside and inside diameter, bar through a shaped die, and wire through a series of dies. Because the metal is cold worked, drawn products are stronger in non-heat-treatable alloys and have tighter tolerances and smoother surfaces than extrusions.

Drawn tube is used where exact sizes and finish matter: hydraulic and pneumatic cylinders, instrument and heat-exchanger tubes, antenna and telescopic tubes. Wire drawing produces electrical conductors (1350, 6101, 8xxx alloys), welding wire and rivet wire.

Process 4

Forging: strength where it counts

Forging compresses a heated billet between dies, usually in a hydraulic press for aluminium. Open-die forging makes blocks, discs, shafts and rings; closed-die forging makes near-net-shape parts in volume; ring rolling makes seamless rings. Forging closes porosity and aligns the grain with the part outline, giving the best combination of strength, ductility, fatigue and toughness of any route.

Strengths

Highest mechanical integrity, grain flow following the load path, reliable fatigue performance.

Limits

Dedicated tooling for closed-die parts; less geometric freedom than casting; machining usually needed.

Choose it for

Wheels, suspension and steering parts, aircraft fittings, high-pressure fittings, rings and flanges.

Process 5

Casting: complex shapes in one step

Casting methodHow it worksBest for
High-pressure die castingMolten alloy injected at high speed into steel diesThin-walled housings and structural parts in very high volume
Low-pressure and gravity die castingMetal fills a permanent mould under low pressure or gravityWheels, cylinder heads, heat-treatable A356 parts
Sand castingMetal poured into a sand mouldLarge or low-volume parts, prototypes
Investment castingCeramic shell around a wax patternSmall, intricate, high-precision parts

Casting alloys are high in silicon for fluidity. Castings allow internal passages and complex geometry that no wrought route can make, but they usually have lower ductility and fatigue strength than wrought products of similar strength because of porosity and coarser structure.

Temperature

Hot, warm and cold working

ModeTemperatureEffect on aluminiumExamples
Hot workingAbove recrystallisation, roughly 300 – 500 °C depending on alloyLow forces, large reductions, no work hardeningHot rolling, extrusion, hot forging
Warm workingBetween room temperature and recrystallisationModerate forces, better tolerance than hotWarm forging, warm forming of 5xxx sheet
Cold workingRoom temperatureWork hardening, tight tolerance, fine finishCold rolling, drawing, cold forging, stamping

For non-heat-treatable alloys (1xxx, 3xxx, 5xxx), cold work is the main way to raise strength, recorded in the H temper designation. For heat-treatable alloys (2xxx, 6xxx, 7xxx), strength comes from solution treatment and ageing, recorded in the T temper, and cold work is used mainly for shape and flatness.

Developments

Newer and hybrid aluminium processes

  • Continuous casting and rolling: twin-roll and belt casters produce strip directly, skipping hot rolling, for foil stock and building sheet.
  • Continuous rotary extrusion (Conform): feedstock rod is extruded continuously into small sections, conductors and multiport tube.
  • Semi-solid forming: partly solidified alloy is cast or forged, combining casting's shape freedom with lower porosity.
  • Squeeze casting and liquid forging: metal solidifies under high pressure for near-wrought soundness.
  • Roll bonding and clad rolling: layers of different alloys rolled together, as in brazing sheet and clad aerospace plate.
  • Additive manufacturing: laser powder-bed fusion of AlSi10Mg and newer high-strength alloys for complex low-volume parts.
Selection guide

How to choose the right route

If your part is…ConsiderWhy
Flat, panel-like or thinRolled sheet or plate, then cut, formed or machinedLowest cost per kilogram; huge size range
Long with a constant sectionExtrusionLow tooling cost; features built into the profile
Round tube or wire needing tight sizeDrawn tube or wirePrecise OD/ID and finish
Highly stressed, fatigue-criticalForgingBest grain structure and toughness
Complex 3D shape with internal cavitiesCastingShape freedom in one step
Low volume, high accuracyMachining from plate, bar or forged blockNo tooling; fast to change
  1. Define loads and environmentStrength, fatigue, corrosion and temperature set the alloy family.
  2. Define geometryFlat, constant-section, round or 3D determines the candidate routes.
  3. Estimate volumeTooling cost per part falls with volume, favouring forging and die casting at high quantities.
  4. Check tolerances and finishDrawn, machined and precision-forged parts reach tighter tolerances than as-cast or as-extruded.
  5. Compare total costInclude material yield, tooling, machining, finishing and scrap, not only price per kilogram.

FAQ

Aluminium production processes: frequently asked questions

What are the main aluminium manufacturing processes?

Rolling (plate, sheet, foil), extrusion (profiles, bar, tube), drawing (precision tube and wire), forging (high-strength parts) and casting (complex shapes). Machining and forming then finish most parts.

Is extruded or rolled aluminium stronger?

Neither in general; strength depends on alloy and temper. 6061-T6 has similar minimum properties whether rolled or extruded. Forgings typically offer the best fatigue and toughness for the same alloy.

When should I choose forging over machining from plate?

When the part is highly stressed or fatigue-loaded and the volume justifies tooling. Forging keeps grain flow along the part outline and uses less material than machining from a thick plate.

Why is cast aluminum less ductile than wrought aluminum?

Castings contain some porosity and a coarser, silicon-rich structure. Wrought processes break up and close these features, giving higher elongation and fatigue strength.

Which process is cheapest for aluminium parts?

It depends on volume and shape. For long constant sections, extrusion is usually cheapest; for flat parts, rolled sheet; for complex parts in high volume, die casting; for low volumes, machining from stock.
