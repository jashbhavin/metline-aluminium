7 Measuring Tools for Inspecting Aluminium Products

Most disputes over aluminium tolerances are really disputes over measurement. Callipers, micrometers, height gauges, feeler gauges, pin gauges, optical 2D systems and coordinate measuring machines each suit different features, and each gives misleading numbers if used on the wrong job. This article explains which tool to use for which dimension on sheet, bar, tube and extruded profiles, and the aluminium-specific traps to avoid.

Before you measure

Three rules that matter more than the tool

A measuring tool is only useful when its resolution, its accuracy and the conditions of the measurement all fit the tolerance being checked. Get those three right and the choice of instrument becomes straightforward.

Match resolution to tolerance. A common rule of thumb in workshops is that the instrument should resolve about one tenth of the total tolerance band. A wall thickness of 2.00 mm ±0.15 mm has a band of 0.30 mm, so a calliper reading to 0.01 mm is adequate. A bore of 32.00 mm +0.039/0 has a band of only 0.039 mm, which calls for a bore gauge, micrometer or pin gauges, not a calliper.

Control temperature. Dimensions on drawings refer to 20 °C, the standard reference temperature defined in ISO 1. Aluminium expands by roughly 23 µm per metre for every degree Celsius, about twice the rate of steel. A 1 m long profile measured 8 °C above reference will read about 0.18 mm long, and a steel tape or steel gauge will not cancel that error because it expands only half as much. Let parts settle to the room temperature before precise checks, and note the temperature on the report.

Respect the softness of aluminium. Annealed sheet, thin-wall tube and 6063 profiles deflect under jaw pressure. Heavy-handed measurement squeezes a thin wall and reads small, or presses a tube oval. Use light, repeatable force (ratchets and friction thimbles exist for this reason) and support long parts so that they do not sag during the check.

Tool 1

Callipers: the everyday all-rounder

A calliper measures outside dimensions, inside dimensions, steps and depths with a single instrument, which is why it sits on every inspection bench. Three versions are common, and they differ mainly in how the reading is shown.

Digital calliper

Typically resolves 0.01 mm, switches between metric and inch, and can zero at any point for comparative checks. The default for profile and bar inspection.

Dial calliper

Reads 0.02 mm (or 0.01 mm on finer models) from a dial. No battery, and the needle makes small variations easy to see while sliding along a part.

Vernier calliper

Reads 0.02 mm or 0.05 mm from a vernier scale. Robust and cheap, but slower to read and more prone to reading error; best for rough checks.

  • Wipe the jaws by closing them lightly on clean paper and drawing it out, then zero the calliper with the jaws closed.
  • Keep the jaws square to the surface; a tilted calliper always reads large on outside dimensions.
  • Measure with the part of the jaws closest to the beam, where the instrument is most accurate.
  • For depth near an internal radius, keep the depth rod vertical and as close to the radius as possible without sitting on it.
  • On round bar and tube, rotate the part and measure at several points along its length to reveal ovality and taper.
  • Check the calliper against a gauge block at regular intervals as part of your calibration routine.
Tool 2

Micrometers: thickness, diameter and wall

Where a calliper reads to 0.01 mm, an outside micrometer reads to 0.01 mm with far better accuracy, and digital models resolve 0.001 mm. That makes it the right tool for sheet and plate thickness, bar diameter and tube wall thickness, all of which are commonly toleranced in hundredths of a millimetre.

Always turn the ratchet or friction thimble rather than the main barrel, so that every reading is taken at the same measuring force. Hold the frame by its insulated pads; the heat of a hand can grow a 25 mm steel frame by a micrometre or two within a minute.

Sheet and plate

Rolled products are often slightly thicker at the centre of the width than at the edges (crown). Take readings across the width at least 10 mm in from the edge, as the tolerance standards require, rather than relying on one corner reading.

Tube wall

Use a tube micrometer with a ball or pointed anvil inside the bore, so that contact is a point rather than a flat face spanning the curved inner wall.

Bar diameter

Measure two diameters at right angles at each position; the difference shows ovality, which drawn bar and extruded bar can have to different degrees.

Tool 3

Height gauges on a surface plate

A height gauge stands on a granite or cast-iron surface plate and measures vertical distances from that reference: step heights on a machined block, the position of a slot in a profile, or the distance between two flanges. Digital height gauges can also scribe layout lines for drilling or cutting.

Accuracy depends on the reference as much as the gauge. Clean both the plate and the gauge base, check the plate is free of burrs and swarf (aluminium chips are soft but still lift the base), and approach each surface slowly so the probe does not bounce. For profiles with an unstable shape, clamp the part to an angle plate so that it cannot rock while measured.

Tool 4

Feeler gauges for flatness, straightness and squareness

Form tolerances on aluminium products are usually checked by placing the part against a reference and measuring the largest gap with feeler (thickness) gauge leaves. It is simple, quick and close to how the standards define these tolerances.

Flatness of sheet or plateLay a precision straightedge on the surface, or lay the part on a surface plateLargest gap under the straightedge over the stated length

Long products should be laid on the plate under their own weight only. Pressing a twisted profile flat to measure it gives a good number and a wrong result. Where the standard specifies a tolerance per length (for example per metre or over the full length), measure over exactly that length so the result can be compared directly.

Tool 5

Pin gauges for holes, slots and gaps

Pin gauges are hardened, ground cylinders made in fine size steps. Choosing the largest pin that enters a hole freely gives its effective diameter, which is often more meaningful than a two-point calliper reading because it reflects the size a mating shaft will actually see.

  • Insert the pin square to the hole; forcing it at an angle damages soft aluminium bores and gives a false fit.
  • Hold pins in a pin holder or magnetic V-block so they do not roll, get dropped or warm up in the hand.
  • For slot widths, test along the whole slot length; extruded slots can narrow where the die bearing changes.
  • For holes larger than any pin in the set, use a bore gauge or inside micrometer instead of combining pins.
  • Use go/no-go plug gauges for production checks where the decision is simply pass or fail.

For fits defined by ISO 286 tolerance classes such as H7, our fit and tolerance calculator gives the limits to check against.

Tool 6

Optical 2D measuring systems for profile sections

A video or optical 2D measuring system uses a camera, telecentric optics and edge-detection software to measure a flat object without touching it. For aluminium extrusions it is the most efficient way to check a complete cross-section: a thin slice is cut square from the profile, placed on the glass stage, and the software compares every edge, radius, angle and wall against the CAD drawing in one pass.

Results are only as good as the sample. Cut the slice square, deburr it without rounding the edges, and keep it thin enough that the walls are seen as sharp silhouettes. For profiles larger than the field of view, the stage moves and the software stitches the images together. Contour reports from such systems are a practical basis for first-article approval of a new die.

Tool 7

Coordinate measuring machines for complex parts

A coordinate measuring machine (CMM) moves a touch or scanning probe in three axes and records points on the part, from which software calculates sizes, positions, flatness, parallelism, perpendicularity and true position to the datums on the drawing. It is used for first-article inspection of machined parts, for parts with geometric dimensioning and tolerancing (GD&T), and wherever hand tools cannot reach every feature.

Because aluminium is so sensitive to temperature, CMMs belong in temperature-controlled rooms, and parts need time to soak before measurement; a heavy block straight from a warm machine shop can take hours to settle. Fixturing must hold the part without distorting it, which matters especially for thin-wall machined housings.

Practical advice

Common measurement mistakes on aluminium

  • Measuring hot material. Profiles fresh from ageing ovens or parts straight from machining read large; wait for them to reach room temperature.
  • Using a calliper for a tight bore. Two-point calliper readings miss ovality and cannot resolve tolerances of a few hundredths.
  • Squeezing thin walls. Excess force on soft tempers or 1 mm walls reads small and can leave marks on decorative surfaces.
  • Measuring at the edges only. Rolled sheet and plate vary across the width; extrusions vary along the length and near the ends.
  • Forcing parts flat. Clamping a bowed or twisted part before measuring form hides the defect you are trying to find.
  • Unclear datums. Without stated datums, two inspectors can measure the same position from different edges and both be right.
  • Uncalibrated tools. Every instrument used for acceptance should carry a traceable calibration status.

When ordering aluminium, the simplest way to avoid disagreements is to mark the critical dimensions on the drawing, name the tolerance standard, and agree how and where they will be measured before production starts.

FAQ

Measuring aluminium parts: frequently asked questions

What is the most accurate hand tool for measuring aluminium sheet thickness?

An outside micrometer with a ratchet stop. Digital micrometers resolve 0.001 mm, and readings should be taken at several points across the width, at least 10 mm from the edge, because rolled sheet is not perfectly uniform.

Why do aluminium measurements change during the day?

Aluminium expands about 23 µm per metre per °C, twice as much as steel. A change of a few degrees in workshop temperature moves the length of a long profile by tenths of a millimetre, so precise checks should be made at, or corrected to, 20 °C.

Can I use a calliper to check an H7 hole?

Not reliably. An H7 tolerance on a 20 mm hole is only 0.021 mm wide, which is close to a calliper's resolution and below its practical accuracy. Use pin gauges, a plug gauge or a bore gauge.

How is flatness of aluminium plate checked?

The plate is laid on a surface plate, or a precision straightedge is laid on the plate, and feeler gauges measure the largest gap over the length stated in the tolerance standard. The plate must not be pressed down during the check.

When is a CMM needed for aluminium parts?

For machined parts with positional tolerances, GD&T callouts, many features or first-article approval. Simple bar, sheet and profile dimensions are normally checked faster with hand tools and optical 2D systems.

What inspection documents come with aluminium (aluminum) material from Metline?

Each shipment is supplied with a mill test certificate (EN 10204 3.1) showing chemistry and mechanical properties, plus a packing list. Dimensional reports on agreed critical features can be arranged on request.
