Ball Bonding vs Wedge Bonding: How Semiconductor Wire Bonding Works

Ball bonding melts a small sphere on the wire tip and presses it onto the chip pad with a capillary, which suits fine-pitch ICs and LEDs bonded with gold or copper wire. Wedge bonding flattens the wire under a wedge tool with ultrasonic energy and no ball, which is the standard method for aluminium wire and ribbon in IGBT, MOSFET and EV power modules. The right choice follows from pitch, current and the pad metallisation.

The short answer

Two ways to make the same connection

Wire bonding is still the most widely used method of connecting a semiconductor die to its package, despite flip-chip and sintered clip alternatives. It is flexible, cheap per connection and proven over decades. Almost every bond made is either a ball bond or a wedge bond.

Process

How ball bonding works

  1. Free-air ballAn electronic flame-off (EFO) spark melts the wire tail. Surface tension pulls the molten metal into a sphere, typically 1.5 – 2.5 times the wire diameter.
  2. First bondThe capillary lowers the ball onto the heated die pad and applies force and ultrasonic vibration. The ball deforms and forms an intermetallic weld with the pad metallisation, usually aluminium.
  3. LoopingThe capillary rises and moves along a programmed path to form a loop of controlled height and shape, clearing the die edge and neighbouring wires.
  4. Second (stitch) bondThe capillary presses the wire onto the lead frame or substrate finger, forming a crescent-shaped stitch bond.
  5. Tail and breakThe clamp closes and the capillary rises, breaking the wire and leaving a tail ready for the next free-air ball.

Gold was the traditional ball-bonding wire because it does not oxidise, so the free-air ball forms cleanly in air. Copper replaced much of it on cost grounds and offers higher conductivity, but copper oxidises when molten, so the ball is formed under forming gas (nitrogen with a few percent hydrogen), and its greater hardness needs careful control to avoid cratering the pad. Palladium-coated copper and silver alloys are further options.

Aluminium is rarely ball bonded: its oxide skin makes a consistent free-air ball very difficult. That is the main reason aluminium wire lives in the wedge-bonding world.

Process

How wedge bonding works

  1. FeedThe wire or ribbon passes through a hole at the back of the wedge and lies under the flat tip.
  2. First bondThe wedge presses the wire onto the pad while an ultrasonic transducer (typically about 40 – 120 kHz) scrubs it. The vibration breaks the oxide films and creates a solid-state weld without melting.
  3. LoopThe tool moves to the second position, forming a loop. Because the wire leaves the wedge in one direction, loops run in line with the tool.
  4. Second bondA second wedge bond is made on the terminal, substrate or busbar.
  5. CutFine wire is torn off by a clamp; heavy wire and ribbon are cut by a blade built into the bond head.

Aluminium wedge bonding to aluminium pads forms a monometallic joint, so there is no gold-aluminium intermetallic growth to worry about at high temperature. That is an important reliability advantage in power electronics that run hot for years.

Why aluminium dominates power wedge bonding: it is soft enough to bond on sensitive chip metallisations with ultrasound alone, it carries large currents at low weight and cost, and it forms monometallic bonds with aluminium pads. Its weakness is power-cycling fatigue at the heel and bond interface, caused by the thermal-expansion mismatch between aluminium (≈ 23 µm/m·K) and silicon (≈ 2.6 µm/m·K). Ribbon, larger bond footprints, doped wire and copper-cored wire are all responses to that.

Pitch first

Pad pitches below roughly 60 µm almost always point to ball bonding with fine wire.

Current second

Above a few amperes per connection, multiple heavy aluminium wires or a ribbon in wedge bonding is the practical solution.

Temperature third

If the substrate or die cannot take a heated stage, room-temperature aluminium wedge bonding is the safe choice.

Quality

Testing bond quality

Common test methods

Destructive wire pull
MIL-STD-883 Method 2011: hook pulls the loop until failure; force and failure location are recorded
Non-destructive pull
MIL-STD-883 Method 2023: loop pulled to a set force without breaking
Bond shear
JEDEC JESD22-B116: shear tool pushes the ball or wedge off the pad; for aluminium wedges the result is compared with the wire tensile strength
Visual inspection
Bond footprint, deformation ratio, heel cracks and loop shape under magnification
Reliability
Temperature cycling, power cycling, high-temperature storage and humidity tests to qualification standards

Typical failure modes differ by method. Ball bonds suffer from intermetallic growth and voiding (gold on aluminium) or pad cratering (copper). Aluminium wedge bonds most often fail by heel cracking and lift-off after many power cycles, which is why heel geometry and loop shape are tightly controlled.

FAQ

Ball and wedge bonding: frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between ball and wedge bonding?

Ball bonding forms a melted ball on the wire and bonds it with a capillary using heat, force and ultrasound; it is omnidirectional and suits fine pitch. Wedge bonding presses the wire flat under a wedge with ultrasound and no ball; it is directional and suits aluminium wire, ribbon and high current.

Why is aluminium wire not used for ball bonding?

Aluminium forms an oxide skin instantly when molten, so a round, consistent free-air ball is very hard to produce. Wedge bonding avoids the ball entirely and breaks the oxide mechanically with ultrasound.

Why do power modules use aluminium (aluminum) wire?

Heavy aluminium wire carries high current cheaply, bonds at room temperature without damaging the chip and forms monometallic joints with aluminium pads. Ribbon or copper-cored wire is used where power-cycling life must be extended.

What size aluminium wire is used in IGBT modules?

Heavy wire, usually between about 100 and 500 µm in diameter, with several wires per chip in parallel. Ribbon is used when fewer, larger connections are preferred.

How is wire bond strength tested?

By destructive pull testing (MIL-STD-883 Method 2011), non-destructive pull (Method 2023) and bond shear (JESD22-B116), plus visual inspection of bond footprint and heel.

Does Metline supply gold or copper bonding wire?

No. Metline supplies aluminium only: fine Al-1 % Si wire, heavy aluminium wire and aluminium ribbon. Gold and copper are discussed here as comparisons.
