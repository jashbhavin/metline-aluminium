Aluminium Foil vs Tin Foil: The Real Difference

The roll in your kitchen drawer that people call "tin foil" is almost certainly aluminium foil. True tin foil was common until the early twentieth century, when rolled aluminium replaced it because it is lighter, cheaper, does not taint food and handles oven heat. Here is how the two metals compare, property by property.

Definition

What is aluminium foil?

Aluminium foil is aluminium or aluminium alloy rolled thinner than about 0.2 mm. Household foil is typically 9 to 25 µm thick and is usually made from alloys 8011, 8079 or 1235 in the soft annealed (O) temper, which makes it easy to fold and shape around food.

Thin foil is rolled two layers at a time in the final pass. The faces in contact with the polished rolls come out bright, and the faces touching each other come out matt. That is the only reason one side is shiny: both sides perform the same in cooking, and the difference in heat reflection is too small to matter in a home oven.

Definition

What was tin foil?

Tin foil was a thin sheet of tin, sometimes alloyed with lead, beaten or rolled from the metal. It was used from the nineteenth century to wrap food, tobacco and confectionery and in early sound recordings on phonograph cylinders. Tin is soft and corrosion resistant, but it is heavy, expensive and gives a faint metallic taste to food it touches.

Commercial aluminium foil rolling began in Europe around 1910, and over the following decades aluminium displaced tin almost completely. The name stuck: in the UK, US and many other countries people still say "tin foil" for what is in fact aluminium.

Food contact

Is aluminium foil safe for food?

Yes, for normal use. Aluminium foil for food contact is made to standards such as ASTM B479 and EN 546, and in Europe food-contact materials must also comply with Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004. Small amounts of aluminium can migrate into food, and migration rises with acidity, salt and temperature.

Practical guidance from food-safety agencies is simple: wrapping, covering and cooking with foil is fine, but avoid storing very acidic or salty foods (tomato sauce, citrus, pickled or marinated dishes) in direct contact with foil for long periods. Use a lining of baking paper, or a different container, for those.

Kitchen tips

  • Either side of the foil can face the food
  • Do not let foil touch the heating element or block oven vents
  • In an air fryer or microwave, follow the appliance maker's instructions
  • Store acidic leftovers in glass or plastic, not foil
Beyond the kitchen

Where aluminium foil is used industrially

Household rolls are a small part of the market. Most aluminium foil goes into flexible packaging, pharmaceutical blister and lidding, containers, cable wrap, insulation facings, heat-exchanger fins, transformer windings and lithium battery current collectors. Tin has no practical role in any of these today. Metline supplies aluminium foil in 1xxx, 3xxx and 8xxx alloys for these industrial uses.

Sustainability

Recycling and reuse

Aluminium is recyclable again and again without losing its properties, and remelting needs only a small fraction of the energy used to make primary metal. Clean foil can go into household recycling in many areas: scrunch it into a ball at least the size of a fist so sorting equipment can catch it, and remove food residue first. Foil laminated with paper or plastic is harder to recover and depends on local facilities.

FAQ

Aluminium foil vs tin foil: frequently asked questions

Is tin foil the same as aluminum foil?

Not technically. Tin foil was made of tin; modern "tin foil" is aluminium foil. Aluminium replaced tin during the first half of the twentieth century and the old name stayed in everyday speech.

Can you still buy real tin foil?

Only as a specialist product for crafts, laboratory or electronics use. It is not sold as kitchen foil in normal retail channels.

Which side of aluminium foil should face the food?

Either. The shiny and matt sides are a result of rolling two sheets together and perform the same in cooking. Non-stick foils are the exception: follow the label.

Why did aluminium replace tin foil?

Aluminium is lighter, cheaper, conducts heat better, has a far higher melting point and does not give food a metallic taste.

Is aluminium foil recyclable?

Yes, if it is clean. Rinse it, scrunch it into a ball and check local recycling rules.
