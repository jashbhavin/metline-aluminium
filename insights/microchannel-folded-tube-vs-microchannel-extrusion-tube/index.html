Folded vs Extruded Microchannel Tube: Which Suits Your Heat Exchanger?

Extruded multiport tube is the established workhorse of brazed aluminium condensers and gas coolers; folded tube, roll-formed from clad aluminium strip, is the challenger that offers thinner walls, lower weight and corrosion protection built into the material. Neither is better everywhere. This article compares the two on forming route, alloy, wall thickness, corrosion, thermal and hydraulic performance, pressure rating and cost, and ends with a practical selection table.

Quick answer

The difference in one table

Choose extruded microchannel tube for proven, cost-optimised, high-volume designs and for high-pressure circuits with many small ports. Consider folded tube when weight, long corrosion life in salty or humid air, or thinner walls justify requalifying the heat exchanger.

Manufacturing

How each tube is made

Extruded multiport tube. A preheated billet of a soft, highly extrudable alloy is pushed through a porthole die that splits and rewelds the metal around a set of mandrel tongues, forming all ports in one pass. The flat tube is often extruded directly into coils, then straightened, zinc-sprayed in line and cut to length.

Because the ports are shaped by the die, designers can choose many small channels, round or rectangular ports, internal micro-fins and a thickened leading nose. The trade-off is that the die has to survive very high extrusion pressures through tiny gaps, which sets a practical limit on how thin walls and webs can go.

Folded tube. A thin brazing strip, typically under 0.3 mm, is roll-formed through a series of stands into a flat tube. In one-piece designs the strip is folded back on itself to form the internal webs; in two-piece designs a separate corrugated inner fin is inserted into a formed outer shell.

The seams and web contacts are sealed by the braze clad during furnace brazing of the core, so the tube only becomes leak-tight when the heat exchanger is brazed. Wall thickness is set by rolling, not by die strength, which is why folded tube can be thinner and more uniform.

Both tubes are used in controlled-atmosphere (CAB) brazed aluminium heat exchangers with louvered fins and header pipes. Neither is a finished heat exchanger; Metline supplies the aluminium tube, fin stock, brazing sheet and header material to heat-exchanger makers.

Metallurgy

Material structure: monolithic extrusion vs multi-layer clad strip

An extruded tube is a single alloy throughout. Extrusion alloys must flow through very fine die gaps, so they are lean: commercially pure 1xxx or dilute Al-Mn 3xxx grades, often with tightly controlled iron and silicon and small additions of copper, manganese or titanium to improve strength and corrosion behaviour. These "long-life" extrusion alloys resist pitting better than plain 1100 or 3003, but the tube still relies on an external zinc layer as its main sacrificial protection.

Folded tube starts as a rolled composite. A typical strip has a 3xxx-type core for strength, an outer Al-Si braze layer (4343 or 4045 type) that bonds the fins, and in many designs an internal or interliner layer. During brazing, silicon diffuses from the clad into the core and forms a dense precipitate zone, often called the brown band, which is electrochemically more active than the core beneath it. Together with deliberate zinc or copper gradients through the layers, this makes corrosion spread sideways along the surface rather than drilling straight through the wall.

Layer (folded tube, typical)Function
Outer braze clad (Al-Si)Melts during brazing to join fins and seal folds; after brazing leaves a protective, more anodic zone
Core (Al-Mn based)Carries pressure and strength; alloyed so its potential is nobler than the surface layers
Interliner or inner clad (where used)Limits silicon penetration, adds sacrificial protection or brazes internal webs

The practical result is that folded-tube corrosion protection is part of the alloy design and does not depend on how evenly a zinc spray was applied on a production line.

Weight

Wall thickness and weight

Wall thickness is the biggest single driver of tube weight. Extruded microchannel tubes typically have walls of about 0.22 – 0.40 mm, while folded tubes commonly run 0.18 – 0.26 mm with internal webs as thin as about 0.08 mm in two-piece designs. Published supplier data for a 12 × 1.4 mm two-piece folded tube with a 0.20 mm wall give a mass of roughly 20 g per metre.

Across comparable envelopes, folded tube is generally 10 – 20 % lighter than extruded tube. Because tubes make up a large share of a condenser's aluminium, that saving carries through to the heat exchanger, and further weight can be removed if the fins can be made from unclad fin stock (the folded tube's own clad supplies the braze filler).

Where the weight saving matters most

  • Electric-vehicle thermal systems, where every kilogram affects range
  • Rooftop and transport refrigeration units that are lifted or carried
  • Large commercial condensers and heat pumps, where tube mass dominates material cost
Durability

Corrosion resistance: zinc spray vs designed-in protection

The standard accelerated test for brazed aluminium heat exchangers is SWAAT, the sea-water acetic acid test of ASTM G85 Annex A3. Cores are cycled in an acidified synthetic sea-salt fog, and examined for fin detachment, tube perforation and leaks at intervals.

Zinc arc spray on extruded tube works by diffusing into the surface during brazing and creating a zinc-rich sacrificial layer. Its weaknesses are coverage and consumption: the spray weight varies across the tube, extra zinc is applied to guarantee full coverage, and once the zinc-rich zone and the fin joints corrode, attack can reach the base tube and the tube-to-header joint.

Folded tube relies on layered alloy design instead. Supplier-published SWAAT results report folded-tube cores remaining leak-free after 100 days, while zinc-sprayed extruded cores in comparative tests lost fins and developed leaks or tube perforation much earlier. These are manufacturer data rather than independent studies, so treat them as a reason to test, not as a guarantee.

Corrosion factorExtruded + zinc sprayFolded clad tube
Protection mechanismZinc-diffused sacrificial surfaceBrown band, clad layers and potential gradient
UniformityDepends on spray process controlSet by rolling of the clad strip
Fin joint stabilityFins can detach as zinc layer is consumedClad-formed fillets tend to stay attached longer
Best environmentNormal inland serviceCoastal, humid, road-salt and polluted environments

Whatever tube you choose, the fin alloy, header material and flux residue all affect corrosion. Qualify the complete brazed core, not the tube alone.

Performance

Heat transfer and refrigerant-side pressure drop

Thinner walls and thin internal webs give folded tube a larger free-flow area and a smaller hydraulic diameter for the same outside dimensions. In comparative tests of equal-size cores, folded-tube heat exchangers have shown a few per cent to around ten per cent more heat rejection, with the largest gains at low air velocity. Performance retention after corrosion exposure also tends to be higher, because fins stay bonded longer.

Heat transfer is only half of the story. Refrigerant-side pressure drop depends on port count, port shape and the number of tube passes. Depending on the geometry, folded tube can show either higher or slightly lower pressure drop than an extruded tube of the same envelope. Higher pressure drop lowers the saturation temperature along the circuit and can cancel out a gain in heat transfer, so the tube must be matched to the circuiting, refrigerant and compressor.

Check capacity

Compare heat rejection at your design air velocity and refrigerant inlet conditions, not at a single catalogue point.

Check pressure drop

Measure refrigerant-side pressure drop at the design mass flux; re-optimise the pass arrangement if it rises.

Check after ageing

Retest capacity after a defined SWAAT exposure to see how much performance each design keeps.

Pressure

Pressure resistance and low-GWP refrigerants

Refrigerant transitions are raising pressure demands. R32 runs somewhat above R410A, and transcritical CO2 (R744) gas coolers operate at high-side pressures above 100 bar, so tubes need burst pressures several times higher. Extruded tubes handle this well because ports, webs and nose can be thickened locally where stress is highest.

Folded tubes can also reach high burst pressures; supplier data for brazed folded tubes report values in the region of 18 – 20 MPa. The weak points to qualify are the leading nose, whose thickness equals the strip gauge, and the brazed fold contacts that carry internal pressure. For CO2 systems, extruded tube with small round ports remains the conservative choice unless the folded design has been validated by burst, pressure-cycling and SWAAT tests in the brazed condition.

Sustainability

Material, refrigerant charge and recycling

  • Less metal: thinner-walled folded tube reduces aluminium per kilowatt of capacity, and therefore embodied carbon.
  • Lower charge: all microchannel heat exchangers hold much less refrigerant than round-tube-plate-fin coils, which matters for flammable A2L/A3 refrigerants and charge limits.
  • Mono-material: all-aluminium brazed cores avoid copper-aluminium separation and are simpler to recycle than mixed-metal coils.
  • Unclad fins: with folded tube supplying the braze metal, unclad fin stock can be used, which simplifies scrap recycling.
Cost

Cost: purchase price vs life-cycle value

Extruded tube usually wins on purchase price per kilogram. It is made by many producers on standard extrusion lines, and the zinc spray is a mature in-line process. Folded tube needs precise clad strip and high-speed roll-forming lines, and fewer companies make it.

The comparison changes when you look per heat exchanger rather than per kilogram. Folded tube uses less metal, may allow unclad fins, and can lower warranty costs if corrosion leaks are a known failure mode. Suppliers quote total heat-exchanger savings in the range of 5 – 20 % for some two-piece folded-tube designs, but this depends heavily on volume and on what the design replaces. Requalification, new tooling for headers and testing must also be counted.

FAQ

Folded and extruded microchannel tube: frequently asked questions

What is a folded microchannel tube?

It is a flat multiport tube roll-formed from thin clad aluminium strip, with internal webs formed by folding the strip or by inserting a separate inner fin. The folds are sealed by the braze clad when the heat exchanger is furnace brazed.

Why is extruded microchannel tube zinc-sprayed?

The lean extrusion alloys need a sacrificial surface layer. Zinc sprayed on the tube diffuses in during brazing and corrodes preferentially, protecting the tube wall from pitting.

Is folded tube always more corrosion resistant?

Not automatically. Its protection depends on the clad design and on the brazing cycle. Supplier SWAAT data are favourable, but the complete brazed core should be tested in your conditions.

Can folded tube be used with CO2 (R744)?

Possibly, but extruded tube with small round ports is the conservative choice for transcritical CO₂ gas coolers. A folded design must pass burst, pressure-cycling and corrosion tests in the brazed condition first.

Is folded aluminum tube cheaper than extruded tube?

Usually not per kilogram. It can be cheaper per heat exchanger because it uses less metal, can allow unclad fins and may reduce corrosion failures.

Can I switch an existing condenser from extruded to folded tube?

Not as a drop-in. Header slots, fin height, circuiting and brazing parameters usually need re-optimisation, followed by thermal and durability requalification.

Planning a microchannel heat exchanger?
