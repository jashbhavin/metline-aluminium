Anodising Aluminium: How It Works, Types, Alloys and Colours

Anodising grows a hard, transparent layer of aluminium oxide out of the metal itself by making the part the anode in an acid electrolyte. The result is a finish that cannot peel, resists corrosion and wear, and can be dyed in a wide range of colours. Its quality depends heavily on the alloy, the process type and the sealing step, so this guide covers all three, with practical advice for designers and buyers.

Basics

What is aluminium anodising?

Aluminium forms a natural oxide film only a few nanometres thick the moment it meets air. Anodising thickens that film in a controlled way, to anywhere from under 1 µm to more than 100 µm, and gives it a regular porous structure that can absorb dyes before being sealed.

Unlike paint or powder coating, an anodic film is not applied on top of the metal. It is converted from the surface aluminium, so it is integral to the part and cannot flake or chip away in the way a coating can. Roughly one third to one half of the final film thickness grows outward from the original surface and the rest penetrates into it, which matters when tolerances are tight: a 25 µm sulfuric film typically adds about 8 – 12 µm per surface, while a 50 µm hard anodic film adds about 25 µm per surface.

Why anodise?

  • Corrosion protection, especially in outdoor and architectural use
  • Hardness and wear resistance; hard anodising approaches the hardness of hardened tool steel at the surface
  • Decorative colour that will not peel, with a metallic appearance paint cannot match
  • Electrical insulation, since aluminium oxide is a dielectric
  • A good base for adhesives, primers and dry lubricants
  • No change to the recyclability of the aluminium
Principle

The electrochemical principle

The part is connected as the anode (positive electrode) in a tank of electrolyte, most often sulfuric acid, with lead or aluminium cathodes. When direct current flows, water is split: hydrogen is released at the cathode, while oxygen at the anode reacts with the aluminium surface to form aluminium oxide (Al2O3). At the same time the acid slowly dissolves the oxide, and the balance between growth and dissolution creates a dense barrier layer at the metal interface topped by a honeycomb of fine, parallel pores.

The pores, typically tens of nanometres in diameter, are what make anodising so versatile. They can be filled with organic dyes or metal salts for colour and are then closed by sealing, which hydrates the oxide and locks in the colour and corrosion resistance. Film thickness depends on current density and time; hardness and pore size depend on electrolyte, temperature and voltage.

  1. PretreatmentDegrease, then etch (often in sodium hydroxide) for a matte finish or chemically or electrolytically brighten for a bright finish; desmut in acid.
  2. AnodiseTreat in the electrolyte at controlled temperature, voltage and current density until the required thickness is reached.
  3. Colour (optional)Dip in organic dye, or electrolytically deposit tin or other metal salts in the pores for bronze-to-black tones.
  4. SealClose the pores in hot deionised water, mid-temperature nickel-based seals or cold seals.
  5. InspectMeasure film thickness by eddy current; check seal quality, colour and appearance.
Process types

Type I, Type II and Type III anodising

The US military specification MIL-A-8625 is the most widely used way to describe anodising worldwide. It divides coatings by process type and by class: Class 1 is undyed and Class 2 is dyed.

Architectural anodising in Europe is specified differently, by film class to EN ISO 7599 and the Qualanod quality label: classes 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 represent minimum average thickness in micrometres. Class 20 or 25 is normal for exterior work and class 25 for aggressive coastal or industrial atmospheres.

Alloy selection

Which aluminium alloys anodise well?

Alloying elements behave differently during anodising. Magnesium and small amounts of silicon in solution anodise cleanly; copper, high iron, high silicon and zinc either dissolve, discolour the film or leave particles that make it cloudy. The alloy therefore sets the limits of what any anodiser can achieve.

  • Order "anodising quality" material when appearance matters; it has tighter controls on chemistry and surface
  • Do not mix alloys or even different batches in one visible assembly if colour must match
  • Avoid joining dissimilar alloys before anodising; welds made with 4043 filler turn dark grey, while 5356 filler matches better
  • Remember that machined, rolled and extruded surfaces of the same alloy can anodise to slightly different tones
Colour

Anodised colours and how they are produced

Clear (natural)

The undyed film on 6063 or 5005 gives a silvery metallic finish. It is the most durable outdoors.

Electrolytic colour

Tin or other metal salts deposited at the base of the pores give champagne, bronze and black shades with excellent light-fastness; the standard for architectural colour.

Organic dyes

Almost any colour, including red, blue, gold and black, by immersing the unsealed film in dye. Some dyes fade under UV, so check outdoor suitability.

Integral or interference colour

Colour created by the film or by optical interference effects; used for special architectural finishes.

Exact colour matching is difficult because the final shade depends on alloy, surface texture, film thickness, dye concentration and sealing. Agree physical limit samples (a lightest and darkest acceptable panel) for any visible project. Surface texture changes the look too: an etched or bead-blasted surface gives a matte appearance, while brightened or polished surfaces give a mirror-like finish. For detail on black finishes see black anodised aluminium, and for troubleshooting see anodising colour defects.

Our view: choose anodising for metallic appearance, high-wear surfaces, thin dimensional build and parts that will be handled often; choose powder coating for broad colour choice, strong colour matching across mixed materials, and situations where on-site repair matters. Many architectural projects use both, anodised visible profiles and powder-coated secondary elements. See our anodising and coating selection guide.

Applications

Where anodised aluminium is used

  • Architecture: window, door and curtain-wall profiles, façade panels, sunshades, ceilings and handrails
  • Consumer electronics: phone and laptop housings, audio equipment, camera bodies
  • Appliances and furniture: control panels, trims, cabinet profiles, handles
  • Engineering: pneumatic cylinders, pistons, slides, gears, fixtures and anti-static workbench frames
  • Transport and aerospace: trims, interior parts, fittings and fatigue-critical components
  • Lighting and signage: reflectors, lamp bodies, nameplates and sign blanks
  • Outdoor and sports: tent poles, climbing hardware, bicycle components

FAQ

Anodising aluminium (aluminum): FAQs

What is the difference between Type II and Type III anodising?

Type II is conventional sulfuric acid anodising, typically up to about 25 µm, clear and easily dyed, used for decorative and protective finishes. Type III, hard anodising, is run colder at higher current density to produce thicker (commonly 50 µm), much harder films for wear resistance, with a naturally grey to bronze colour.

Does anodising change part dimensions?

Yes. Part of the film grows outward. Type II films typically add about one third of their thickness per surface, and hard anodic films about half; so a 50 µm hard coat increases a diameter by roughly 50 µm. Allow for this on tight-tolerance features or mask them.

Which aluminium alloy is best for anodising?

For decorative work, 6063 and 6060 extrusions and 5005 or 1xxx sheet give the most uniform, clear results. 6061, 6082 and 5052 also anodise well. 2xxx alloys and high-silicon castings give poor, discoloured films.

Is anodised aluminium suitable for outdoor use?

Yes. Properly sealed films of 20 – 25 µm in clear or electrolytic colours perform for decades outdoors. In coastal or industrial environments, specify 25 µm and clean the surface periodically.

Can anodised aluminium be welded or bent?

Welding destroys the film locally and the area must be re-anodised. Thin decorative films tolerate gentle bending, but tight bends crack the oxide; form parts before anodising wherever possible.

Why does anodised colour vary between parts?

Shade depends on alloy, temper, surface texture, film thickness, dye and sealing. Different batches or product forms (sheet versus extrusion) often anodise slightly differently, so use one alloy source and agreed limit samples where colour matters.
