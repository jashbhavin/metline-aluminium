Honeycomb Core Joining Methods and Their Effect on Panel Performance

A honeycomb sandwich panel is only as good as its joints. Adhesive bonding is the standard route for aluminium and aramid cores and suits most lightweight panels; brazing creates an all-metal aluminium panel with stronger skin-to-core joints, higher service temperature and non-combustible construction; welding and diffusion bonding are reserved for specialist high-temperature metal cores. The right choice depends on load, temperature, fire rules and cost, not on which process sounds strongest.

Background

Why the joining method deserves separate attention

In a sandwich panel the face sheets carry bending as tension and compression, and the core carries shear and keeps the faces apart. Every load therefore passes through two kinds of joint: the nodes where the cell walls of the core are joined to one another, and the skin-to-core interface where each face is attached to the cell edges. If either joint is weaker than the core itself, the panel fails early by node splitting, skin debonding or peel.

Joints also decide the panel’s service temperature, its fire behaviour, its resistance to moisture and corrosion, and how flat it stays. Two panels with identical 5052 core and 1 mm aluminium skins can perform very differently if one is bonded with a room-temperature adhesive and the other is cured with a structural epoxy film or brazed.

Inside the core

How the core itself is joined: expansion and corrugation

Most aluminium honeycomb is made by the expansion process. Lines of node adhesive are printed on aluminium foil (typically 3003, 5052 or 5056), the sheets are stacked with the lines staggered, and the stack is cured under heat and pressure into a solid block called HOBE (honeycomb before expansion). The block is sliced to thickness and pulled open to form hexagonal cells. Node adhesive quality, measured as node tensile strength, controls whether cells open evenly and how much shear the core carries in the L direction.

The corrugation process is used for high-density cores and for metals that cannot be expanded easily. Foil is corrugated first, then the corrugated sheets are stacked and joined by adhesive, brazing, resistance welding or diffusion bonding. Corrugated cores cost more but allow heavier foils and adhesive-free all-metal cores for high temperatures.

Buyers rarely specify node adhesive directly, but it is worth asking for node tensile results (for example to ASTM C363) when a panel will see high shear or elevated temperature.

Method 1

Adhesive bonding: the standard skin-to-core route

Adhesive bonding joins faces to core with a structural film adhesive, a two-part paste or a reactive hot-melt, cured in a press, autoclave or vacuum bag. It works with almost any face material: aluminium sheet, pre-painted coil, stainless steel, glass-fibre or carbon-fibre laminates, high-pressure laminate and stone veneer. That flexibility, together with mature processes and good flatness, is why most aluminium and aramid honeycomb panels are bonded.

Epoxy film adhesive

The aerospace and high-performance choice. A thin, supported film forms fillets up the cell walls, giving high peel and shear values. Cured at elevated temperature in a press or autoclave.

Two-part polyurethane or epoxy paste

Common in building, transport and furniture panels. Roller- or spray-applied, cures at room or moderate temperature; lower cost, lower temperature limit.

Reactive hot-melt and thermoplastic films

Fast continuous lamination for large volumes of cladding and flooring panels.

Surface preparation

Aluminium faces need degreasing and a conversion coating, anodising or primer for durable bonds. Poor preparation causes most long-term bond failures, especially in humid service.

Where adhesive bonding is limited

  • Service temperature is capped by the adhesive, typically far below the limit of the aluminium itself
  • Organic adhesive adds combustible content, which can restrict use under strict facade fire rules
  • Moisture and poor surface treatment can degrade the bond over years
  • Peel strength depends strongly on fillet formation, so adhesive weight and cure must be controlled
Method 2

Brazing: all-metal aluminium honeycomb panels

In a brazed aluminium honeycomb panel, the faces are aluminium brazing sheet: a core alloy such as 3003 clad with a thin layer of aluminium-silicon filler (4343 or 4045). The assembly is heated in a vacuum furnace or a controlled-atmosphere brazing (CAB) furnace to about 590 – 610 °C, just above the melting range of the cladding. The filler flows by capillary action and forms a metallic fillet at every cell edge, turning faces and core into one continuous metal structure.

PropertyAdhesive-bonded aluminium panelBrazed aluminium panel
Skin-to-core jointPolymer adhesive filletContinuous metallic fillet
Peel and flatwise tensionGood with film adhesive; depends on cure and preparationHigh; failure tends to move into the core
Service temperatureLimited by adhesiveLimited by the aluminium alloys, far higher
Fire behaviourContains organic adhesiveAll-metal, no organic content in the core joint
RecyclabilityMixed materialsSingle-metal panel, straightforward to recycle

Cost and sizeLower cost; very large panels possibleHigher cost; size limited by furnace

Alloy choice matters. Magnesium above roughly 0.5 % interferes with fluxed CAB brazing, so brazed cores are usually 3003 rather than 5052 or 5056; vacuum brazing tolerates more magnesium. Brazing temperature also anneals the parts, so a brazed panel’s strength comes from its geometry rather than from cold-worked tempers.

Method 3

Welding and diffusion bonding: specialist metal cores

Resistance welding, laser welding and diffusion bonding join cores made of stainless steel, titanium and nickel superalloys, which serve in turbine seals, exhaust systems and hot aerospace structures where no adhesive survives. These routes are slow and expensive and are rarely used for aluminium honeycomb, because aluminium’s oxide film, high conductivity and thin foils make spot welding difficult, and brazing already gives an all-metal joint.

Metline supplies aluminium honeycomb and aluminium face materials. Steel, titanium and superalloy cores are mentioned here only to explain where the joining routes differ; for aluminium panels the realistic choice is between adhesive bonding and brazing.

The failure mode is as informative as the number. A good bond fails by core shear or cell-wall tearing; a failure cleanly at the adhesive line, with bare face or bare cell edges, means preparation, adhesive quantity or cure needs attention.

Common mistakes

  • Specifying brazing for strength when the real limit is face buckling or core shear
  • Using 5052 core in a CAB-brazed design
  • Skimping on surface treatment of aluminium faces before bonding
  • Ignoring core splices and insert potting, where many panels actually fail

FAQ

Honeycomb core joining: frequently asked questions

What adhesive is used to bond aluminium honeycomb?

High-performance panels use epoxy film adhesive cured under heat and pressure. Building, transport and furniture panels often use two-part polyurethane or epoxy, or reactive hot-melt systems for fast lamination.

Is a brazed honeycomb panel stronger than a bonded one?

Its skin-to-core joint is usually stronger in peel and flatwise tension, and it keeps that strength at higher temperatures. Overall panel stiffness, however, depends mainly on face thickness and core height, so brazing does not automatically make a stiffer panel.

Why is 3003 used for brazed honeycomb instead of 5052?

Magnesium in 5052 and 5056 forms oxides that interfere with fluxed controlled-atmosphere brazing. 3003 brazes easily and is the usual core for brazed aluminum panels.

Can aluminium honeycomb be welded?

Rarely. Thin aluminium foil with its oxide film and high conductivity is hard to weld reliably, so aluminium cores are joined by adhesive or brazing. Welding is mainly used for steel, titanium and superalloy cores.

How do I check bond quality on honeycomb panels?

Use flatwise tension (ASTM C297) and climbing drum peel (ASTM D1781) tests on samples from the same lay-up, and inspect failure surfaces. A good bond fails in the core, not at the adhesive line.
