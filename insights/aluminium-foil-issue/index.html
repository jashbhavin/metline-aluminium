34 Aluminium Foil Defects and How to Identify Them

Most aluminium foil complaints trace back to a short list of defects: pinholes, oil residue, poor shape, coil winding faults and surface marks. This guide groups 34 common foil defects by where they come from, shows how to spot each one on incoming inspection and explains which ones actually matter for packaging, lamination, fin stock and battery foil.

Context

Why foil defects cost more than they look

Foil is aluminium rolled down to between roughly 6 and 200 µm. At those gauges a particle of oxide, a speck of dust in the roll gap or a slight tension imbalance leaves a visible mark, and a single bad lap can stop a converting line running at several hundred metres per minute.

The cost of a defect is rarely the foil itself. It is the downtime on a laminator, the scrapped printed web, the failed seal integrity test on a pharmaceutical blister or the leak in a brazed heat exchanger. That is why experienced buyers specify defects explicitly in their purchase terms instead of relying on "commercial quality".

Our view: most disputes come from vague specifications rather than bad foil. If the order says only "8011-O, 20 µm", the supplier and the converter will judge a borderline coil differently. A short defect table in the specification, aligned with EN 546 or ASTM B479, prevents most arguments before they start.

Four questions to ask about any defect

  • Does it affect barrier performance (pinholes, holes, cracks, inclusions)?
  • Does it affect running on the line (shape, adhesion, telescoping, loose winding)?
  • Does it affect bond or seal strength (oil residue, poor wettability, stains)?
  • Is it only cosmetic (stripes, bright lines, herringbone), and does the end use care?
Group 1

Surface and appearance defects

These defects show on the foil face. Many are cosmetic on plain household foil but become rejection causes on printed, lacquered or decorative foil, where the coating magnifies every variation in surface reflectivity.

A useful check for periodic defects: measure the spacing between repeats and compare it with the circumference of the rolls on the line. A match identifies which roll to clean or regrind.

Group 2

Oil residue, stains and wettability

Foil is rolled with a light mineral oil, which is driven off in the final annealing furnace. When that process is incomplete, coatings, lacquers, adhesives and inks do not wet the surface evenly, and bond strength drops.

#DefectHow to detect itWhy it matters
15Oil stainsPale yellow to brown spots, often near the coil edges or in the inner lapsWeak adhesion in lamination and printing, odour in food packs
16Incomplete degreasingWater or test-solution brush test: the liquid beads instead of forming a continuous filmThe main cause of poor lacquer and adhesive bonding
17Adhesion (sticking laps)Laps cling together; foil tears when unwound or hangs short in a free-hang testWeb breaks and stoppages on converting lines

The wettability brush test is described in EN 546-4. A brush dipped in a water and ethanol mixture of defined strength is drawn across the foil; if the stroke stays continuous, the surface meets the class tested. Converters who laminate or lacquer should state the required wettability class on the order rather than "clean surface".

Group 3

Pinholes, holes and cracks

Barrier defects are the most important group for food, pharmaceutical and battery packaging, because a single through-hole lets oxygen, moisture or electrolyte pass.

#DefectHow it looksTypical cause
18PinholesTiny holes visible against a light tableInclusions, gas porosity or dust in the roll gap; frequency rises steeply below about 9 µm
19HolesLarger perforations, sometimes torn edgesCoarse inclusions, foreign particles or mechanical damage
20Airway (gas streak)Crushed, band-shaped area along the rolling directionExcess hydrogen in the melt, inadequate degassing
21CracksLengthwise splits after rollingBrittle strip, coarse intermetallics or over-reduction
22Edge crackingNotches or splits along the edgesPoor edge trimming, edge thinning or worn slitter knives

Pinholes are tested on a light box in a darkened room; EN 546-4 defines pinholes, the porosity test and acceptance criteria. For thin household and packaging foil some pinholes are normal and accepted, which is why the gauge, the end use and the acceptance level must be agreed together.

Group 4

Shape and flatness defects

#DefectHow it looksEffect on the line
23Poor shape: edge waveWavy edges with a tight centreEdge wrinkles, poor tracking
24Poor shape: centre waveBaggy centre with tight edgesCreases in the middle of the web
25Poor shape: quarter (rib) waveWaves between centre and edgeLocal wrinkling, uneven lamination
26WrinklesSmall lengthwise or diagonal raised foldsVisible marks after lamination
27Creases (permanent folds)Folds that cannot be flattenedCracks and holes at the fold, reject for barrier use
28BucklesLengthwise ridges you can feel; may vanish after a few laps or run through the coilTension variation, poor winding

Shape is hard to judge in a wound coil. A simple test is to unwind several metres on a flat table under light tension and look for waves; for production control, mills use shape-meter rolls on the mill. Buyers can specify maximum wave height or require a flat-lay test on a sample from each coil.

Group 5

Winding, slitting and handling defects

#DefectHow it looksTypical cause
30Loose windingLaps shift when the coil is pushed along the core; finger pressure dents itToo little rewind tension
31Misaligned (uneven) end faceLaps stagger irregularly at the coil endTracking errors during slitting or rewinding
32Telescoping (tower shape)The coil end forms a cone, like a tower tipTension or core problems, a severe form of misalignment
33Burrs and end-face patternsRough edges, or radial streaks seen on the coil endWorn slitter knives, gauge variation across the width
34Impact and transit damageDented coil ends, crushed edges, flattened coilsPoor packing, dropping, inadequate support of the core

Swallow-tail or "arrowhead" patterns, where a group of laps protrudes progressively from the coil end, are a variant of misalignment and are judged the same way. Transit damage is the easiest to prevent: coils should travel on core supports or eye-to-sky on pallets, wrapped in moisture barrier with desiccant, and never be lifted by the outer laps.

Practical checklist

An incoming inspection routine for foil

  1. Check the packingLook for crushed cases, wet wrap and missing desiccant before unpacking. Photograph damage for the claim.
  2. Inspect the coil endCheck end-face alignment, telescoping, burrs and dents with the coil still on its core.
  3. Measure gauge and widthUse a micrometer or the weight method (mass per area divided by 2.71 g/cm³) and compare against the order tolerance (EN 546-3 or ASTM B479).
  4. Unwind and lookRemove the outer laps, unwind a few metres on a flat table and check shape, wrinkles and surface marks under good lighting.
  5. Run the wettability brush testEssential when the foil will be lacquered, printed or laminated.
  6. Check pinholesOn a light box, at the frequency agreed in the specification.
  7. Compare with the certificateCheck alloy, temper, mechanical properties and, for food contact, the declaration of compliance.
Our view

How to reduce defect risk when you buy foil

  • Specify the end use, not only the alloy and gauge. Lidding, blister, battery and fin foil each need different checks.
  • Name the standard and acceptance levels: EN 546 parts 1 to 4 or ASTM B479, plus pinhole, wettability and shape limits.
  • Choose the right alloy for thin gauges: 8079 and 8011 resist pinholes and tearing better than pure 1235 at the same thickness in many packaging uses.
  • Agree core size, coil OD and splice limits, because splices and core problems cause many line stoppages.
  • Ask for export packing with moisture barrier, desiccant and core supports for sea freight.
  • Keep retained samples from each coil so that complaints can be traced and resolved quickly.

FAQ

Aluminium foil defects: frequently asked questions

Are pinholes in aluminium foil a defect?

Below about 20 µm, a small number of pinholes is normal for rolled foil and is accepted within agreed limits. They become a defect when the count exceeds the limit set in the order or standard, or when the end use needs a full barrier, such as pharmaceutical blister or battery pouch foil.

How do I test aluminum foil for oil residue?

Use the brush (wettability) test described in EN 546-4: draw a brush wetted with a water and ethanol solution across the foil. A continuous film means the surface is clean enough for the class tested; beading shows residual rolling oil.

What causes foil coils to telescope?

Uneven or insufficient winding tension, a damaged or undersized core, or poor edge tracking during slitting. Telescoped coils are difficult to run and are usually rejected.

Can surface stripes be ignored?

For unprinted household or insulation foil they are usually cosmetic. For printed, lacquered or decorative foil, stripes, bright lines and herringbone marks often show through the coating and should be limited in the specification.

What should a foil complaint include?

Coil number from the label, photos of the defect and its position in the coil, retained samples, the test method used and the quantity affected. This lets the mill trace the coil and process history.

Buying foil for a demanding application?

Send the alloy, temper, gauge, width, end use and inspection criteria. We will reply with the matching foil and certification.
