Sacrificial Anodes: Zn-Al-Cd Zinc and Aluminium Alloy Anodes Compared

A sacrificial anode is a block of a more active metal, usually a zinc-aluminium-cadmium alloy, an aluminium-zinc-indium alloy or magnesium, that corrodes in place of the steel or aluminium structure it is connected to. Zinc anodes are the traditional choice for ships, ballast tanks and seawater cooling systems; aluminium anodes deliver more than twice the charge per kilogram and now dominate offshore work. This guide compares the alloys, their standards and how to size them.

Principle

How a sacrificial anode protects a structure

Corrosion is an electrochemical cell: metal dissolves at anodic sites and electrons are consumed at cathodic sites. Connect a metal that is more electrically negative than the structure, and the whole structure becomes the cathode. The anode dissolves instead, releasing a protective current through the electrolyte (seawater, brackish water or soil).

For steel in seawater, protection is normally achieved when the structure potential is shifted to −0.80 V or more negative against a silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) reference electrode. Aluminium structures are protected at around −0.95 to −1.10 V Ag/AgCl, and must not be driven too negative, because excess alkalinity at the surface attacks aluminium. That limit is why magnesium anodes are avoided on aluminium hulls in seawater.

A good anode alloy needs three things: a sufficiently negative working potential, a high and predictable current capacity (ampere-hours delivered per kilogram consumed), and uniform dissolution, so the surface stays active and corrosion products fall away rather than forming an insulating crust. Small alloy additions (aluminium and cadmium in zinc; zinc and indium in aluminium) exist precisely to keep the anode active.

Capacity and potential values are design values from DNV-RP-B401 for seawater service; manufacturers’ test values are often higher, but designs should use the code values.

Weight for weight, an aluminium anode delivers about 2.5 times the charge of a zinc anode, so the same protection needs far less anode mass. That saving matters on offshore jackets and long pipelines, and on weight-sensitive vessels. Zinc remains popular in ballast tanks, on ships that move between waters and in seawater cooling systems because of its long track record and tolerance of intermittent wetting.

Chemical composition

Zn-Al-Cd and Al-Zn-In anode chemistry (wt %)

Zinc anodes for seawater are made to ASTM B418 Type I or the near-identical US military specification MIL-DTL-18001. Some national standards allow slightly higher aluminium and cadmium contents, for example Al 0.3 – 0.6 % and Cd 0.05 – 0.12 %, which is the chemistry quoted on many Zn-Al-Cd datasheets. Iron is the critical impurity: above about 0.005 % it forms an insulating layer and the anode stops working.

Raw material purity drives performance. Zinc anodes are normally cast from special high grade zinc (99.99 % or better), and aluminium anodes from primary aluminium of at least 99.7 – 99.8 % purity, with indium and zinc added as master alloys. Cadmium-free zinc anodes (ASTM B418 Type II, high-purity zinc) are specified where cadmium is restricted, mainly in brackish water and for environmental reasons.

Shapes & fixing

Anode shapes and where each is used

Weld-on hull anodes

Long trapezoidal blocks with a steel strap or single/double foot welded to the hull. Distributed along the bilge keel, stern frame and around sea chests.

Bolt-on hull anodes

The same blocks with a drilled or slotted insert, bolted to studs. Used where hot work is restricted or for easy replacement at dry-dock.

Ballast and cargo tank anodes

Heavier tapered blocks on long inserts, positioned to protect tank floors and lower walls that are wet only when ballasted.

Port and jetty anodes

Large stand-off anodes on steel cores for sheet piles, piers, dolphins and mooring structures.

Seawater cooling system anodes

Bar and disc anodes fitted in sea chests, strainers, coolers and pipe headers to protect steel and copper-alloy components.

Pipeline anodes

Bracelet anodes for subsea pipelines; packaged zinc or magnesium anodes in backfill for buried pipelines.

Design

How anode mass is sized

The basic rule from DNV-RP-B401 and ISO 15589-2 is that the anodes must supply the mean current demand for the whole design life. The net anode mass is:

Anode mass calculation

Formula
M = (Imean × t × 8760) ÷ (u × ε)
Imean
Mean current demand in amperes = protected area × mean current density × coating breakdown factor
t
Design life in years (8760 hours per year)
u
Utilisation factor, typically 0.80 – 0.90 for stand-off anodes and lower for flush-mounted or bracelet types
ε
Electrochemical capacity in Ah/kg (780 for zinc, 2000 for Al-Zn-In in seawater ≤ 30 °C)

Worked example. A section of coated steel with a mean current demand of 2 A for 5 years needs 2 × 5 × 8760 ÷ (0.85 × 780) ≈ 132 kg of zinc anode, or 2 × 5 × 8760 ÷ (0.85 × 2000) ≈ 52 kg of aluminium anode. The designer then checks that the anodes can also deliver the initial and final current (which depends on anode surface area and shape) and spreads them to give uniform protection.

Aluminium structures

Protecting aluminium hulls and structures

Aluminium boats in 5083, 5086 and 5383 plate are naturally resistant to seawater, but any contact with stainless steel shafts, bronze propellers or steel fittings creates a galvanic couple, and stray currents in marinas can attack the hull. Anodes on an aluminium vessel protect both the hull and these dissimilar fittings.

  • Use aluminium (Al-Zn-In) or zinc anodes in seawater; never magnesium, which can over-protect and cause alkaline corrosion of the hull.
  • Insulate stainless and bronze fittings from the hull where possible, and bond them to the anode system where not.
  • Do not paint anodes; check them at every haul-out and replace at about 50 % consumption.
  • Avoid copper-based antifouling paints on aluminium hulls; use paints formulated for aluminium.
  • In fresh water, zinc passivates; magnesium or special aluminium alloys are used instead, with care on aluminium hulls.

FAQ

Sacrificial anodes: frequently asked questions

Are zinc or aluminum anodes better for a boat?

In seawater, aluminium (Al-Zn-In) anodes last longer per kilogram and also work in brackish water, so many boat builders now prefer them. Zinc anodes are equally effective in full-strength seawater. In fresh water, zinc stops working and magnesium is normally used, except on aluminium hulls where it can over-protect.

Why does a Zn-Al-Cd anode contain cadmium?

Small amounts of aluminium and cadmium refine the grain and stop the zinc forming a dense, insulating corrosion film, so the anode keeps dissolving evenly and delivering current. Cadmium-free high-purity zinc is an alternative where cadmium is restricted.

How long does a sacrificial anode last?

It depends on the current it supplies. Anodes are sized for a design life, typically 2 – 5 years on ships between dry-dockings and 20 – 30 years on offshore structures. Replace hull anodes when about half the material is consumed.

Can I use magnesium anodes on an aluminium hull?

Not in seawater. Magnesium drives the hull potential too negative, which produces alkalinity at the surface and can corrode aluminium and lift paint. Use aluminium or zinc anodes instead.

What happens if iron content in a zinc anode is too high?

Iron above about 0.005 % forms an insulating layer on the anode, which then stops delivering current even though it looks intact. This is why anode specifications cap iron so tightly and why chemistry is tested on every heat.

Does Metline supply zinc anodes?

Metline works only in aluminium. We can discuss aluminium-alloy anodes and the marine aluminium plate, pipe and extrusions they protect; zinc and magnesium anodes are covered on this page for comparison.
