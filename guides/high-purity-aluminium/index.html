High-Purity Aluminium: 4N, 4N5 and 5N Grades Explained

High-purity aluminium is metal refined to 99.99 % aluminium (4N) or better, with iron, silicon, copper and other impurities held to a few parts per million. It is chosen where impurities would spoil electrical, optical or semiconductor performance: capacitor foil, sputtering targets, bonding wire, reflectors and cryogenic conductors. This guide covers the grades, how purity is made and measured, and how to specify it; Metline supplies high-purity aluminium in ingot, plate, sheet, foil, disc and wire form worldwide.

Overview

What counts as high-purity aluminium?

Ordinary primary aluminium from a smelter is about 99.7 % pure. Commercial "pure" wrought grades such as 1050 and 1070 are 99.5 % and 99.7 %. High-purity aluminium starts at 99.99 %, which means the sum of the measured impurities is no more than 100 parts per million.

Purity is usually written with the "N" (nines) shorthand. 4N is 99.99 %, 4N5 is 99.995 %, 5N is 99.999 % (10 ppm of impurities) and 6N is 99.9999 % (1 ppm). Each extra nine cuts the allowed impurity by a factor of ten and needs an additional refining step, so price rises steeply with purity. The skill in buying high-purity aluminium is choosing the lowest grade that meets the real requirement, and specifying the impurities that actually matter for the application rather than only the headline number.

Removing impurities changes the metal in useful ways. Electrical and thermal conductivity rise, because dissolved iron, silicon, titanium, vanadium and chromium scatter electrons. Reflectivity and anodised brightness improve, because there are fewer intermetallic particles at the surface. The metal becomes softer and more ductile, recrystallises at lower temperature and develops a more controllable grain texture, which matters for capacitor foil and sputtering targets.

Why buyers specify high-purity aluminium

  • Low and stable impurity levels for predictable electrical and thermal behaviour
  • Clean microstructure for etching, anodising, sputtering and fine-wire drawing
  • Control of trace and radioactive elements for semiconductor processes
  • Lot-by-lot traceability with a certificate of analysis

Up to 4N the registered alloy designations apply. AA 1199 (EN AW-1199, Al99.99) sets maximum limits of a few thousandths of a percent for each of silicon, iron, copper, magnesium and zinc, with even tighter limits for manganese, titanium and other elements. Above 4N there is no single public designation: the grade is defined by a purchase specification listing each impurity in ppm, the analytical method, and whether gases such as oxygen and carbon are counted.

Refining

How high-purity aluminium is produced

Smelter metal cannot be purified simply by electrolysing it again in the normal way. Three main refining processes are used, often in sequence.

  1. Three-layer electrolytic refiningMolten impure aluminium alloyed with copper forms the dense bottom anode layer, a fluoride electrolyte sits in the middle, and pure aluminium collects at the top as the cathode. Elements nobler than aluminium stay behind. This route (often called the Hoopes process) gives about 99.99 – 99.998 % aluminium.
  2. Fractional (segregation) crystallisationMolten metal is cooled slowly so that the first crystals to form are purer than the liquid. Collecting those crystals removes eutectic-forming impurities such as iron and silicon and can lift smelter metal to 4N or refined metal to 5N.
  3. Zone refiningA narrow molten zone is passed repeatedly along a bar, sweeping impurities to one end. Used for 5N5 and above, in small quantities, for research and cryogenic conductors.
  4. Casting and conversionRefined metal is cast into ingot or slab under clean conditions and then rolled, forged, drawn or machined, using tools and handling that avoid picking up iron or other contamination.

Peritectic impurities such as titanium, vanadium, zirconium and chromium behave differently from iron and silicon in crystallisation refining; they are often reduced by a boron treatment before refining. That is why a 4N certificate can show very different trace profiles depending on the route.

At cryogenic temperatures the gap widens dramatically. The residual resistivity ratio (RRR), the resistance at room temperature divided by that at 4.2 K, is only a few tens for commercial aluminium but can run into the thousands for 5N metal and far higher after zone refining. That is why 5N and 6N aluminium are used to stabilise superconducting magnets and in cryogenic heat links.

The low strength is the price of purity. High-purity aluminium is easy to scratch and dent, recrystallises even near room temperature after heavy cold work, and cannot be heat treated for strength. Parts are designed with that softness in mind, or the metal is used as a coating, conductor or feedstock rather than a structural member.

Buying advice

How to specify and verify purity

The most common problem with high-purity aluminium is a certificate that says "99.999 %" but does not say which elements were measured. Purity is normally calculated by difference: 100 % minus the sum of the listed impurities. If only ten elements are analysed, the headline number can look better than the metal really is.

  • Name the critical elements with ppm limits (for example Fe, Si, Cu, Ti, V, Cr, Zr, Ga, and U and Th for targets).
  • Name the method: optical emission or ICP-OES is adequate for 4N; glow-discharge mass spectrometry (GDMS) is the normal method for 5N and above.
  • State what is excluded: gases (O, N, C, H) are usually not counted in the purity figure unless the specification says so.
  • Specify grain size and texture where they matter, such as targets and anode foil.
  • Ask for clean packing: sealed bags, inert gas or vacuum packing and clean cases to stop surface contamination and oxidation.
  • Require traceability: one certificate of analysis per melt or lot, matched to the marking on each piece.

Common mistakes include specifying 5N where 4N would do (paying many times more for no benefit), machining high-purity blanks with steel tooling that leaves iron at the surface, and storing foil or targets in humid conditions where the surface oxide thickens.

FAQ

High-purity aluminium (aluminum): frequently asked questions

What is 4N aluminium?

4N aluminium is 99.99 % pure, with no more than 100 ppm of measured impurities in total. It corresponds to alloy 1199 (EN AW-1199) and is the usual grade for capacitor foil, bright reflectors and alloy feedstock.

What is the difference between 4N5 and 5N aluminium?

4N5 allows up to 50 ppm of impurities and 5N up to 10 ppm. 5N needs additional refining and GDMS analysis and is mainly used for sputtering targets and semiconductor processes.

Is 1050 or 1070 aluminium high purity?

Not in the technical sense. 1050 is 99.5 % and 1070 is 99.7 % aluminium. They are excellent for general conductive, reflective and chemical uses, but high-purity grades start at 99.99 %.

How is the purity of aluminium measured?

By analysing individual impurities and subtracting their sum from 100 %. Spark OES or ICP-OES is used for 4N; GDMS is used for 5N and above. Always check which elements were included.

Can high-purity aluminium be strengthened?

Only slightly, by cold work. It is not heat treatable, and cold-worked high-purity aluminium can soften again over time. If strength is needed, use a dilute alloy or a commercial grade.

Yes. Metline supplies 4N to 5N aluminium in ingot, plate, sheet, foil, disc and wire form worldwide, with a certificate of analysis for each lot and clean export packing.
