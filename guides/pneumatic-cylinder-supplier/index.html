Guide for cylinder manufacturers

Aluminium for Pneumatic Cylinder Manufacturers

Most industrial pneumatic cylinders are largely aluminium: the barrel, end covers, piston and guide or slide bodies are extruded, machined, forged or cast aluminium. Metline supplies that aluminium stock (barrel profiles and tubes, bar, plate, forgings and foundry alloy) to cylinder manufacturers, rebuilders and automation builders worldwide. This guide maps each ISO cylinder family to the aluminium parts it needs, the alloys and finishes used, and how to specify them.

Overview

What Metline supplies to the pneumatic cylinder industry

A pneumatic cylinder converts compressed air into straight-line motion. Its pressure envelope, normally rated to 10 bar (1.0 MPa) working pressure, is a tube closed by two end covers, with a piston and rod sliding inside. Because the pressures are moderate and weight, corrosion resistance and machinability matter, aluminium is the dominant material for the body parts.

Metline does not make or sell finished cylinders. We supply the aluminium that cylinder makers turn into cylinders: extruded profile barrels and round tubes with finished bores, extruded bar and profiles for pistons and guide bodies, plate for slide tables and manifolds, forgings for end covers on larger bores, and aluminium casting alloy for die-cast covers. The rest of the cylinder, such as the steel or stainless piston rod, tie rods, seals and magnets, comes from the manufacturer's other suppliers.

This page is written for engineers and buyers at cylinder manufacturers, OEMs that build their own actuators, and repair shops that remanufacture cylinders. It explains which parts are aluminium, which alloys and finishes are normal, and the dimensional standards that set the shape of those parts. For the barrel tube alone, see our detailed pneumatic cylinder tube guide.

Why cylinder makers use aluminium

  • Low moving mass and low installed weight on robots and machines
  • Extrusion produces complex barrel profiles with sensor slots and tie-rod channels in one piece
  • Hard anodising gives a hard, low-friction, corrosion-resistant bore surface
  • Fast machining of end covers, pistons and guide bodies keeps unit costs down
Standards

ISO cylinder standards and the aluminium they use

Cylinder standards fix mounting dimensions and bore sizes so that cylinders from different makers are interchangeable. They do not prescribe materials, but in practice each family has a typical construction.

ISO 15552 (current edition 2018) superseded ISO 6431 and the German VDMA 24562, so drawings that still quote the older numbers can be read as ISO 15552 for mounting dimensions. Within the ISO 15552 range, smaller and medium bores are usually built on a one-piece profile barrel, while the largest bores often use a round barrel held between end covers by four tie rods.

Design drivers

Cylinder features that shape the aluminium parts

Each option a cylinder maker offers changes the aluminium parts behind it. Knowing these links helps when drawing up a material specification or when a part has to be redesigned for a new variant.

Mounting style (foot, flange, trunnion, clevis)End covers need thread bosses and bolt patterns to ISO 15552; trunnion versions need a stronger mid-section or separate trunnion block machined from bar or plate
Adjustable air cushioningEnd covers carry cushion bores, needle-valve threads and extra seals, so wall sections and porosity control become more critical
Magnetic position sensingThe piston carries a magnet ring; the barrel profile needs sensor slots on one or more faces, and aluminium is chosen because it is non-magnetic

Through-rod or double-rod versionsBoth covers carry rod bearings and seals; gland housings are machined from 6061 or 6082 bar
Lock units and brakesAdditional housings, often machined aluminium, mounted on the rod-end cover
Long strokesBarrel straightness and tie-rod or profile stiffness become critical; support brackets may be needed
High temperature or washdown optionsSeal changes plus thicker, well-sealed anodic layers; some designs move to stainless parts

Common sourcing mistakes

  • Ordering barrel stock without a bore tolerance agreed with the seal supplier
  • Using 2011 free-machining bar for parts exposed to humid or washdown environments without anodising
  • Specifying die-cast covers where porosity can leak into cushion or port passages, instead of forged or machined covers
  • Ignoring straightness on long barrels and rodless profiles, which leads to seal wear and binding
  • Mixing ISO 15552 and older national-standard mounting patterns in the same product range

Barrels

What a cylinder barrel must achieve

The barrel is the most demanding aluminium part, because the piston seal runs on its bore for millions of cycles. The main requirements are:

  • Bore size and roundness: held to an ISO 286 tolerance class agreed with the seal supplier, commonly in the H8 – H11 range depending on the seal design
  • Bore surface finish: a smooth, consistent surface, achieved by precision extrusion with drawing, honing or roller burnishing
  • Straightness: controlled over the full length, especially for long-stroke and rodless cylinders
  • Hard anodised bore: a Type III hard anodic coating gives hardness and wear resistance; it is often finished to reduce friction
  • Wall strength: wall thickness sized for the working pressure with a safety margin for proof testing, usually at 1.5 times the working pressure
  • Profile features: sensor slots, tie-rod or screw channels and flat mounting faces, all produced in the extrusion

Barrel stock is usually supplied in mill lengths of several metres, or cut to stroke-dependent lengths with square, deburred ends. Bores are protected with caps and the tubes packed to avoid any contact damage. Full details of profile types, bore tolerances and finishing are in the cylinder tube guide.

In practice, designers apply a load ratio of roughly 50 – 70 % of theoretical force for dynamic loads, to leave margin for friction, acceleration and pressure drops. Larger bores also call for heavier end covers and stronger cover fixings, which is why the largest ISO 15552 bores often move from profile barrels to tie-rod construction.

Forces are calculated from geometry: F (N) = p (bar) × 0.1 × π × D² ÷ 4, with D in millimetres.

Other designs

Aluminium in guided, compact and rodless cylinders

Compact cylinders

The body is a short square extrusion that contains the bore and the mounting threads. Profile accuracy and bore finish are critical, and the body is usually clear anodised outside and hard anodised in the bore.

Twin-rod and three-rod guided cylinders

A cylinder body plus a guide plate and bearing housings. The body is extruded or machined from 6061; the end plate is machined plate. Bearing bores must be accurately aligned to avoid binding under side load.

Frame-guided and slide tables

A machined aluminium table runs on linear guides over a cylinder body. Flatness of the table and the body mounting faces is the key requirement, so stress-relieved 6061-T651 plate or precision extrusions are used.

Magnetically coupled rodless cylinders

A smooth round barrel carries an external carriage coupled by magnets. The barrel needs a very consistent outside diameter as well as an accurate bore.

Slotted rodless cylinders

A long extrusion with a slot along its length, sealed by bands. These are some of the largest and most complex profiles in the industry, often in 6005A or 6063, and must stay straight over several metres.

Mini cylinders

Small ISO 6432 cylinders often use stainless barrels for durability; aluminium appears in the covers, pistons and mounting nuts, and some makers offer aluminium-barrel versions.

Across all of these designs the same pattern holds: extrusions carry the long, precise features such as bores, slots and guide channels, while bar, plate and forgings supply the compact, heavily machined parts such as covers, pistons, carriages and tables. Planning a product range around a small number of shared profiles and bar sizes simplifies material buying, shortens set-up times and keeps stock of semi-finished aluminium manageable for the cylinder maker. It also makes it easier to keep consistent alloy, temper and anodising quality across every bore size in a series.

Processing

Machining, anodising and corrosion protection

Aluminium cylinder parts are machined at high speed with sharp carbide tools. 6061-T6 and 6082-T6 give short chips and good finish on covers and pistons; 2011-T3 is preferred on automatic lathes for small turned parts because it breaks chips even more readily. Port threads (G, NPT or M) and mounting threads should be cut in well-supported material, away from thin profile walls.

FinishWhere it is usedNotes
Hard anodise (Type III)Barrel bores, compact bodies, piston surfacesHard, wear-resistant layer; specify to ISO 10074 or MIL-A-8625 Type III

Powder coat or paintCovers and accessories for washdown or outdoor useExtra protection and colour coding

For food and beverage plants, washdown equipment and marine automation, cylinder makers often move to stainless designs; where aluminium is retained, a thicker anodic layer, sealed properly, and 6061 or 6063 rather than copper-bearing 2011 gives the best corrosion behaviour.

Specification

How to specify aluminium for cylinder parts

  1. Name the part and the standardFor example: "barrel profile for ISO 15552 Ø63 cylinder, to our drawing". Include the cylinder standard so mounting dimensions are clear.
  2. Give alloy, temper and product standardE.g. 6063-T6 to EN 755-2 or ASTM B221 for extrusions; 6061-T651 to ASTM B209 for plate; 2011-T3 bar to ASTM B211.
  3. Define critical dimensionsBore size and tolerance class, wall thickness, straightness, cut length and end condition; for bar and plate, size and tolerance.
  4. State the finishMill finish for parts you will machine and anodise yourselves, or anodised barrel stock with specified layer thickness.
  5. Agree inspection and documentsDimensional reports for bore and straightness, EN 10204 3.1 mill certificates, and packing that protects bores and ends.

FAQ

Aluminium for pneumatic cylinders: frequently asked questions

Does Metline sell complete pneumatic cylinders?

No. Metline supplies the aluminium used to make cylinders, such as barrel profiles and tubes, bar, plate, forgings and casting alloy, to cylinder manufacturers and rebuilders. Finished cylinders are bought from cylinder makers.

What aluminium alloy is used for pneumatic cylinder barrels?

6063-T5 or T6 is most common because it extrudes complex profiles with good surface quality. 6061-T6 and 6005A-T6 are used where more strength is needed.

What are pneumatic cylinder end caps made of?

High-volume end covers are usually aluminium die castings such as A380 or ADC12. Low-volume and large-bore covers are machined from 6061 or 6082 bar or plate, or from forgings.

Which ISO standard replaced ISO 6431?

ISO 15552 replaced ISO 6431 and VDMA 24562 for standard cylinders with bores of 32 to 320 mm. Mounting dimensions remain interchangeable.

Why are cylinder bores hard anodised?

Hard anodising creates a hard, wear-resistant aluminium oxide layer on the bore, which lengthens seal and barrel life and protects against corrosion.

Can you supply barrel stock cut to length?

Yes. Barrel profiles and tubes can be supplied in mill lengths or cut to stroke lengths with deburred ends and bore protection, with mill certificates.

What force does an aluminum pneumatic cylinder produce?

Theoretical push force is pressure times piston area: a Ø63 mm cylinder at 6 bar gives about 1,870 N before friction. See the force table above for other bores.
