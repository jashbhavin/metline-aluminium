7075 Aircraft Aluminium: What Makes It Aerospace Grade

7075 has been an aircraft structural alloy since the 1940s because it combines steel-like strength with the low density of aluminium. But aerospace-grade 7075 is defined by more than its chemistry: the temper, the AMS specification for each product form, inspection and traceability are what separate it from commercial 7075. This guide explains each of these and how to buy 7075 for flight hardware.

Overview

Why 7075 became an aircraft alloy

Aircraft design is a constant trade between weight and strength. 7075-T6 offers a typical tensile strength of about 572 MPa and yield of about 503 MPa at a density of 2.81 g/cm³, roughly one-third that of steel. In specific strength (strength per unit weight) it beats many structural steels and competes with titanium in moderately loaded parts, at a fraction of the cost and machining time.

The alloy was developed in the early 1940s and quickly adopted for wartime aircraft. Since then, it has been used for wing and fuselage structure, fittings, landing-gear parts and countless machined components. Modern airframes often use newer alloys such as 7050, 7150 and 7085 for thick, damage-tolerance-critical parts, but 7075 remains one of the most used aerospace alloys, especially for sheet, extrusions, bar and smaller forgings.

The weakness of 7075 is just as important to understand. In the peak-aged T6 temper it is susceptible to stress-corrosion cracking (SCC) and exfoliation corrosion, particularly in the short-transverse direction of thick sections. That is why aerospace engineers specify over-aged T73 or T76 tempers for many parts, and why thin 7075 sheet for skins is usually Alclad.

What aerospace buyers actually choose between

  • 7075-T6 / T651: maximum strength; fine where SCC risk is low or the part is thin
  • 7075-T73 / T7351: best SCC resistance; about 10 – 15 % less strength
  • 7075-T76 / T7651: a compromise focused on exfoliation resistance
  • Alclad 7075: sheet with a thin pure-aluminium-zinc cladding for corrosion protection of skins
Aerospace grade

Commercial 7075 versus aerospace 7075

The alloy limits are the same in both cases. What differs is the specification the material is made to and the evidence that comes with it. A plate sold as "7075-T651 to ASTM B209" may be perfectly good material, but it is not acceptable for a part whose drawing calls up an AMS specification.

In practice, buyers of aerospace 7075 should check three things on the certificate: the exact specification and revision, the temper and product form it covers, and the test results it requires (including ultrasonic class where relevant). If any of these is missing, the material should not enter a flight-hardware supply chain.

Corrosion

Stress corrosion, exfoliation and Alclad protection

Most 7075 failures in service are not overload failures but corrosion-assisted ones. Two mechanisms matter. Stress-corrosion cracking is cracking under sustained tensile stress in a moist environment. In 7075-T6 it runs along the elongated grain boundaries, so it is worst when stress acts in the short-transverse direction, across the thickness of a plate or forging. Interference-fit bushings, clamped joints and residual stress from quenching or machining can all supply that stress without any external load.

Exfoliation is layered corrosion that lifts the surface in leaves, again following the flattened grain structure of rolled and extruded products. It shows up around fastener holes and cut edges where the end grain is exposed. The T76 temper was developed specifically to resist exfoliation while keeping more strength than T73.

Over-ageing works because it coarsens and spaces out the precipitates on grain boundaries and changes their electrochemistry, which interrupts the continuous anodic path along which cracks grow. The cost is strength. Aerospace specifications for T73 and T76 therefore include acceptance tests, typically a combination of electrical conductivity and tensile yield limits, to confirm the over-ageing has been done properly.

Protection methodHow it worksTypical use
Over-aged temper (T73, T76)Changes grain-boundary precipitates to resist SCC or exfoliationForgings, thick plate parts, fittings, skins
Alclad sheetThin anodic cladding corrodes sacrificially and protects the core, including at scratchesFuselage and wing skins
Anodising and conversion coatingBarrier film and paint baseMachined parts and extrusions
Primer, sealant and wet-installed fastenersExcludes moisture from joints and holesAll assembled structure
Shot peeningPuts the surface into compression, reducing crack initiationLugs, fittings and fatigue-critical parts

Note that Alclad reduces fatigue strength slightly, because the soft cladding is a weaker surface layer, and the cladding is removed wherever the part is machined. It is a sheet solution, not a cure for machined parts.

A typical design path for a machined fitting is to start with 7075-T7351 plate or bar, because the part will carry sustained loads near bolt holes. If the fitting is thicker than about 75 – 100 mm, or toughness governs, engineers usually move to 7050-T7451. For thin formed brackets, 7075-T6 sheet or extrusion is common, with anodising and primer for corrosion protection.

Working with 7075

Machining, forming, joining and finishing for flight parts

Machining

Excellent machinability in T6, T651 and T7351. Use stretched tempers for heavy pocketing to control distortion, and balance material removal on both sides of the plate.

Forming

Poor cold formability in T6. Severe forming is done in O or freshly quenched (W) condition, followed by ageing; this requires controlled processing.

Joining

Not fusion-weldable for structural use; joined by rivets, bolts and Hi-Lok type fasteners, sometimes with adhesive bonding. Friction stir welding is possible but needs qualification.

Surface treatment

Chromic, sulphuric, boric-sulphuric or thin-film anodising, chemical conversion coating, then primer and topcoat. Alclad skins rely on the cladding for galvanic protection.

Shot peening

Commonly applied to fatigue-critical parts such as lugs and fittings to add compressive surface stress.

Heat exposure

Avoid unplanned heating above about 120 °C; it can over-age the alloy and reduce strength. Conductivity checks reveal heat damage.

Ordering

Buying 7075 for aerospace: a checklist

  1. Specification and revisionQuote the exact AMS, AMS-QQ or customer specification, including the revision letter.
  2. Form, temper and sizeFor example 7075-T7351 plate, 50 mm, or 7075-T73511 extrusion to drawing.
  3. InspectionUltrasonic class, conductivity, grain direction marking and any first-article requirements.
  4. Cutting and traceabilityCut pieces should carry the heat or lot number; ask for re-marking on every piece.
  5. DocumentsEN 10204 3.1 mill certificate with chemistry, mechanical and other required test results, plus any certificate of conformity your quality system requires.

Common mistakes

  • Using T6 or T651 in thick parts with sustained short-transverse stress, instead of T7351
  • Accepting ASTM-only certification for a part that calls up an AMS specification
  • Losing traceability when plate is cut into blanks without transferring lot numbers
  • Forming 7075-T6 cold to tight radii, which causes cracking

FAQ

7075 aircraft aluminium (aluminum): FAQs

Is all 7075 aircraft grade?

No. The chemistry is the same, but aircraft-grade 7075 is produced, tested and certified to an aerospace specification such as AMS-QQ-A-250/12 or AMS 4078, with the required tests and traceability. Commercial 7075 certified only to ASTM is not acceptable where an AMS specification is called up.

What is the difference between 7075-T6 and 7075-T73?

T6 is peak-aged for maximum strength (about 572 MPa typical tensile). T73 is over-aged, giving about 505 MPa but far better resistance to stress-corrosion cracking. T73 is preferred for forgings and thick parts under sustained stress.

What is Alclad 7075?

Sheet or plate with a thin metallurgically bonded layer of a more anodic aluminium alloy on each face. The layer corrodes sacrificially to protect the 7075 core, which is why Alclad is used for aircraft skins.

Is 7075 stronger than 2024?

Yes in static strength: 7075-T6 is about 572 MPa typical tensile versus about 483 MPa for 2024-T3. 2024 has better fatigue-crack-growth resistance and damage tolerance, which is why it is preferred for lower wing skins and fuselage skins on many transport aircraft.

Can 7075 be welded for aircraft repairs?

Not by conventional fusion welding; it cracks and loses strength. Repairs use riveted or bolted doublers according to approved structural repair data.
