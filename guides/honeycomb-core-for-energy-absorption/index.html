Honeycomb Core for Energy Absorption

Crushed along its cell axis, aluminium honeycomb folds progressively at an almost constant force, turning impact energy into permanent deformation over up to about 70 – 75 % of its thickness. That flat, predictable force plateau is why it is used in crash-test barriers, truck-mounted attenuators, rail crash absorbers, landing systems and battery protection. This guide explains how to size an absorber, which core parameters set the crush strength, and what to specify.

Mechanism

How honeycomb absorbs energy

An ideal energy absorber stops a moving mass with the lowest possible peak force, which means applying a constant force over the longest possible distance. Aluminium honeycomb loaded along its cells (the T direction) comes remarkably close to that ideal: after an initial peak, the thin cell walls buckle and fold one layer at a time, and the load stays nearly flat until the folded material starts to stack up.

StageWhat happens in the coreForce behaviour
1. Elastic loadingCell walls compress elasticallyForce rises steeply
2. Initial peakFirst row of walls bucklesPeak typically well above the plateau on bare core
3. Progressive crushingWalls fold in a regular concertina patternNear-constant force: the crush strength
4. DensificationFolded layers stack solid at roughly 70 – 75 % strokeForce rises sharply again

The energy absorbed is the area under the force–displacement curve, so a flat plateau over a long stroke gives the most energy for the lowest peak deceleration. Because the deformation is plastic, almost none of the energy is returned as rebound, which protects occupants and payloads from a second impulse.

The initial peak in stage 2 is unwanted in most designs. It is removed by pre-crushing: the producer crushes a few millimetres off the block so that the folding pattern is already started. A pre-crushed block begins to crush at the plateau force from the first millimetre, which is why crash-barrier and attenuator specifications normally call for pre-crushed core.

Why aluminium honeycomb for absorbers

  • Constant crush force that can be set by choosing cell size, foil and alloy
  • Very high energy absorbed per kilogram compared with solid metal or foams
  • Predictable, repeatable behaviour that can be certified lot by lot
  • Non-combustible, does not age like polymer foams and is fully recyclable
Design maths

How to size a honeycomb energy absorber

Sizing comes down to three simple equations. Work them through for your case before choosing a core; they tell you the crush strength, area and thickness you need.

Basic sizing relations

Energy to absorb
E = ½ m v² (plus m g h for a drop)
Allowed crush force
F = m × amax, where amax is the permitted deceleration
Crush strength needed
σc = F / A, where A is the loaded core area
Core thickness needed
t = E / (σc × A × s), where s is usable stroke ratio, about 0.70 – 0.75

Worked example. A 1,000 kg mass hits at 8 m/s, so E = ½ × 1,000 × 8² = 32,000 J. If the deceleration must stay below 20 g, the force limit is 1,000 × 20 × 9.81 ≈ 196 kN. With a 0.30 m² absorber face, the crush strength needed is 196,000 / 0.30 ≈ 0.65 MPa (about 95 psi). Thickness is then 32,000 / (650,000 × 0.30 × 0.70) ≈ 0.23 m, so a block about 250 mm thick gives a safety margin on stroke.

Real designs add factors for the tolerance on crush strength, the higher crush force at impact speed compared with a slow test, off-axis loading and temperature. Final confirmation always comes from a dynamic test on the real absorber.

Core selection

What sets the crush strength of aluminium honeycomb

Crush strength is controlled by the same parameters that define any honeycomb: alloy, cell size and foil thickness, which together set density. For the same alloy, crush strength rises faster than density, roughly with density to the power 1.5 – 1.7, so small changes in foil gauge or cell size move the plateau force considerably.

ParameterTypical optionsEffect on crush behaviour
Foil alloy3003 (economical), 5052 (standard), 5056 (strongest)Higher-strength foil raises crush strength at the same density
Cell sizeAbout 3 mm to 19 mm across flatsSmaller cells give a smoother, more uniform plateau; larger cells for low strengths
Foil thicknessAbout 0.018 – 0.1 mmThicker foil raises density and crush strength
DensityFrom under 30 kg/m³ to around 190 kg/m³ in standard rangesBest single predictor of crush strength
Pre-crushBare or pre-crushedPre-crushing removes the initial peak
Load directionAlong cells (T) versus off-axisCrush strength and stroke fall as load angle grows

Producers certify energy-absorbing core to a nominal crush strength with a tolerance band, and barrier standards set tight limits. The US offset deformable barrier in 49 CFR Part 587, for example, specifies a main honeycomb block of 3003 aluminium with 0.342 MPa crush strength and a bumper element of 1.711 MPa, and the same two strength levels appear in European barrier specifications. Because lot-to-lot variation is real, ask for crush-strength certification on samples cut from the actual production block.

The Apollo lunar module landing gear famously used crushable aluminium honeycomb cartridges in its struts, and the same principle is used today in reusable launch-vehicle and lander concepts. In each case the absorber is a one-shot device: once crushed, the honeycomb is replaced.

Design

Design and integration tips

  • Keep the load axial. Guide the impact so it travels along the cells; off-axis loading lowers crush force and can make the block tip or shear.
  • Use pre-crushed core unless you deliberately need the initial peak, for example as a breakaway trigger.
  • Allow for rate effects. Dynamic crush force is somewhat higher than static; test at representative impact speed or use the producer's dynamic factor.
  • Do not count on the last quarter. Plan usable stroke at about 70 % of thickness; beyond that the core densifies and force rises sharply.
  • Back the core properly. Bond or mechanically retain blocks on a stiff aluminium plate so the reaction does not deform the mounting.
  • Protect against moisture with coated foil or sealed housings in outdoor attenuators and rail fronts.
  • Grade the stack with a softer first block and stiffer second block when the same absorber must handle both minor and severe impacts.

FAQ

Energy-absorbing aluminium (aluminum) honeycomb: FAQ

What is honeycomb crush strength?

It is the average stress on the loaded area while the core is crushing steadily along its cells, after the initial peak. It is the value used to size absorbers, and it is certified on samples from the production block.

How much of the thickness can be crushed?

Plan on about 70 – 75 % of the original thickness. After that the folded walls stack up and the force rises steeply.

What does pre-crushed honeycomb mean?

The producer crushes a small depth off the block so the folding pattern is already started. A pre-crushed block crushes at its plateau force from the start, with no initial peak load.

Which aluminium alloy is used for crash barriers?

3003 aluminium honeycomb is specified for the main block and bumper element of standard offset deformable barriers, for example in 49 CFR Part 587. 5052 and 5056 are used where higher strength per unit weight is needed.

Can honeycomb absorbers be reused?

No. Crushing is permanent plastic deformation. The crushed section must be replaced, which is why absorbers are usually designed as replaceable cartridges or blocks.

How do I choose between a higher crush strength and a thicker block?

The deceleration limit sets the maximum force, which with the available area fixes crush strength. Thickness then follows from the energy. If space is short, increase area rather than crush strength, so the force stays within the limit.
