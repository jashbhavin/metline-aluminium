Copper Aluminium Bimetallic Washers

A copper aluminium bimetallic washer (often called a Cu-Al or cupal washer) is a flat transition piece with solid copper on one face and solid aluminium on the other, metallurgically bonded in between. Placed in a bolted joint with the copper side against the copper conductor and the aluminium side against the aluminium conductor, it removes the direct copper-to-aluminium contact that causes galvanic corrosion, oxide build-up and overheating. This guide explains how the washers work, how to choose the construction, size and layer ratio, and how to install them so the joint stays cool for decades.

Overview

What is a copper aluminium bimetallic washer?

Copper and aluminium are the two conductor metals of the power industry, and they meet constantly: aluminium cable on copper switchgear terminals, aluminium busbars connected to copper transformer bushings, copper earth bars bonded to aluminium structures, aluminium battery busbars on copper-plated cell terminals. Bolting the two metals directly together is one of the most common causes of hot joints in electrical installations.

A bimetallic washer solves this by moving the copper-to-aluminium interface inside the washer, where it is a sealed, metallurgical bond rather than a mechanical contact exposed to air and moisture. Each outer face then forms a like-to-like joint: copper against copper, aluminium against aluminium. Those like-metal contacts behave predictably, and the bonded interface inside carries the current with very low, stable resistance.

The washers are stamped, laser-cut or machined from copper-clad aluminium sheet or strip produced by roll bonding or explosive bonding. They are made in round, square, rectangular and custom shapes, with one or several holes, to suit everything from M6 control-panel terminals to M20 bolts on high-current busbars.

In one sentence

  • A bimetallic washer turns one unreliable copper-aluminium contact into two reliable like-metal contacts plus a sealed, bonded interface.
The problem

Why direct copper-to-aluminium joints fail

A direct copper-aluminium joint may test perfectly on the day of installation and still fail months or years later. Four mechanisms act together, and each makes the others worse.

Galvanic corrosion

Copper and aluminium sit far apart in the galvanic series; in seawater the potential difference is roughly 0.5 – 0.6 V. When moisture bridges the contact, the joint becomes a battery and the aluminium, as the anode, corrodes. Corrosion products are bulky and non-conductive, so contact area shrinks and resistance rises.

Aluminium oxide

Aluminium forms a hard, insulating oxide film within seconds of exposure to air. Unless the film is broken during assembly and kept from reforming, current flows only through a few small metal-to-metal spots, which run hot.

Thermal expansion mismatch

Aluminium expands about 23 µm/m·°C and copper about 17 µm/m·°C, while steel bolts expand about 12 µm/m·°C. Each load cycle heats and cools the joint unevenly, so clamping force changes and the contact surfaces micro-slide (fretting).

Creep and relaxation

Soft, high-purity aluminium slowly creeps under bolt pressure, especially when warm. As it flows, bolt tension drops, contact pressure falls and resistance increases.

The result is a feedback loop: higher resistance makes more heat, heat accelerates oxidation, creep and corrosion, and those raise resistance further. Thermal imaging surveys of distribution boards routinely find copper-aluminium joints running tens of degrees above neighbouring connections for exactly this reason. In the worst cases the joint fails open or starts a fire.

Mechanism

How a bimetallic washer creates a transition connection

ZoneWhat happensEffect on the joint
Copper faceCopper layer contacts the copper conductorLike-metal contact with no galvanic couple
Bonded Cu-Al interfaceMetallurgical bond formed by roll or explosive bonding, sealed from air and moistureLow, stable interface resistance that cannot corrode from outside
Aluminium faceAluminium layer contacts the aluminium conductorLike-metal contact; only the normal oxide film needs managing
Clamped areaBolt load spreads across the full washer faceLarger effective contact area and more even pressure

The galvanic couple has not disappeared; it has been moved to the bonded interface inside the washer, where electrolyte cannot reach it. The washer edge is the only place the two metals meet the atmosphere, which is why edge quality and, in damp locations, edge sealing matter.

A bimetallic washer is not an insulating washer. Its purpose is to conduct current with low resistance, so it must sit in the current path between the two conductors, not under the bolt head.

For any joint carrying significant current or exposed to humidity, specify a clad washer. A plated washer can look identical on delivery; ask for a cross-section photograph or a layer-thickness measurement on the certificate to confirm which construction you are buying.

Layer ratio

Choosing the copper-to-aluminium layer ratio

The layer ratio is expressed as the percentage of total thickness that is copper. It controls cost, weight, how much copper is available for the contact face, and how much machining or cleaning the copper side can tolerate.

Ratio (Cu / Al by thickness)Copper layer in a 2 mm washerEffective densityWhen to use it
15 / 850.30 mm3.64 g/cm³Weight- and cost-sensitive joints in dry indoor service
20 / 800.40 mm3.95 g/cm³The common standard for busbar and terminal transitions
30 / 700.60 mm4.57 g/cm³Thicker copper face for rough mating surfaces, re-dressing or heavier clamping
50 / 501.00 mm5.82 g/cm³Special designs where copper-side robustness matters more than weight

Electrically, the washer is short and wide, so its own resistance is tiny compared with the two contact interfaces on either side. Changing the ratio therefore has little effect on joint resistance; the practical reasons to change it are mechanical robustness of the copper face, weight and cost. A 20/80 washer weighs well under half as much as a solid copper washer of the same size.

Shapes

Washer shapes and when each is used

Round flat washer

Central hole sized to the bolt, outer diameter sized to the terminal pad. The standard choice for single-bolt terminals on cable lugs, small busbars, switchgear and transformer studs.

Square washer

Covers more of a square terminal or bar end than a round washer of the same width, giving more contact area for the same footprint.

Rectangular transition plate

Matches the width of the busbar, with one, two or four holes in standard busbar drilling patterns. Common on transformer low-voltage terminals and high-current links.

Custom profile

Notched, stepped, slotted or offset-hole shapes made to drawing for confined spaces, replacement parts or non-standard terminals.

As a rule, the washer should cover the whole overlapping contact area of the two conductors. A small round washer on a wide busbar joint only transfers current through the area it covers, leaving the rest of the overlap unused.

Round washers in volume are normally stamped, which gives the best consistency once tooling is made. Square, rectangular and complex shapes, and short runs, can be laser-cut, waterjet-cut or machined, which avoids tooling cost but needs closer deburring. Laser cutting heats the edge, so the bond at the cut edge should be checked on first-off parts.

Manufacturing

How bimetallic washers are made

  1. Material preparationCopper and aluminium strip or plate are degreased and wire-brushed or chemically cleaned so the bonding surfaces are oxide-free.
  2. BondingRoll bonding presses the two metals together with heavy reduction (often more than 50 % in one pass) so fresh metal surfaces weld in the solid state. Explosive bonding uses a controlled detonation to drive one plate onto the other at high velocity, producing a wavy, very strong bond, and suits thick plate.
  3. Diffusion anneal and finishingA controlled heat treatment strengthens the bond and restores ductility, followed by rolling to final thickness and levelling. Time and temperature are limited to avoid thick, brittle intermetallic layers.
  4. FormingWashers are stamped, punched, laser-cut, waterjet-cut or machined from the clad sheet.
  5. Deburring and cleaningTumbling or brushing removes burrs; cleaning removes oil and metal particles.
  6. Inspection and packingDimensional, visual and edge checks, then packing in sealed bags to keep the faces clean and dry.
Environments

Requirements for dry, humid and outdoor service

EnvironmentRecommended constructionAdditional protection
Dry indoor (switchboards, panels)Clad washer; plated washer acceptable for light dutyClean surfaces and correct torque are usually enough
Humid, condensing (substations, basements, pump rooms)Clad washer onlyOxide-inhibiting joint compound on the aluminium face; consider edge sealing
Outdoor (transformers, poles, solar fields)Clad washer with sound edgesJoint compound, sealing tape or heat-shrink boots over the joint; stainless or plated fasteners
Marine and salt sprayClad washer, thicker copper layerFull sealing of the joint and washer edge; regular inspection
Chemically aggressive (plants, electrolysis, fertiliser)Clad washer, engineered for the siteSealing compounds or enclosures selected for the specific chemicals

Copper-plated aluminium washers should be avoided wherever condensation, rain or salt can reach the joint: once moisture finds a pore or scratch in the plating, the thin copper layer and aluminium form a galvanic cell under the plating, and the coating can lift.

Installation

How to install a copper aluminium bimetallic washer

  1. Identify the facesThe copper side is the reddish face, the aluminium side silver-grey. Copper faces copper; aluminium faces aluminium. A washer installed upside down makes the joint worse than having no washer.
  2. Clean the conductorsRemove oil, dirt and loose oxide. Wire-brush the aluminium contact surface immediately before assembly to break the oxide film, then apply a thin film of oxide-inhibiting compound if the design calls for it.
  3. Inspect the washerReject washers that are bent, scratched through the copper layer, or show any separation at the edge.
  4. Align and stackOrder the stack correctly: bolt head, flat steel washer, conductor, bimetallic washer, conductor, flat washer, spring washer (or conical disc spring), nut. Holes must line up so the washer seats flat.
  5. Torque to specificationTighten to the equipment maker's value for the bolt size and material. Conical spring washers help maintain clamping force as aluminium creeps and as the joint heats and cools.
  6. Protect the jointIn damp or outdoor locations seal the joint and the washer edge with compound, tape or boots.
  7. VerifyMeasure joint resistance with a micro-ohmmeter after assembly where specified, and include the joint in thermal-imaging checks after commissioning and at routine maintenance.

In practice these methods are combined. A transformer connection might use a copper-clad aluminium busbar on long runs, tin-plated aluminium where bars meet each other, and a bimetallic washer where a plain aluminium bar must meet an existing copper terminal.

Applications

Where bimetallic washers are used

  • Transformers and reactors: aluminium busbars or cable lugs on copper bushings and terminal studs.
  • Switchgear and distribution boards: aluminium cable lugs on copper busbars and breaker terminals.
  • Busbar trunking: aluminium trunking joined to copper switchboard busbars.
  • Earthing and bonding: copper earth bars to aluminium structures, cable trays and conductors.
  • Solar and wind: aluminium string cables and busbars in combiner boxes and inverters with copper terminals.
  • Battery and energy storage: aluminium busbars on copper or copper-plated terminals, where vibration and thermal cycling are severe.
  • Electrolysis and smelting: high-current copper-aluminium transitions in rectifier and cell connections.
  • Rail and traction: return-current and feeder connections between copper and aluminium conductors.
Pitfalls

Common mistakes with copper-aluminium transition joints

Washer under the bolt head

A bimetallic washer placed under the nut or bolt head, outside the current path, does nothing to protect the copper-aluminium contact between the conductors. It must sit between the two conductor faces.

Upside-down installation

With copper against aluminium on both faces, the joint now has two galvanic contacts. Mark the copper face on drawings and check it at inspection.

Undersized washer

A small round washer on a wide busbar overlap leaves most of the joint unused, so current crowds through a small area and the joint runs hot.

Plated washer in a damp location

Electroplated copper looks the same as clad copper on delivery but can fail quickly once condensation reaches a scratch or pore.

No allowance for creep

Flat washers alone let bolt tension fall as aluminium creeps. Use conical spring washers or the maker's recommended hardware and re-check torque where procedures require it.

Ordinary grease

Non-conductive grease insulates the contact. Use only compounds designed for electrical aluminium joints, applied as a thin film.

Most of these mistakes are invisible once the joint is assembled, which is why thermal imaging after the first weeks of load is so valuable. A transition joint that runs at the same temperature as its neighbours is working; one that runs noticeably warmer should be opened, cleaned and remade before it degrades further.

FAQ

Copper aluminium (aluminum) bimetallic washers: FAQs

Which side of a bimetallic washer faces the copper conductor?

The copper (reddish) side faces the copper conductor and the aluminium (silver) side faces the aluminium conductor. Installing it the other way round creates two copper-aluminium contacts instead of none.

Is a bimetallic washer the same as a copper-plated aluminium washer?

No. A bimetallic washer has a solid copper layer metallurgically bonded to aluminium, typically tenths of a millimetre thick. A plated washer has a thin electroplated coating that can be scratched through and is unsuitable for humid or outdoor joints.

Is 20 % copper / 80 % aluminium a fixed ratio?

No. 20/80 is the most common standard, but 15/85, 30/70 and 50/50 are also made. The ratio affects weight, cost and how robust the copper face is far more than it affects joint resistance.

Do I still need joint compound with a bimetallic washer?

In dry indoor service, clean surfaces and correct torque are usually enough. In humid, outdoor or marine locations, apply an oxide-inhibiting compound on the aluminium face and seal the joint edges.

Can a bimetallic washer be reused?

Only if it is flat, the copper face is intact and the edges show no separation. Because washers are inexpensive compared with the cost of a joint failure, most maintenance procedures replace them when a joint is remade.

Can washers be made from a sample or an old part?

Yes. A sample, a dimensioned photo or a drawing is enough to reproduce the shape; the copper layer thickness is confirmed by measuring a cross-section of the original where possible.

What size bimetallic washer do I need?

Match the hole to the bolt and make the washer large enough to cover the overlapping contact area of the two conductors. On wide busbar joints, use a rectangular transition plate drilled to the busbar pattern rather than small round washers.
