Braided Aluminium Wire: Flat and Tubular Braid

Braided aluminium wire is made by interlacing many fine aluminium-magnesium filaments into a flat strap or a hollow sleeve. Flat braid is used for flexible earthing straps, jumpers and braided busbars; tubular braid is used as an electromagnetic shield over cables and harnesses. Compared with copper braid it weighs roughly 70 % less for the same metal cross-section, at the cost of lower conductivity, so it suits weight-sensitive vehicles, vessels and energy-storage systems.

Overview

What braided aluminium wire is

A braiding machine winds fine wire filaments from a set of bobbins, called carriers, that travel around each other in opposite directions so the strands pass over and under one another. The result is a hollow, flexible sleeve. Left round, it is a tubular braid for shielding; pressed or woven flat, it becomes a flat braid that behaves like a highly flexible strap.

Braid construction is written as carriers – ends – filament gauge. A "24-5-36" braid, for example, uses 24 carriers, each laying 5 parallel filaments (ends) of 36 AWG wire, so it contains 120 filaments in total. The number and size of filaments set the metallic cross-section, and therefore the current capacity and weight; the braid angle and coverage set the flexibility and shielding performance.

Copper, tinned copper and silver-plated copper are the traditional braid materials. Aluminium braid has grown with vehicle electrification, lightweight aircraft and marine design and the steady pressure on copper prices, because aluminium gives the same flexibility and shielding function at much lower weight.

Where aluminium braid makes sense

  • Weight is a design constraint: EVs, aircraft, rail vehicles, boats and portable equipment
  • The circuit can tolerate a larger cross-section or slightly higher voltage drop than copper
  • Earthing, bonding, shielding and flexible links rather than precision low-resistance shunts
  • Mating parts are aluminium, so an aluminium braid avoids a copper-aluminium junction
Material

Why 5154 aluminium-magnesium alloy is used

Pure electrical aluminium such as 1350 conducts best (about 61 – 62 % IACS) but its fine filaments are soft and break easily on braiding machines and in repeated flexing. Most aluminium braid therefore uses 5154 or 5154A, an alloy with roughly 3.1 – 3.9 % magnesium. Magnesium strengthens the filament by solid-solution hardening and improves resistance to salt and humidity, while conductivity falls to about 32 % IACS.

Where conductance matters more than filament strength, some makers braid 1350 or other 1xxx alloys, or use copper-clad aluminium filaments that combine a copper surface for contact and plating with an aluminium core for weight. Each option is a balance between resistance, flex life and termination method.

Braided flexible strip

Raw flat braid supplied on reels or in cut lengths for further processing into straps, connectors or flexible busbars.

Flexible braided busbar

Wide, multi-layer flat braid pressed into a dense strap for high-current paths in battery packs, energy storage and traction equipment.

Braided connectors

Flat braid with crimped or pressure-welded lugs at both ends, linking busbars, motors and switchgear while taking up vibration and misalignment.

Earthing and bonding straps

Narrow braid with punched or crimped terminals for chassis grounding, enclosure bonding and equipotential connections on vehicles and vessels.

Tubular braid

Tubular braided aluminium wire for shielding

Tubular braid slides over a cable or wire bundle and is connected to earth at the ends, forming a conductive screen that intercepts electromagnetic interference. Optical coverage, meaning the share of the surface hidden by filaments, is the first quality measure; well-made aluminium braid typically reaches 85 – 90 % or more in normal sizes, with lower values on very small diameters.

A simple way to decide: if the circuit is limited by voltage drop or heating, or must solder to copper hardware, copper braid is the safe choice. If the goal is weight saving and the design can accommodate a larger cross-section and correct terminations, aluminium braid delivers a lighter assembly. For equal resistance, aluminium alloy braid needs about three times the copper area, which ends up only slightly lighter; for shielding and earthing, where resistance is rarely the limit, the weight saving approaches the full 70 %.

Design

Terminating and installing aluminium braid

Crimped lugs

Use lugs designed for aluminium conductors, filled with contact compound, and crimp tooling qualified for the braid area. Aluminium creeps under constant pressure, so the crimp geometry matters.

Pressure or ultrasonic welding

Consolidating the braid ends into a solid block by pressure or ultrasonic welding removes the gaps between filaments and gives a stable, low-resistance contact face for bolting.

Copper-aluminium joints

Where a braid meets copper busbars or terminals, use bimetallic lugs or copper-aluminium washers and keep the joint dry to prevent galvanic corrosion.

Bolted connections

Use flat washers and spring or conical washers to maintain clamp force, follow the torque value for the hardware size and re-check after thermal cycling where practical.

Bend radius

Keep bends generous and avoid sharp kinks at the lug barrel, where filaments break first under vibration. A strain-relief sleeve helps.

Plating

Tin or silver plating of terminal areas can be specified where a contact surface needs lower and more stable resistance.

Applications

Typical applications

  • Electric and conventional vehicles: flat braid earthing jumpers, engine and body straps, tubular braid over harness bundles
  • Battery packs and energy storage: braided flexible busbars between modules, pack grounding and auxiliary links
  • Marine and offshore: shielding over shipboard power and control cables, earthing straps in salt air
  • Rail and traction: flexible links in panels and across bogie or car-body joints
  • Data centres and telecom: bundle-level shielding sleeves and cabinet bonding straps
  • Aerospace and defence: lightweight bonding jumpers and shielding where every gram counts

FAQ

Braided aluminium (aluminum) wire: frequently asked questions

Is braided aluminium wire as good as copper braid?

For shielding, earthing and flexible links it performs the same function at about 70 % less weight for the same metal area. It conducts about a third as well as copper, so for current-carrying links you need a larger cross-section, and terminations must be designed for aluminium.

Why is 5154 alloy used rather than pure aluminium?

Fine filaments of pure aluminium are weak and break during braiding and flexing. The magnesium in 5154 raises filament strength and corrosion resistance; the trade-off is lower conductivity, about 32 % IACS.

What does a construction such as 24-5-36 mean?

It means 24 carriers, 5 ends (parallel filaments) per carrier, and 36 AWG filaments of about 0.127 mm. That gives 120 filaments and a metal area of about 1.5 mm².

Can aluminum braid be soldered?

Not easily, because of the tough oxide film. Aluminium braid is normally crimped with aluminium-rated lugs, pressure welded or ultrasonically welded at the ends.

How do I connect aluminium braid to copper busbars?

Use bimetallic copper-aluminium lugs or washers, apply contact compound, and keep the joint protected from moisture to avoid galvanic corrosion.

What shielding coverage can tubular aluminium braid achieve?

Standard sizes are commonly built to around 85 – 90 % optical coverage or more; very small diameters usually achieve less. Shield performance should be confirmed by transfer impedance testing.
