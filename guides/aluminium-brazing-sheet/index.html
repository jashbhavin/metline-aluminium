Aluminium Brazing Sheet & Clad Coil

Aluminium brazing sheet is a roll-bonded composite: a core alloy such as 3003 or 6951 clad on one or both sides with an aluminium-silicon filler alloy, and often with a zinc-bearing liner for corrosion protection. In the brazing furnace the cladding melts and joins every contact point in the assembly. This guide covers the heavier gauges used for headers, tanks, plates, side supports and battery cold plates: structures, alloys, properties, sizes and how to specify them.

Overview

What is aluminium brazing sheet?

Brazing sheet was developed so that a whole heat exchanger can be joined in one furnace cycle. Instead of adding filler at each joint, the filler is rolled onto the sheet itself. Parts are stamped, formed and assembled, then heated to about 600 °C; the cladding melts, runs into every joint by capillary action, and solidifies into fillets.

The core alloy melts at a higher temperature than the cladding, typically about 615 – 655 °C depending on alloy, so it stays solid and carries the load. The cladding, with 7 – 13 % silicon, begins to melt at 577 °C. That narrow gap between filler melting and core melting is why aluminium brazing needs accurate furnace control.

This page covers the thicker end of the range: sheet and coil from roughly 0.4 mm upwards, used for headers, manifolds, tanks, plates, side supports, flat-tube stock and cold plates. Thin fin foil has its own considerations, covered in our brazing foil guide; narrow slit material is covered under brazing strip.

Why heat exchanger makers use brazing sheet

  • One furnace pass joins hundreds or thousands of joints with uniform quality
  • Leak-tight, metallurgically continuous joints, suitable for pressurised refrigerant and coolant circuits
  • An all-aluminium core is light, recyclable and free of galvanic couples with other metals
  • Clad layers can be designed for brazing, corrosion protection or both

Manufacture

How brazing sheet is made

Brazing sheet starts as separate slabs. A thick core ingot is scalped, and thinner liner plates of filler alloy (and of 7072 where needed) are rolled to a precise thickness. The liner plates are laid on the core, strapped or welded at the edges, preheated and hot-rolled. The first passes press the layers together so hard that the oxide films break and the metals bond across the interface, forming a single composite slab.

The composite is then hot-rolled to coil and cold-rolled to final gauge, with intermediate and final anneals to set the temper. Because every layer is reduced in the same proportion, the clad ratio set by the starting plate thickness is carried through to the finished sheet. That is why clad ratio is specified as a percentage rather than as an absolute thickness: a 10 % clad 1.0 mm sheet carries about 0.1 mm of filler per clad face.

Edge trimming removes the zone where the cladding thins at the slab edges, and finished coils are slit or cut to length. Quality checks on the cross-section confirm that the clad layer is continuous and within tolerance across the width.

Properties

Mechanical and thermal properties before and after brazing

A brazing cycle at about 600 °C fully anneals non-heat-treatable cores. Whatever temper the sheet arrives in, a 3003 core ends up close to its O-temper properties after brazing, so design strength should be based on post-braze values. Heat-treatable 6951 cores are different: they are solution-treated by the brazing cycle and gain strength by natural or artificial ageing afterwards.

Common delivery tempers are O (for deep drawing and complex stamping), H24 (partially annealed, a balance of strength and elongation) and H14 (half-hard, for flat parts and better handling). Because brazing anneals the core anyway, choose the temper for how the part is formed, not for final strength.

Selection

How to select brazing sheet, step by step

  1. Identify faces that brazeOne face, both faces, or one braze face plus a coolant face. This sets single, double or three-layer structure.
  2. Match the brazing processCAB with standard flux needs a low-magnesium core and 4343 or 4045 cladding. Vacuum brazing uses Mg-bearing 4004 or 4104 cladding and tolerates 6951 cores.
  3. Plan corrosion protectionFor coolant circuits, a 7072 liner or zinc-bearing core makes the inner surface sacrificial and slows pitting through the wall.
  4. Size for post-braze strengthUse post-braze properties for burst and fatigue calculations. If 3003 is too weak, consider 3005, long-life cores or 6951.
  5. Set clad ratioThicker plates with larger joints often use 10 – 15 %; thinner tube stock uses less to limit erosion of the core.
  6. Choose temper for formingO for deep draws, H24 for moderate forming, H14 for flat or lightly formed parts.
Quality

Tolerances, testing and common mistakes

  • Thickness and flatness to EN 485-4 / ASTM B209 or tighter by agreement
  • Clad ratio per side with tolerance, measured on metallographic cross-sections
  • Chemistry of core and cladding on the EN 10204 3.1 mill certificate
  • Mechanical properties as delivered, plus post-braze tests if required
  • Brazeability, erosion and sag tests on request for critical parts
  • Surface cleanliness: low residual oil for consistent flux adhesion

Mistakes to avoid

  • Designing to delivered-temper strength when the core will be annealed in the furnace
  • Specifying a magnesium-rich core for a CAB line that uses standard potassium fluoroaluminate flux
  • Putting the cladding on the wrong face; mark the braze side on drawings and coils
  • Running long or overheated braze cycles that let silicon erode thin cores
Applications

Where brazing sheet is used

Automotive

Radiator and heater headers and tanks, condenser manifolds, evaporator plates, oil cooler plates, charge-air cooler end plates.

EV and battery

Liquid cold plates, battery module cooling plates, inverter and motor cooling jackets.

HVAC and heat pumps

Microchannel heat exchanger headers, plate heat exchangers and side plates.

Industrial

Plate-fin heat exchangers for compressors, hydraulics and gas processing.

Electronics

Vacuum-brazed cold plates for power electronics, radar and data-centre cooling.

Rail and off-highway

Radiators and oil coolers for locomotives, buses, construction and agricultural machines.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) brazing sheet: frequently asked questions

What is brazing sheet made of?

A core alloy, usually 3003 or a modified 3xxx alloy (or 6951 for higher strength), roll-bonded with an Al-Si filler such as 4343 or 4045 on one or both faces. Some structures add a 7072 zinc liner for corrosion protection.

Does brazing change the strength of the sheet?

Yes. The brazing cycle anneals non-heat-treatable cores, so a 3003-based sheet ends up close to O-temper strength whatever temper it was delivered in. A 6951 core can recover strength by ageing after brazing.

What clad ratio is normal?

Most brazing sheet has 5 – 15 % cladding per side. The right figure depends on joint size, sheet thickness and how much core erosion can be tolerated.

Which side of the sheet is clad?

Single-side clad sheet has filler on one face only. Suppliers mark the clad side; make sure drawings and press tooling keep the braze face where the joints are.

Can brazing sheet be welded or used without brazing?

It can be formed and even welded, but the cladding adds cost and offers no benefit unless the part is brazed. For unbrazed parts, plain 3003 or 5xxx sheet is usually the better buy.

Is brazing sheet suitable for vacuum brazing?

Yes, when clad with a magnesium-bearing filler such as 4004 or 4104. Standard 4343 and 4045 clad sheet is designed for flux-based CAB brazing.
