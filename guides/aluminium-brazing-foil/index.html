Aluminium Brazing Foil & Fin Stock for Heat Exchangers

Aluminium brazing foil is thin aluminium, usually 0.05 – 0.3 mm, with an aluminium-silicon filler layer roll-bonded to one or both sides of a higher-melting core. When the assembled heat exchanger is heated to about 600 °C, the cladding melts and brazes fins, tubes and plates together while the core stays solid. This guide explains clad structures, alloys, tempers, sizes and how to choose foil for radiators, condensers, evaporators and battery cold plates.

Overview

What is aluminium brazing foil?

Brazing foil is the thin-gauge end of the brazing-sheet family. A core alloy with a high melting point is clad on one or both sides with a filler alloy containing 7 – 12 % silicon, and the composite is rolled down to foil. It is then slit to narrow coils for fin mills, or cut and stamped into plates and separators.

The point of cladding is that the filler is already where it is needed. When a stacked or assembled core goes through a brazing furnace, the cladding melts at 577 – 615 °C, flows by capillary action to every contact point between fin and tube or plate and plate, and solidifies into fillets. Thousands of joints are made in one pass, with no filler wire and no manual work.

Not all heat exchanger foil is clad. In many designs only one side of each joint needs filler: for example, clad tubes with bare fins in radiators, or bare extruded multi-port tubes with clad fins in condensers. Choosing which component carries the filler is one of the first design decisions, and it determines whether you need clad brazing foil or bare fin stock.

Brazing foil vs bare fin stock

  • Clad brazing foil: carries filler; used when the mating part (often an extruded tube) is bare
  • Bare fin stock: no filler; used when the tube or plate is clad
  • Zinc-alloyed fin: bare or clad, made sacrificial to protect the tubes from corrosion
  • The choice affects price, fin formability and corrosion behaviour of the whole core

Structures are written filler / core / filler or liner, from one surface to the other. Clad ratio, the thickness of each clad layer as a percentage of the total, is specified per side.

Alloys

Filler and core alloys explained

Filler alloys are chosen for their melting range and flow. All melt above the aluminium-silicon eutectic at 577 °C; the higher the silicon, the narrower the melting range and the more fluid the filler.

3003 core

Al-Mn alloy, solidus about 640 °C. The standard core: good strength after brazing, easy to form, good corrosion resistance.

3102 core

Leaner Al-Mn alloy with higher thermal conductivity; common for condenser and air-conditioning fins.

3003 + Zn core

Zinc addition makes the fin electrochemically anodic, so it corrodes preferentially and protects tubes.

Modified long-life cores

Al-Mn cores with copper, titanium or controlled microstructure to resist silicon penetration, sagging and pitting through the tube wall.

6951 core

Al-Mg-Si heat-treatable alloy (solidus about 615 °C) that regains strength after brazing; used where fins or plates must carry pressure.

7072 liner

Al-1 % Zn sacrificial layer on the coolant side of tubes and plates; it is not a filler.

Tempers

Tempers, formability and sag resistance

Fin foil is corrugated or louvred at high speed and must then stay upright through the brazing furnace without collapsing. Temper controls both behaviours. Brazing foil is mostly supplied in strain-hardened tempers:

TemperConditionTypical use
H14Half-hard, rolled to final gaugeFins needing high stiffness for louvre forming; good sag resistance from a coarse recrystallised grain after brazing
H24Rolled harder, then partially annealedFins and plates that need more elongation for forming
H16 / H18Three-quarter hard / full hardThin fins where stiffness during handling matters
OFully annealedDeep-drawn plates and complex stampings, e.g. evaporator plates and cold plate halves

Sag is the drooping of thin fins at brazing temperature. Erosion or liquid-film migration is the attack of molten filler on the core, which can thin a fin or perforate a tube. Both are controlled through core chemistry, temper and grain structure, and a long time at peak temperature makes both worse. Specify sag and erosion tests if your design runs close to the limits.

Tolerances & tests

Tolerances, testing and documentation

Fin mills are sensitive to thickness variation and flatness, because they change fin height and pitch. Brazed cores are sensitive to clad ratio and cleanliness. A complete foil specification typically covers:

  • Thickness tolerance: tight, often a few micrometres on the thinnest fin gauges; agree the value on the order
  • Clad ratio: nominal percentage per side with a tolerance, measured on cross-sections
  • Width and camber: slit width tolerance and edge straightness for narrow fin coils
  • Mechanical properties: tensile strength, proof strength and elongation as delivered, and after a simulated braze cycle
  • Brazeability: test brazing (for example angle-on-coupon tests) and sag tests on request
  • Surface: residual oil level and cleanliness, important for flux adhesion
  • Certification: mill test certificate to EN 10204 3.1 with chemistry of core and cladding

Relevant product standards include EN 683 (aluminium finstock), EN 573-3 (chemical composition), EN 485 and ASTM B209 for sheet properties, and AWS A5.8 / ISO 17672 for filler alloy classification.

Process fit

Which brazing process suits which foil?

CAB (controlled-atmosphere brazing)

Nitrogen furnace with non-corrosive fluoride flux. Uses 4343 or 4045 cladding on low-magnesium cores. Most automotive and HVAC heat exchangers are made this way.

Vacuum brazing

No flux; magnesium in 4004 or 4104 cladding disrupts the oxide under vacuum. Used for oil coolers, aerospace and electronics cold plates and Mg-bearing cores.

Flux-free CAB

Newer clad systems allow brazing in nitrogen without flux. They need matched cladding chemistry and good atmosphere control; qualify carefully.

Torch and induction

Rarely used for foil, which is too thin to heat locally without burning through; relevant for thicker clad strip and repairs.

Flux choice is covered in our aluminium brazing flux guide.

Selection

How to choose a brazing foil, step by step

  1. Decide where the filler goesLook at each joint in the core. If tubes or plates are already clad, the fin can be bare; if tubes are bare extrusions, the fin must be clad. Double-clad sheet is needed wherever a part brazes on both faces.
  2. Choose the brazing processCAB with flux points to 4343 or 4045 cladding on a low-magnesium core. Vacuum brazing needs 4004 or 4104. Flux-free systems need their own matched cladding.
  3. Set the corrosion strategyDecide which component should corrode first. Zinc in the fin core, or a 7072 liner on the coolant side, makes that part sacrificial and protects the tube wall from perforation.
  4. Check post-braze strengthAfter brazing, a 3xxx core is essentially annealed. If fins or plates carry pressure or vibration, consider a long-life or heat-treatable core such as 6951.
  5. Match forming and temperLouvred fins need a temper that forms cleanly at speed and resists sag; deep-drawn plates need O or H24 elongation.
  6. Fix gauge, clad ratio and widthThinner fins save weight and cost but are more prone to erosion and sag. Confirm the clad ratio with test brazes on real parts.

Common mistakes when buying brazing foil

  • Specifying only "4343/3003" without clad ratio, temper or post-braze properties, which leaves too much open
  • Using a magnesium-bearing core in a CAB line with standard flux and then fighting poor joints
  • Switching suppliers without re-running sag, erosion and corrosion tests on the new material
  • Ignoring residual rolling oil, which hurts flux adhesion and causes patchy brazing
  • Choosing the thinnest fin gauge without checking erosion at your actual furnace cycle

One furnace pass brazes every fin crest, tube end and header joint at once, which is why consistent foil gauge and clad ratio matter so much on high-volume lines.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) brazing foil: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between brazing foil and normal aluminium foil?

Normal foil is a single alloy such as 1235 or 8011. Brazing foil is a composite with an aluminium-silicon filler layer bonded to a higher-melting core, so it can be joined in a brazing furnace without separate filler.

What does 4343/3003/7072 mean?

It describes a three-layer clad product: 4343 filler on one face, 3003 core in the middle and a 7072 zinc-bearing sacrificial liner on the other face. The liner protects the coolant side from pitting.

Should I choose 4343 or 4045 cladding?

4343 has a wider melting range and gives controlled fillets; it is the usual CAB choice. 4045 has more silicon, melts over a narrower range and flows further, which helps where joints are hard to fill or brazing cycles are short.

What thickness is typical for heat exchanger fins?

Most automotive and HVAC fins are between about 0.05 and 0.10 mm. Plates, headers and separators use thicker clad sheet and strip.

What clad ratio should I specify?

Typical clad ratios are 5 – 15 % per side. Thin fins usually use lower ratios to limit erosion; thicker plates and parts with large joint gaps use higher ones. Base the choice on joint design and brazing trials.

Can brazing foil be used for vacuum brazing?

Yes, but it needs a magnesium-bearing cladding such as 4004 or 4104. Standard 4343 or 4045 clad foil is designed for flux-based CAB brazing.
