Aluminium Substrate for CTP Printing Plates

Almost every offset printing plate in use today is a thin sheet of high-purity aluminium that has been grained, anodised and coated with a light- or heat-sensitive layer. The substrate is 1050, 1060 or 1070 in a hard H18 or H19 temper, typically 0.14 to 0.30 mm thick, with chemistry and surface held far tighter than ordinary sheet. This guide covers what plate makers specify and why, and Metline supplies litho-quality aluminium sheet and coil worldwide.

Overview

Why offset plates are made from aluminium

Offset lithography works because the image areas of the plate attract ink and the non-image areas attract water. Aluminium is ideal for the non-image areas: once its surface is roughened and anodised it holds a thin, even film of fountain solution, and the oxide layer is hard enough to survive hundreds of thousands of impressions.

Aluminium also bends around the plate cylinder without cracking, keeps its dimensions so colour separations register, is light enough to handle in large formats and is fully recyclable after the print run. Used plates are one of the cleanest aluminium scrap streams available, which is why plate makers increasingly ask about recycled content.

The substrate therefore has three jobs: respond evenly to electrochemical graining, form a defect-free anodic layer, and stay flat and strong after the coating is baked. Each of these depends on chemistry, grain structure and surface quality that ordinary commercial 1050 sheet does not guarantee.

Applications

Plate types that use an aluminium substrate

Conventional PS plate

Pre-sensitised plates exposed through film. Still used for newspaper and book printing; tolerant substrate requirements.

Thermal CTP

Imaged by an 830 nm infrared laser. Long run lengths, often baked to extend life further, so baking resistance matters.

Violet CTP

Photopolymer plates imaged by a 405 nm laser; popular in newspaper production for speed.

UV-CTP (CtCP)

Conventional-chemistry plates imaged directly by UV light, a lower-cost route for commercial printers.

Process-free plates

Develop on the press with ink and fountain solution. They demand an exceptionally uniform grained surface.

Other printing

Thin aluminium also serves as base for some gravure, screen-frame and flexographic applications.

Run length, imaging method and whether the plate will be baked all affect which alloy and temper a plate maker chooses. Newspaper plates are often the thinnest and least demanding; long-run commercial and packaging plates need higher strength and better heat resistance.

Surface

Why surface quality decides plate quality

Plate production begins with degreasing, then electrochemical graining in hydrochloric or nitric acid, desmutting, anodising and coating. Anything in the substrate that disturbs the graining shows up as a visible defect on the finished plate, and often as a print fault that only appears on press.

  • Streaks and bands: caused by uneven grain structure or rolling, they appear as dark or bright lines after graining and anodising.
  • Roll marks and pressure scratches: mechanical damage that the grain does not hide; they can pick up ink.
  • Inclusions and oxide: non-metallic particles from casting leave pits in the anodic film.
  • Oil residues: rolling oil burnt onto the surface during annealing resists cleaning and causes patchy graining.
  • Free silicon and coarse intermetallics: leave weak points in the oxide where ink and dirt collect in non-image areas.
  • Poor flatness: waves and edge buckle cause coating thickness variation and registration problems.

This is why litho substrate is cast with in-line degassing and filtration, rolled on dedicated work rolls with controlled roughness, and inspected over the full coil width. Plate makers usually qualify a substrate with a trial graining run before approving it for production.

A good substrate specification avoids disputes later and makes it possible to qualify a second source.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) printing plate substrate: FAQs

Which aluminium alloy is used for CTP printing plates?

Mostly 1050, 1050A, 1060 and 1070 in H18 or H19 temper, chosen for their excellent response to electrochemical graining. Stronger 1xxx variants with a small magnesium addition, or 3103, are used where the plate must be baked or run very long.

How thick is an offset printing plate?

Most are 0.14 to 0.30 mm. Small-format and newspaper plates sit at the thin end; large-format and packaging plates use 0.24 to 0.30 mm for stiffness.

Why must litho substrate have low copper and silicon?

Copper creates coarse pits during electrolytic graining and silicon particles leave defects in the anodic film. Both cause scumming or toning in non-image areas on press.

What is the difference between H18 and H19 substrate?

Both are fully hard, cold-rolled tempers. H19 has been rolled further and is stronger, which helps plates hold their shape and resist stretching on long runs.

Can you supply cut sheets for small plate lines?

Yes. Substrate can be supplied as slit coil or cut to sheet sizes such as 830 × 645 mm or 650 × 550 mm, interleaved and packed flat.

Is used printing plate aluminium recycled?

Yes. Used plates are a clean, high-purity scrap stream and are widely remelted, often back into litho or other 1xxx products.
