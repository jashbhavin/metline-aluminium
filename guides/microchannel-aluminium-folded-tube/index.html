Microchannel Aluminium Folded Tube

A folded microchannel tube is a flat, multi-port heat exchanger tube made by roll-forming and folding clad aluminium strip, instead of extruding it through a die. Because the strip is rolled, the wall can be thinner and stronger, the inner channels can carry thin fins, and the braze and corrosion layers are built into the material. This guide explains how folded tube is made, how it compares with extruded microchannel tube, which material structures are used and what to specify for condensers, evaporators and heat pumps. Metline supplies folded tube and the clad strip it is made from to heat exchanger builders worldwide.

Overview

What is a folded microchannel tube?

Microchannel (or multi-port) heat exchangers use flat aluminium tubes divided into many small parallel channels, brazed between louvred fins and connected by round headers. They hold far less refrigerant than round-tube-plate-fin coils, weigh less and are fully recyclable as a single metal.

Most microchannel tube is extruded: a billet is pushed through a porthole die that forms the outer wall and the internal webs in one pass. Folded tube takes a different route. A clad aluminium strip, a fraction of a millimetre thick, is fed through a sequence of forming rolls that bend it into a flat tube and, in many designs, fold part of it inward to make the webs. Two-piece designs instead insert a separately corrugated inner fin into a folded outer shell. The seam and the internal joints are closed during the same furnace brazing cycle that joins the coil.

The idea is not new. Folded "B-type" tubes have been used in automotive radiators and heater cores for many years. What has changed is the push for higher-pressure, lower-charge refrigerants and lighter coils in air conditioners, heat pumps and data-centre cooling, which plays to the strengths of rolled, clad material: a thin, strong wall with corrosion protection engineered through the thickness.

Manufacturing

How folded tube is produced

  1. Clad stripA multi-layer strip is hot-rolled from a core ingot with liner plates, then cold-rolled to final gauge and slit to the exact width the tube mill needs. Clad thickness on each side is controlled as a percentage of total gauge.
  2. Roll formingThe strip passes through a series of roll stands that progressively form the flat tube profile and, in one-piece designs, fold the centre to create internal webs.
  3. Inner fin insertion (two-piece)A thin corrugated fin strip is formed separately and inserted into the outer shell, dividing the bore into many small ports.
  4. Cutting to lengthTubes are cut to the exact coil length, cleanly and without crushing the ends, then stacked with fins and inserted into headers.
  5. BrazingThe whole core is brazed in a controlled-atmosphere (CAB) furnace with non-corrosive flux at around 600 °C. The braze clad melts, seals the tube seam and webs, and forms fillets to the fins and headers.

Because the tube remains unsealed until brazing, cleanliness and dimensional control of the strip are critical. A burr on the slit edge or an uneven clad layer can leave a channel unbonded, which shows up as a weak web or a leak at pressure test.

Materials

Clad material structures for folded tube

The tube strip is a laminated material. Each layer has a job, and the combination sets brazeability, strength and corrosion life.

LayerTypical alloy typePurpose
Braze clad (outside)4343 or 4045 Al-SiMelts in the furnace to bond fins and headers and close the seam
CoreLong-life 3xxx (Al-Mn with controlled Cu, Ti, sometimes Mg)Carries pressure; dense precipitate band resists pitting penetration
Sacrificial or inter-linerZn-bearing Al alloy (e.g. 7072 type) or modified 3xxxCorrodes preferentially and laterally, protecting the core
Inner fin3003-type or long-life 3xxx, bare or cladDivides channels, adds heat transfer area and supports the wall against pressure

Three-layer strip

Core plus braze clad on one or both sides. Gives good formability and brazing with long-life corrosion performance from the core chemistry.

Five-layer strip

Core with inter-liners and braze clads on both sides. The extra layers tune the corrosion potential through the wall and limit silicon diffusion into the core, raising post-braze strength and corrosion life.

Potential matching

The tube is made slightly more noble than the fins, and its outer layers more anodic than its core, so corrosion runs sideways along the surface instead of through the wall.

Extruded tube relies on a zinc arc-spray layer applied after extrusion to create the same sacrificial effect. With folded tube the protection is rolled in, so its thickness and zinc content are uniform along the tube and around the edges.

Mechanical properties

Typical strength before and after brazing

Tube strip is usually supplied in H24 or a similar partially annealed temper, hard enough to roll-form accurately but ductile enough to fold without cracking. Brazing anneals the material, so the number that matters for burst and fatigue design is the post-braze strength.

Higher post-braze strength is what lets the wall come down from the 0.25 – 0.40 mm common in extruded tube to around 0.18 – 0.26 mm in folded tube while keeping the same burst margin. Many internal fin-to-wall braze joints also act like tie-bars, holding the flat walls together under pressure.

Neither route is universally better. Extruded tube is flexible, quick to tool for a new profile and well proven in automotive and HVAC coils. Folded tube wins when wall thickness, material efficiency, corrosion life and high-pressure performance justify investment in roll tooling. Performance

Heat transfer, pressure drop and pressure rating

More, smaller ports and inner fins give the refrigerant more wetted surface, so a folded tube can transfer more heat than an extruded tube of the same outside size. Published comparisons on R134a condensers report capacity gains of a few per cent up to around 10 % at the same air flow. The price is often a higher refrigerant-side pressure drop at high mass flux, so the circuit (number of passes and tubes per pass) is usually re-balanced when switching tube types.

For pressure, the relevant tests are those of the equipment standard and refrigerant: strength or burst tests, pressure cycling and leak tests under UL 207 or UL 60335-2-40, EN 378 or ISO 5149 and the customer's own specification. Higher-pressure refrigerants such as R32, R410A replacements and especially R744 (CO2) raise the required burst pressure considerably, which is where the thin, strong, many-webbed folded tube is most useful. Always qualify the brazed coil, not just the tube, because the header joints and tube ends are part of the pressure boundary.

  • Corrosion testing: SWAAT (ASTM G85 Annex A3) is the usual accelerated test for brazed aluminium coils; results should be compared on complete coils with the same fin and header materials
  • Charge reduction: small internal volume is a major reason microchannel coils suit flammable A2L and A3 refrigerants such as R32 and R290, where charge limits apply
  • Drainage: evaporators with flat tubes need fin and tube orientation that sheds condensate, or frosting and water retention reduce performance
Applications

Where folded microchannel tube is used

Air-conditioner condensers

Residential and commercial outdoor units, where coil weight, refrigerant charge and corrosion life in coastal air all matter.

Heat pumps

Reversible coils working as both condenser and evaporator, increasingly with R32, R454B, R290 or R744.

Microchannel evaporators

Compact evaporators where fine ports and inner fins improve refrigerant distribution and support thin walls.

Data-centre and process cooling

Dry coolers, condensers and rear-door coils that run continuously and need long corrosion life.

Automotive thermal management

Radiators, condensers, heater cores and EV battery chillers, where folded tube has a long history.

Refrigeration

Commercial refrigeration condensers and gas coolers for CO2 systems.

Ordering

How to specify folded tube and common mistakes

  1. Define the tubeWidth, height, port count, wall and inner fin thickness, one-piece or two-piece, length and length tolerance.
  2. Define the dutyRefrigerant, design and test pressures, operating temperatures and expected corrosive environment (coastal, industrial, rural).
  3. Define the materialLayer structure (three- or five-layer), clad alloys and ratios, core chemistry family and supply temper; or ask for a proposal matched to your fins and headers.
  4. Define the brazing processCAB cycle, flux type and loading, peak temperature and time; these affect post-braze strength and silicon penetration.
  5. Documents and testingMaterial certificates for each coil of strip, dimensional reports and, where agreed, burst and SWAAT test data on sample coils.

Common mistakes. Changing fin alloy without re-checking corrosion potentials, so the tube becomes anodic to the fin and pits first. Copying an extruded-tube circuit unchanged, so pressure drop rises. Ignoring end deformation at cut-off, which causes header leaks. And comparing tubes on burst pressure alone, when fatigue under pressure cycling and corrosion after brazing decide field life.

FAQ

Microchannel aluminium (aluminum) folded tube: FAQ

What is the difference between folded and extruded microchannel tube?

Extruded tube is pushed through a die from a billet; folded tube is roll-formed from clad aluminium strip and sealed during brazing. Folded tube allows thinner, stronger walls, finer internal ports and built-in braze and sacrificial layers.

How thin can the wall of a folded microchannel tube be?

Typical walls are about 0.18 – 0.26 mm, compared with roughly 0.22 – 0.40 mm for extruded tube, with inner fins as thin as about 0.08 mm in two-piece designs.

Is folded tube suitable for R32, R290 or CO2 heat pumps?

Yes, it is used for these refrigerants, but the tube and the complete brazed coil must be qualified to the burst, cycling and leak requirements of the applicable standard for the specific refrigerant and pressure.

Why does folded tube resist corrosion better?

Its sacrificial zinc-bearing layer is rolled into the strip at uniform thickness, and the long-life core resists pitting, so corrosion spreads sideways instead of through the wall. Extruded tube depends on a sprayed zinc layer instead.

Does folded tube need extra braze filler?

Usually not. The Al-Si braze clad on the strip supplies filler for the seam, internal webs, fins and header joints; flux is still used in CAB brazing.

What does Metline supply for folded tube coils?

Folded tube to drawing and the clad tube strip, fin stock, header tube and brazing materials, with material certificates for each lot.
