Enamelled Wire (Magnet Wire): Types, Classes and Sizes

Enamelled wire, also called magnet wire or winding wire, is a metal conductor coated with thin layers of cured polymer insulation so it can be wound into the coils of motors, transformers, generators, relays and inductors. Choosing it means making three decisions: the conductor (aluminium, copper or copper-clad aluminium), the enamel chemistry that sets the thermal class, and the insulation build or grade. This guide explains each, with IEC dimension data for round aluminium wire.

Overview

What enamelled wire is and how it is made

The enamel on magnet wire is a film only a few hundredths of a millimetre thick, yet it must withstand hundreds or thousands of volts between turns, survive the abrasion and stretching of high-speed winding, resist impregnating varnishes and oils, and keep its properties for decades at the winding hot-spot temperature.

  1. DrawingElectrical-grade rod (for aluminium, usually 1350 EC grade) is drawn through a series of dies to the final conductor diameter or rolled to a rectangular section.
  2. AnnealingThe drawn wire is annealed in-line to restore softness and maximum conductivity, which also makes it easy to wind and form.
  3. EnamellingLiquid enamel is applied by dies or felts and cured in an oven; the cycle is repeated many times to build the film in thin, even layers, which avoids pinholes and solvent blisters.
  4. LubricationA thin surface lubricant lowers friction so the wire runs smoothly through winding machines.
  5. Spooling and testingWire is wound on spools under controlled tension and sampled for dimensional, electrical, thermal and mechanical tests.

Enamelled wire is classified by conductor material, shape (round, rectangular or square) and enamel system. This page focuses on the enamel systems and on round aluminium wire; rectangular wire is covered in our enamelled aluminium flat wire guide.

Conductor choice

Aluminium, copper-clad aluminium and copper conductors

ConductorConductivity (% IACS)Density (g/cm³)Best suited to
Aluminium (EC, annealed)About 61 – 622.70Transformers, reactors, motors and coils where weight and cost matter more than winding size
Copper-clad aluminium (CCA)About 62 – 67 (by copper share)About 3.3 – 3.6Fine and medium wires, high-frequency coils, speaker and electronic windings where light weight and copper-like terminations help
Copper (for comparison)About 100 – 1018.89The most compact windings where space is fixed

An aluminium winding needs about 1.6 times the conductor area of copper for the same resistance, so the wire diameter grows by about 27 %. In exchange the conductor weighs about half as much and the material cost is substantially lower. Aluminium wire is soft and ductile, so winding tension must be controlled to avoid stretching thin sizes. Terminations need methods suited to aluminium, such as welding, crimping with appropriate terminals or cold-pressure welding, because the oxide film prevents ordinary soldering without special fluxes.

Metline supplies enamelled aluminium and copper-clad aluminium winding wire. For coils that must be terminated to copper leads, use bimetallic terminals or welding methods made for aluminium.

Insulation chemistry

Enamel types and thermal classes

The thermal class (temperature index) is the temperature in °C at which the insulation reaches a defined life in accelerated ageing to IEC 60172. A higher class allows a hotter winding or a longer life at the same temperature. Choose a class at or above the hot-spot temperature of your design.

Dual-coat systems combine a base coat chosen for adhesion and thermal endurance with a tougher overcoat for abrasion, chemical and refrigerant resistance. Nylon (polyamide) overcoats are used on polyurethane and polyester wires to reduce friction and protect against winding damage.

Insulation build

Insulation grades and round wire dimensions

IEC grades describe the insulation build. Grade 1 is the thinnest film and leaves the most room for conductor; Grade 2 roughly doubles the build for higher breakdown voltage and robustness; Grade 3 is thicker again for demanding voltage or mechanical duty. NEMA uses the terms single, heavy, triple and quadruple build in the same sense. Quality

How enamelled wire is tested

IEC 60851 defines the test methods referenced by every IEC 60317 product sheet. The most important checks are:

Dimensions

Conductor diameter and overall diameter, which together confirm the insulation build.

Elongation and springback

Show the conductor is properly annealed and will wind and hold its shape.

Flexibility and adherence

The wire is stretched or wound on a mandrel and inspected for cracks or loss of adhesion.

Heat shock

Wound specimens are heated to a specified temperature; the film must not crack.

Cut-through

The temperature at which crossed wires under load short through the enamel, an indicator of thermoplastic flow.

Breakdown voltage

Measured on twisted pairs at room and elevated temperature.

Continuity

High-voltage counting of film faults (pinholes) along a length of wire.

Chemical resistance

Solvent, varnish, oil and refrigerant compatibility for the relevant grades.

Solderability

For polyurethane-type wires, the time to strip and tin in a solder bath at a set temperature.

Selection

Choosing the right enamelled wire

  • Start from the hot-spot temperature of the winding and add margin for overloads; that sets the minimum thermal class.
  • Check the environment: transformer oil favours acetal or compatible polyester systems; hermetic compressors need refrigerant-qualified 200-class wire.
  • Consider the drive: inverter-fed motors see voltage spikes; specify dual-coat or corona-resistant enamels and a heavier grade.
  • Match the termination method: polyurethane allows solder stripping on copper and CCA; plain aluminium wire is usually welded or crimped.
  • Pick the grade from voltage and winding stress: Grade 1 for low-voltage, carefully wound coils; Grade 2 or 3 for higher turn-to-turn voltage or aggressive winding.
  • Confirm varnish compatibility with your impregnation resin, especially for 180-class and higher systems.

Common mistakes

  • Specifying copper wire sizes directly for an aluminium winding
  • Choosing a thermal class without allowing for hot-spot rise above average winding temperature
  • Using single-coat wire on inverter-fed motors
  • Over-tensioning thin aluminium wire, which reduces its diameter and raises resistance

FAQ

Enamelled (enameled) wire: frequently asked questions

Is enamelled wire the same as magnet wire?

Yes. Magnet wire, winding wire and enamelled wire all describe conductors with a thin cured insulating film used to wind electromagnetic coils. "Enameled" is the US spelling.

What do Grade 1, 2 and 3 mean?

They describe insulation build under IEC 60317. Grade 1 is the thinnest film, Grade 2 about twice as thick and Grade 3 thicker again. Thicker grades give higher breakdown voltage and better damage resistance but take more winding space.

Which enamel is best for oil-filled transformers?

Polyvinyl acetal (formal) is the traditional choice because of its toughness and oil resistance; suitable polyester and dual-coat grades are also used. Always confirm compatibility with the specific oil and varnish.

Can enamelled aluminium wire be soldered?

Not easily. The aluminium oxide film resists ordinary solder, so aluminium windings are normally terminated by welding, crimping or cold-pressure welding. CCA wire with a solderable enamel behaves more like copper at terminations.

How much thicker is aluminium magnet wire than copper for the same resistance?

The conductor area must be about 1.6 times larger, which means a diameter about 27 % greater. The aluminium wire still weighs about half as much.

Which thermal class do I need for an inverter-driven motor?

Usually class 200 dual-coat or a corona-resistant enamel in a heavier grade, because fast-switching drives create voltage spikes that stress the turn insulation.
