Aluminium Honeycomb Core: How to Select It

Aluminium honeycomb core is a lightweight cellular material made from thin aluminium foil, bonded into hexagonal cells and sandwiched between two skins to make panels that are very stiff for their weight. Its performance is set by four choices: alloy (3003 for commercial panels, 5052 or 5056 for aerospace and demanding structures), cell size, foil gauge and the resulting density. This guide explains each, with typical strength data, coatings, configurations and supply forms.

Overview

What aluminium honeycomb core is and how it works

A sandwich panel behaves like an I-beam: the two skins carry bending as tension and compression, and the core holds them apart and carries shear between them. Doubling the core thickness roughly quadruples stiffness while adding very little weight. Honeycomb is the most efficient core because almost all of its material sits in thin vertical walls that resist crushing and shear, while the open cells weigh almost nothing.

Aluminium honeycomb is made from foil typically between about 18 and 100 µm thick. Strips of foil are printed with lines of adhesive, stacked and cured into a solid block, then sliced and pulled open (expanded) so the bonded lines become the cell nodes. The result is a hexagonal cell structure with double-thickness walls in one direction and single walls in the others.

Compared with foam or balsa cores, aluminium honeycomb offers higher shear strength and stiffness per unit weight, does not absorb moisture, does not burn, conducts heat and electricity, and crushes in a stable, predictable way, which makes it valuable for energy absorption as well as structure. Its main limitations are susceptibility to corrosion if unprotected, galvanic issues with carbon-fibre skins, and that it cannot be shaped as freely as foam without special cell geometries.

Where aluminium honeycomb core is used

  • Aircraft floors, control surfaces, fairings, nacelles and interior panels
  • Rail and metro car floors, walls, ceilings and doors
  • Building facades, curtain wall panels, partitions and clean-room walls
  • Marine bulkheads, decks and furniture; truck and trailer bodies
  • Crash absorbers, impact attenuators and packaging
  • Airflow straighteners, vent panels, lighting grilles and EMI vent panels

L, W and T directions, cell size and density

Honeycomb is anisotropic, so every specification refers to three directions and three geometric parameters. Getting them right on the drawing avoids the most common ordering errors.

TermMeaningWhy it matters
L (ribbon) directionParallel to the continuous foil ribbons and the double-wall bonded nodesStrongest and stiffest in shear; typically about 1.5–1.8 × the W-direction shear strength
W (transverse) directionPerpendicular to the ribbons, the direction of expansionWeaker in shear; easier to curve the core in this direction
T (thickness) directionCell depth, perpendicular to the skinsCompressive strength and crush strength act in T
Cell sizeDiameter of the inscribed circle of a cell, e.g. 1/8, 3/16, 1/4, 3/8 inSmaller cells give better skin support and finish, but more foil and cost
Foil gaugeFoil thickness, e.g. 0.0007, 0.001, 0.0015, 0.002 in (about 18–50 µm)Thicker foil raises density, strength and stiffness
DensityMass of the expanded core per volume, in pcf or kg/m³ (1 pcf = 16.02 kg/m³)The main driver of all mechanical properties

A standard way to describe aerospace core is cell size, alloy, foil gauge and density, for example 3/16-5052-.001-3.1: 3/16 in cells, 5052 foil, 0.001 in foil gauge, 3.1 pcf (about 50 kg/m³). For a regular hexagonal core, density rises roughly in proportion to foil thickness divided by cell size, so halving the cell size at the same foil gauge roughly doubles the density.

Always state whether a cell size is quoted in inches or millimetres, and whether a density is nominal or minimum. Standard density tolerance is commonly ±10 % of nominal.

Alloy selection

3003, 5052 and 5056 honeycomb: which alloy?

AlloyGradeStrength at equal densityCorrosion resistanceTypical uses
3003 (also 3104, 3105)CommercialLowestGoodFacades, partitions, doors, furniture, signage, transport interiors, airflow and lighting grilles
5052Aerospace / specificationHigher than 3003Very goodAircraft interiors and secondary structure, rail, marine, high-performance commercial panels

3003 commercial core

Al-Mn foil that forms and bonds easily at the lowest cost. Strength is adequate for most architectural and transport panels, where skins and adhesive usually govern. Often supplied in larger cells and thicker foil with a mill or lightly treated surface.

5052 core

Al-Mg foil with higher strength and better corrosion resistance than 3003. The most widely used specification-grade core, available in a broad range of cell sizes and densities and usually with a corrosion-inhibiting coating.

5056 core

Higher magnesium content than 5052, giving roughly 10–30 % higher shear and compressive strength at the same density. Chosen when the core is the critical element in an aerospace sandwich. AMS 4349 covers corrosion-inhibited 5056 core for service to 177 °C (350 °F).

Three patterns stand out. First, density is what matters: 1/8 and 3/16 in cores of the same density have almost identical properties. Second, L-direction shear is consistently about 1.5–1.8 times W-direction shear, so orient the ribbon along the main shear path. Third, 5056 is stronger than 5052 at equal density, particularly in compression and L shear, which is why it is chosen for structural aerospace parts.

Selection method

How to select cell size, foil and density

  1. Find the core shear stressFor a simply supported panel, the core carries roughly the shear force divided by the distance between skin centroids. Compare that stress with the core's W-direction shear strength (the weaker direction) unless the ribbon can be aligned with the load.
  2. Check flatwise compression and local loadsPoint loads, fasteners and foot traffic crush the core locally. Use higher density, potted inserts or local doublers where loads are concentrated.
  3. Choose cell size for skin supportThin skins can dimple into large cells under compression (intracell buckling) and show a "telegraphed" pattern on the surface. Thin skins and visible surfaces call for smaller cells, typically 1/8 to 3/16 in; thicker skins can use 1/4 to 3/8 in cells.
  4. Choose foil gauge to hit the densityAt a given cell size, foil gauge sets the density. Pick the lowest density that meets the strength and crushing checks.
  5. Pick alloy and coating3003 for commercial panels, 5052 for general specification work, 5056 for highest strength. Choose a corrosion-inhibiting coating for humid, marine or long-life service.
  6. Confirm thickness and formSlice thickness, block size, expanded or unexpanded, perforated or not, and any machining.

Common selection mistakes

  • Designing to L-direction shear values while the core is laid with W along the span
  • Using large cells under thin skins, causing dimpling and poor surface finish
  • Specifying unperforated core for a panel cured in an autoclave or exposed to altitude cycling where trapped air must vent, or perforated core where moisture ingress is a concern
  • Bonding aluminium core directly to carbon-fibre skins without an isolating glass ply or adhesive layer, inviting galvanic corrosion
  • Ignoring local crushing at inserts and edges

Corrosion protection and bonding surface treatments

Bare aluminium foil can corrode at the nodes in humid or salty environments, weakening the bond between cells. Specification-grade core therefore uses foil that is treated before it is formed into honeycomb.

Configurations

Hexagonal, overexpanded, flexible and reinforced core

Standard hexagonal

Regular hexagon cells. Highest shear strength for its density and the most common choice for flat panels.

Overexpanded (OX)

Expanded beyond regular hexagons into roughly rectangular cells. Lower L shear but higher W shear, and it curves easily in the W direction for cylindrical parts.

Flexible-cell core

A special cell shape that bends in both directions without anticlastic curvature, used for compound curves such as fairings and radomes.

Reinforced core

An extra flat foil sheet bonded along the nodes in the ribbon direction, raising density and strength for local hard points, insert areas and edge members.

Square or rectangular cell

Corrugated foil stacked with flat sheets. Used for airflow straighteners and specialised panels.

Slanted cell core

Cells angled at, for example, 30°, 45° or 60° to the face, used for directional airflow, light control and shielding.

Micro-cell core

Very small cells, around 1–3 mm, for smooth surfaces under very thin skins, precision panels and flow straightening.

Perforated core

Small holes in the cell walls that let air and gases escape during curing and allow pressure equalisation in flight.

Machined core

Blocks carved, tapered, chamfered or contoured by CNC to the shape of a part before bonding.

Working with core

Bonding, inserts, edges and machining

The core is only as good as the panel built around it. Most sandwich failures start at the skin-to-core bond, at inserts or at unsealed edges rather than in the honeycomb itself, so fabrication details deserve as much attention as core selection.

Skin-to-core bonding

Film adhesives are standard for aerospace panels because they form a controlled fillet up each cell wall; liquid epoxies and polyurethanes are common for architectural and transport panels. Clean, treated skins and adequate pressure during cure matter more than the adhesive brand. Flatwise tension (ASTM C297) and drum peel (ASTM D1781) tests verify the bond.

Inserts and hard points

Fasteners need potted inserts: the cells around the hole are filled with a structural potting compound before the insert is installed. Higher-density or reinforced core is used locally where loads are high, and edge members or extrusions take hinge and mounting loads.

Edge closeouts

Open edges let moisture into the core and leave the skins unsupported. Edges are sealed with potting, folded skins, bonded channels or extruded aluminium edge profiles, depending on the application.

Machining and cutting

Expanded core is cut with sharp knives, band saws with scalloped blades, waterjet or ultrasonic cutters. Contours and tapers are CNC machined with special honeycomb cutters while the core is held by vacuum, double-sided tape or a temporary filler.

Forming curved panels

Standard hexagonal core curves into a saddle shape when bent. Overexpanded core suits single curvature and flexible-cell core suits compound curvature; heat forming is also used on some grades.

Galvanic isolation

With carbon-fibre skins, add a glass-fibre ply or a continuous adhesive layer between skin and core so the aluminium never touches the carbon, and seal the panel edges against moisture.

Honeycomb also has a second life as an energy absorber. When crushed in the T direction it first reaches a peak stress, then collapses at an almost constant crush strength over most of its thickness. That flat force plateau, listed as crush strength in the tables above, lets engineers size crash pads, rail buffers, drop-test attenuators and packaging precisely. Pre-crushing the core removes the initial peak when a smooth force profile is needed. Because crush strength scales with density, the same cell size can be ordered in several foil gauges to tune the force level of an absorber without changing its outside dimensions.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) honeycomb core: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between 3003, 5052 and 5056 aluminum honeycomb core?

3003 is a commercial-grade core for architectural, transport and furniture panels. 5052 is stronger and more corrosion resistant and is the most common specification grade. 5056 has the highest strength at a given density and is used for structural aerospace sandwich parts.

What do L and W mean on honeycomb core?

L is the ribbon direction, parallel to the continuous foil strips and bonded nodes; W is the expansion direction at right angles to it. Shear strength in L is typically about 1.5–1.8 times that in W, so the L direction is aligned with the main shear load where possible.

How do I read a designation such as 3/16-5052-.001-3.1?

It means 3/16 in cell size, 5052 alloy foil, 0.001 in foil thickness and a nominal density of 3.1 lb/ft³ (about 50 kg/m³).

Should I choose perforated or unperforated core?

Perforated core lets air escape during curing and allows pressure equalisation, which is common in aerospace panels. Unperforated core keeps each cell sealed, which helps resist moisture migration. Choose according to the curing process and service environment.

What is unexpanded honeycomb core?

It is the bonded foil block or slice before it is pulled open into cells. It ships in a fraction of the volume of expanded core and is expanded by the panel maker to the required size.

Is aluminium honeycomb suitable for marine and outdoor use?

Yes, if it is a corrosion-inhibited 5052 or 5056 core with properly sealed panel edges. Untreated commercial core should be reserved for dry interior uses.

What temperature can aluminium honeycomb core withstand?

The aluminium itself tolerates high temperatures, but the node adhesive usually limits service. Common corrosion-inhibited aerospace cores are specified for use up to about 177 °C (350 °F); higher-temperature cores use different adhesives or brazed or welded constructions.
