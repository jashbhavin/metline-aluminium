CNC Aluminium Machining: From Material to Finished Part

CNC aluminium machining turns plate, bar, extrusion or forging into precision parts by computer-controlled milling, turning and drilling. Results depend as much on the material as on the machine: the right alloy, temper and stock form decide chip control, flatness after machining, surface finish and how the part anodises. This guide covers alloy selection, stock choice, cutting data, design rules, tolerances and finishing, so drawings and orders are right first time.

Overview

Why aluminium is the most machined metal

Aluminium alloys can be cut several times faster than steel, with lower cutting forces, longer tool life and less power. Combined with their low density, good corrosion resistance and wide choice of finishes, that makes aluminium the default material for machined housings, brackets, manifolds, fixtures and prototypes.

A CNC (computer numerical control) machine follows a programmed tool path generated from a 3D model, so the same part can be repeated with high accuracy from the first piece to the thousandth. For aluminium the main processes are milling, turning and drilling, often combined on multi-axis machines so that a part is finished in as few set-ups as possible.

Typical machined aluminium parts include machine frames and base plates, automation and robot components, semiconductor and vacuum chamber parts, hydraulic and pneumatic manifolds, electronics enclosures and heat sinks, aerospace fittings, medical device housings, vehicle and EV battery components, optical mounts and moulds for plastics and composites.

Processes

CNC processes used for aluminium

3-axis milling

The tool moves in X, Y and Z over a fixed part. Covers most prismatic parts (plates, brackets, pockets and holes) at the lowest cost per feature. Each face needing work requires a separate set-up.

4-axis milling

Adds rotation around one axis, so several faces or features around a cylinder can be machined in one clamping. Useful for manifolds, cams and parts with holes on several sides.

5-axis milling

Two rotary axes let the tool approach from almost any direction. Used for impellers, complex housings, aerospace structural parts and organic shapes; fewer set-ups improve accuracy between features.

CNC turning

The part rotates while a stationary tool cuts, producing shafts, bushes, flanges and other round parts. Operations include facing, profiling, grooving, threading, boring and knurling.

Mill-turn

Turning centres with live tooling mill flats, cross holes and slots in the same set-up, ideal for complex round parts from bar.

Drilling, tapping and boring

Hole-making is a large share of machining time. Thread milling and form taps work well in aluminium; precision bores are finished by boring or reaming.

Non-contact processes complement CNC machining: waterjet and laser cutting produce blanks and flat profiles from plate, and wire EDM cuts hardened inserts or very fine slots. Many parts start as a waterjet- or saw-cut blank to save material and machining time.

Strengths are typical published values for the temper shown, for comparison only; specified minimums depend on form and thickness.

As a rule: start with 6061-T651 (or 6082-T651 in Europe) unless there is a reason not to. Move to 7075 when strength or a very fine finish matters, 2011 for high-volume turned parts, 5083 for seawater exposure, and cast tooling plate when flatness after heavy machining is the priority. Avoid soft 1xxx and 3xxx alloys and O tempers for machining: they smear, form built-up edge and leave burrs.

Choosing the right stock form and temper

Stock formBest forTemper to ask forNotes
Rolled platePrismatic parts, base plates, large pocketsT651 / T7351 (stretched)Stretching relieves quench stress so plate stays flat as material is removed
Extruded or drawn barTurned parts, small blocksT6511 / T351 (extruded, stretched) or drawn T6Round, square, hex and flat bar reduce waste for near-size parts

Residual stress is the most common reason a machined aluminium part warps. Unstretched T6 plate or bar contains quench stresses; when one side is machined away, the part bows. Specifying stretched tempers (Tx51, Tx511) and machining symmetrically, with roughing, a rest period and light finishing passes, keeps parts flat.

Order stock with enough allowance for clean-up, typically 1 – 3 mm per face for sawn plate, and ask for a mill certificate so the alloy and temper are traceable.

Cutting parameters

Tools and cutting data for aluminium

Aluminium machines best with sharp tools, high spindle speeds and good chip evacuation. The values below are conservative starting points for solid carbide tools in 6061-T6 on a rigid machine; adjust for alloy, tool diameter, stick-out and machine power, and follow the tool maker’s recommendations.

OperationToolCutting speed (m/min)FeedNotes
End milling, roughing2 – 3 flute carbide, polished flutes300 – 800+0.05 – 0.20 mm/tooth (by diameter)High-efficiency toolpaths with moderate radial engagement
End milling, finishing2 – 3 flute carbide, sharp edge400 – 1000+0.03 – 0.10 mm/toothLight, constant stock for best surface finish
Face millingCarbide inserts with polished rake (or PCD)500 – 2000+0.05 – 0.20 mm/toothPCD for high-volume production
TurningUncoated or DLC carbide, positive rake300 – 9000.1 – 0.4 mm/revChip breakers sized for aluminium
DrillingCarbide drill, 130 – 140° point, polished flutes100 – 2500.05 – 0.30 mm/revThrough-coolant drills for deep holes

Indicative starting ranges only. Spindle speed limits on many machines cap the achievable cutting speed for small tools.

  • Coatings: uncoated polished carbide, DLC or ZrN coatings resist aluminium build-up; avoid TiAlN-type coatings, which contain aluminium and tend to stick
  • Coolant: flood or high-pressure coolant clears chips and prevents built-up edge; mist or air blast for light finishing
  • Chip evacuation: recutting chips damages finish and breaks tools; use fewer flutes for deep pockets and clear chips from holes
  • Workholding: aluminium is easily marked and distorted; use soft jaws, vacuum fixtures or controlled clamping force on thin parts

Chatter is the usual limit on productivity in aluminium rather than tool wear. Short, rigid tools, balanced tool holders and toolpaths that keep the radial load constant (trochoidal or adaptive clearing) let the machine run at its full spindle speed. Built-up edge, where aluminium welds onto the cutting edge, shows up as a torn surface and oversized features; sharper tools, higher speed, better lubrication and polished flutes cure it.

Burrs are the other recurring issue. Ductile tempers and soft alloys burr more; machining in T6 or T651 temper, climb milling, using sharp tools and adding chamfers on the drawing all reduce manual deburring, which can otherwise be a large share of part cost.

Tolerances drive cost more than any other drawing note. Apply tight tolerances only where parts mate or function depends on them and use a general tolerance standard elsewhere. Defining datums that reflect how the part is located in its assembly helps the machinist hold the important relationships rather than every dimension.

Remember that aluminium expands about 0.023 mm per metre for every degree Celsius. A 500 mm part measured 10 °C warmer than the reference temperature of 20 °C reads more than 0.1 mm long, so precision features must be machined and measured with temperature in mind.

Alloy choice affects anodised appearance: 6061 and 6063 give clear, even films; 7075 anodises well but slightly darker; 2xxx alloys with high copper produce less uniform, softer coatings and are harder to colour evenly. For cosmetic anodised parts, order all material for a batch from the same lot so the colour matches.

Coating thickness, colour and adhesion reports for anodised or coated parts

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using unstretched T6 plate for heavily machined parts, then fighting distortion
  • Applying a tight general tolerance to every dimension instead of only to functional features
  • Forgetting to allow for anodising build-up on fits and threads
  • Specifying sharp internal corners that need tiny tools and slow cutting
  • Mixing material lots on cosmetic anodised parts and getting colour variation

FAQ

CNC aluminium (aluminum) machining: frequently asked questions

What is the best aluminium alloy for CNC machining?

6061-T651 is the best all-round choice: good strength, very good machinability, weldable and anodises well. 7075-T651 is chosen for higher strength and finer finish, 2011-T3 for high-volume turned parts, and 5083 for seawater service.

Why do aluminium parts warp after machining?

Residual stresses from quenching are released as material is removed. Use stretched tempers such as T651 or T6511, machine symmetrically, rough then rest before finishing, and avoid heavy clamping on thin sections.

What tolerance can CNC machining hold in aluminium?

General features are commonly held to ISO 2768-m (around ±0.1 mm for small dimensions). Precision features can be held to ±0.01 – 0.025 mm, and tighter on selected features with controlled conditions.

Does anodising change part dimensions?

Yes. Roughly half of the anodic film grows outward from the original surface, so a 25 µm Type II film adds about 12 µm per face and hard anodising adds more. Allow for this on fits and threads or mask them.

What surface finish does CNC machining give?

Standard machined finish is around Ra 3.2 µm. Ra 1.6 and 0.8 µm are routine with finishing passes, and Ra 0.4 µm or better is possible on selected faces.

Can Metline supply both stock and machined parts?

Yes. Metline supplies aluminium plate, bar, block, extrusions and forgings, and can cut to size or machine parts to drawing on request, with mill certificates and inspection reports.
