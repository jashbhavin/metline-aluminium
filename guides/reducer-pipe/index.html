Aluminium Reducer Pipe & Tapered Tube

An aluminium reducer pipe is a tube whose outside diameter, wall thickness or cross-section changes along its length, either gradually as a taper or in steps at the ends. Engineers use it to put metal only where the load is, to join tubes of different sizes without a separate fitting, or to give a structure a cleaner aerodynamic or visual shape. This guide covers the main types, alloys, forming methods and how to specify them; Metline supplies reducer and tapered tube worldwide.

Overview

What a reducer tube is, and what it is not

In aluminium tube supply, "reducer pipe" usually means a variable-section tube produced from one piece: diameter, wall or shape changes along the length. It is different from a butt-weld reducer fitting, a short concentric or eccentric cone welded between two pipe sizes in a piping system. Both exist in aluminium; this guide focuses on variable-section tube, with fittings covered in our pipe fittings guide.

A one-piece variable-section tube avoids welds and their heat-affected zones, which in heat-treatable alloys can lose a third or more of the parent strength. It also avoids the stress concentration at a joint and the extra weight of sleeves and fasteners. That is why tapered aluminium tube appears in pantograph arms, antenna masts, lighting poles, sports equipment and aircraft and vehicle struts.

Types

Main types of aluminium reducer tube

Equal-wall tapered tube

Outside diameter tapers from one end to the other while the wall stays constant. Common in pantograph frames, masts and poles.

Stepped (double-ended) reducer

Constant section along most of the length, with reduced diameters at one or both ends to fit into bearings, bushes or adjoining tubes.

Inner-cone (variable-wall) tube

Outside size stays constant while the wall thins along the length, putting thickness where the bending moment is highest.

Oval or shaped taper

Elliptical or other non-circular sections that change in size, used for aerodynamic or stiffness reasons in rail and aircraft structures.

Multi-step antenna tube

Several diameters stepping down along the length so sections telescope or nest together, typical of antenna and mast elements.

Spun or tapered poles

Large tapered tubes for street lighting and flagpoles, formed from straight tube by spinning or tapering machines.

Manufacturing

How reducer and tapered tubes are made

MethodHow it worksBest for
Drawing over a tapered mandrelTube is drawn through a die over a shaped mandrel to reduce diameter or wall progressivelyAccurate tapers and variable wall in smaller diameters
Rotary swagingHammering dies rotate around the tube and reduce the diameter locallyStepped ends, short tapers, antenna and sports tubes
Spinning and flow formingA roller forms the tube over a rotating mandrelLong tapers, poles, thin-walled cones
Push-pointing and end formingEnds are pushed into a die to reduce diameterShort reductions for joints and inserts
Variable-wall extrusion or machiningWall is varied during extrusion or bored internally after extrusionInner-cone tubes with constant outside size
HydroformingInternal pressure expands tube into a shaped dieComplex shapes and oval transitions

Heat-treatable alloys are often formed in a soft temper (O or W, freshly quenched) and then solution treated and aged to T6, or formed in T4 and artificially aged afterwards. Forming in the fully hard T6 temper is possible only for gentle tapers, because elongation is limited.

Practical advice

How to specify a reducer tube

  • Dimension every transition: start and end diameters, taper length, wall at defined stations, and blend radii.
  • State concentricity and straightness between the ends, and how they are measured.
  • Define the temper after forming and where tensile samples are taken.
  • Specify surface limits, for example maximum scratch depth, if the tube is anodised or visible.
  • Give the base standard (ASTM B210, B221 or B241; EN 754 or 755) for chemistry and properties, with the drawing governing geometry.
  • Common mistake: designing a sharp step in a heat-treatable tube without allowing for forming limits; ask for a manufacturability check early.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) reducer and tapered tube: FAQs

What is the difference between a reducer tube and a reducer fitting?

A reducer tube is a single long tube whose diameter or wall changes along its length. A reducer fitting is a short cone welded between two pipes of different sizes in a piping system.

Which alloy is used for pantograph tubes?

7020-T6 is widely used because it combines high strength and fatigue resistance with good weldability. 6061-T6 and 6082-T6 are alternatives for less demanding parts.

Can a tapered tube be heat-treated after forming?

Yes. Heat-treatable alloys are often formed soft and then solution treated and aged, so the finished tube reaches full T6 properties along its length.

What tolerances are possible on reducer tubes?

They depend on size and method. Drawn and machined ends can be held to a few hundredths of a millimetre on diameter; long tapers usually carry wider tolerances. The drawing sets the limits.

Yes. Send the drawing, alloy, temper and quantity and we confirm the forming route, inspection and delivery.
