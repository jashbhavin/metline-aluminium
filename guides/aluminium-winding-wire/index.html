Aluminium Winding Wire: Selection Guide for Motors, Transformers & Coils

Aluminium winding wire is a fully annealed, high-purity aluminium conductor (normally 1350 or 1370), drawn to round or rectangular section and insulated with enamel, paper or fibre so it can be wound into coils. It carries about 61 % of the conductivity of copper at roughly one third of the density, so a winding of equal resistance is about half the weight. This guide explains how to choose it against copper and copper-clad aluminium, which insulation class to specify and how to size and terminate it.

Overview

What aluminium winding wire is, and why designers use it

Every motor, transformer, reactor, ballast, solenoid and relay contains a coil, and the coil is wound from winding wire (also called magnet wire). For most of the twentieth century that wire was copper. Aluminium has taken a growing share since the 1960s, first in distribution transformers and later in fan motors, compressors, air-conditioner motors and induction cookers, because it delivers the same electrical job for less weight and, at most points in the metal price cycle, for less money.

The conductor is electrical-conductor (EC) grade aluminium of at least 99.5 % purity, registered as alloy 1350 (EN AW-1350, UNS A91350), or the slightly higher-conductivity 1370. Wire rod is drawn down through a series of dies and then fully annealed so the conductor is soft enough to wind tightly without cracking the insulation. Insulation is applied in-line: several thin coats of enamel for round and small flat wire, or wrapped paper, polyester film, aramid or glass fibre for larger rectangular conductors used in oil-filled and dry-type transformers.

Three things decide whether aluminium is the right conductor for a coil: the space available for the winding, the importance of weight and material cost, and how the coil ends will be joined. The rest of this guide works through each of these, with the numbers you need for a first-pass design.

Where aluminium winding wire makes sense

  • Distribution and dry-type transformers, where copper and aluminium designs are both common and weight matters for transport and pole mounting
  • Fan, pump, compressor and household-appliance motors built in high volume, where material cost per unit dominates
  • Ballasts, chokes, reactors and induction-heating coils, where the slightly larger winding window is acceptable
  • Any coil where lower mass reduces the cost of the frame, the shipping or the supporting structure

The practical consequence is a simple trade. To match the resistance of a copper winding, an aluminium conductor needs about 63 % more cross-sectional area, which means a diameter about 28 % larger. Even with that extra area, the aluminium winding weighs only about 49 % as much as the copper one, because aluminium is so much lighter. Designers therefore accept a slightly larger winding window in return for a much lighter, and usually cheaper, coil.

CCA sits between the two. Its copper skin gives a solderable surface and slightly better high-frequency behaviour, because at high frequency current crowds into the outer layer of the conductor. It is widely used for voice coils, small appliance motors and some telecommunication coils. For low-frequency power windings, where current flows through the whole section, CCA behaves almost like aluminium electrically and costs more, so solid aluminium is usually the better value.

Ratios above are calculated from conductivity and density, taking aluminium at 61.5 % IACS. Check the exact conductivity on the wire certificate for your design.

Product forms

Round, rectangular and specialty aluminium winding wire

Enamelled round wire

The standard form for motors, small transformers, ballasts and coils. Typical nominal diameters run from about 0.2 mm to 5 mm. Grade 1, 2 or 3 refers to enamel build: thicker builds give higher breakdown voltage at the cost of a little winding space.

Enamelled rectangular (flat) wire

Fills the winding window far better than round wire because there are no gaps between turns. Used in traction and EV motors, welding transformers, reactors and compact dry-type transformers. Edge radius and aspect ratio must suit the winding machine.

Paper-covered conductor

Rectangular or round aluminium wrapped in layers of kraft or crêpe paper, then impregnated with transformer oil in service. The traditional insulation for oil-filled distribution and power transformers. See our paper-covered magnet wire guide.

Glass-fibre and film-covered wire

Glass yarn, polyester film or aramid paper wraps for dry-type transformers, traction motors and high-temperature coils, often over an enamel base coat and bonded with varnish.

Bare and strip conductor

Uninsulated round or flat wire, plus aluminium strip and foil for foil-wound low-voltage transformer windings. Foil windings are covered in our transformer foil and strip guide.

Self-bonding and Litz types

Self-bonding wire has a thermoplastic top coat that fuses turns together when heated, removing the need for varnish. Litz wire bundles many fine insulated strands to reduce AC losses at high frequency; aluminium Litz is used in some induction-heating and wireless-charging coils.

Insulation systems

Enamel types and thermal classes

The thermal class of a winding wire is the temperature, in °C, at which the insulation is expected to give a satisfactory life under continuous operation. Classes follow IEC 60085 and the temperature index tests in IEC 60172. Choose a class at least one step above the hot-spot temperature calculated for the coil, so that overloads and ambient extremes do not shorten its life.

Thermal classLetterTypical enamel chemistryTypical use
130 °CBPolyester, polyurethaneSmall motors, relays, general coils
155 °CFModified polyester, polyurethane (solderable)Appliance motors, ballasts, small transformers
180 °CHPolyesterimideIndustrial motors, compressors, dry-type transformers
200 °CNPolyesterimide base with polyamide-imide overcoatHermetic compressors, high-load motors, traction
220 °CRPolyamide-imide or polyimideHigh-temperature motors, aerospace and specialist coils

A dual-coat construction (a polyesterimide base with a polyamide-imide top coat) is the workhorse of modern motor winding because the hard top coat resists the abrasion of high-speed automatic winding and the base coat gives good adhesion and thermal life. For hermetic refrigeration compressors, the enamel must also resist refrigerants and compressor oil, which is a separate qualification test.

Polyurethane enamels are "solderable": the coating burns off in a hot solder bath, which is convenient on copper. On aluminium the bath removes the enamel but not the tough natural oxide, so solderable enamel does not by itself make aluminium wire easy to solder. Plan the termination method before choosing the enamel (see the fabrication section below).

IEC 60317-15 (polyesterimide, class 180), IEC 60317-25 (dual coat, class 200) and IEC 60317-89 (polyesterimide, class 200) are examples of the IEC parts written specifically for enamelled round aluminium wire.

Engineering

Converting a copper winding to aluminium

  1. Fix the resistance targetStart from the DC resistance or the I²R loss the copper design achieved. Keep it the same, or decide how much extra loss the product can accept.
  2. Scale the conductorMultiply the copper cross-section by about 1.6 to keep the same resistance. For round wire this means stepping up roughly two to three sizes in the R20 series, or about two AWG gauges.
  3. Check the winding windowAluminium needs more space. If the window cannot grow, the options are a larger core, rectangular wire for a better fill factor, or accepting higher loss and temperature.
  4. Re-check temperature riseLower thermal conductivity means hot spots run a little hotter for the same loss. Confirm the thermal class still has margin.
  5. Check mechanical forcesIn transformers, short-circuit forces must be resisted by a softer conductor. Designers use work-hardened or proof-stressed conductor, extra bracing or stiffer coil construction where required.
  6. Design the terminationsDecide on welding, crimping or transition to copper leads before release, and qualify the joint by thermal cycling.

In motors the larger conductor can raise slot fill or reduce turns, which affects efficiency classes under IEC 60034-30-1. Many appliance and fan motors run comfortably on aluminium; premium-efficiency industrial motors more often stay with copper because they need maximum conductivity in a fixed frame size. In distribution transformers both conductors meet the same loss standards; the choice is usually economic.

Fabrication

Terminating, joining and handling aluminium winding wire

Why aluminium joints need care

Aluminium forms a hard, insulating oxide film within seconds of exposure to air. It also creeps under sustained pressure and expands more with heat than copper. A joint that ignores these three facts can loosen and overheat over time.

Welding

TIG, resistance, cold-pressure and ultrasonic welding are the most reliable ways to join aluminium winding ends to each other or to copper leads. Friction-welded aluminium-copper transition pieces are common in transformers.

Crimping

Use connectors designed for aluminium, with serrations that break the oxide and, where specified, an oxide-inhibiting compound. Crimp tooling must be matched to the conductor size.

Soldering

Possible with special aluminium fluxes and solders or ultrasonic soldering baths that scrub the oxide away. Standard tin-lead or lead-free baths used for copper are not sufficient.

Winding

Annealed aluminium is softer than copper, so set winding tension lower, check guide rollers and nozzles for sharp edges, and avoid kinks that thin the conductor.

Storage

Keep spools in their packaging in a dry store and use first-in, first-out. Enamel absorbs some moisture; condensation from cold transport should be allowed to clear before winding.

A useful order specification states the conductor (1350 annealed), nominal size and tolerance, insulation type and build, thermal class and the governing standard. Ask for a test certificate that reports, per lot, conductor diameter, overall diameter, resistance, elongation, breakdown voltage, pinhole or continuity results and heat-shock results.   • Conductor: resistance and conductivity against the minimum for annealed 1350
  • Insulation: breakdown voltage, continuity (pinholes per length), adhesion and flexibility after elongation
  • Thermal: heat shock and cut-through temperatures for the specified class
  • Dimensions: bare and overall diameter, or thickness, width and edge radius for flat wire
  • Packaging: spool type, net weight per spool, labelling and traceability to the lot
Buying advice

Common mistakes when specifying aluminium winding wire

  • Substituting size for size. Replacing copper with the same diameter of aluminium raises resistance by about 60 % and the coil will overheat.
  • Specifying the wrong alloy. Structural alloys such as 6061 or 3003 have much lower conductivity. Winding wire should be EC-grade 1350 or 1370, annealed.
  • Assuming solderable enamel solves termination. It removes the coating, not the oxide. Qualify the actual joint.
  • Choosing thermal class on ambient temperature. Size the class to the calculated hot spot plus margin, not the room temperature.
  • Ignoring the spool. Spool size and flange type must suit the winding machine pay-off; a mismatch causes tension problems and downtime.
  • Leaving out the test standard. Without IEC 60317 or NEMA MW 1000 on the order, there is no agreed basis for acceptance.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) winding wire: frequently asked questions

Is aluminium winding wire as good as copper?

Electrically it does the same job once it is sized correctly: about 1.6 times the copper cross-section gives the same resistance at about half the weight. Copper is preferable where the winding space is fixed and small, or where maximum efficiency in a given frame size is required.

Which alloy is used for aluminium winding wire?

Electrical-conductor grade 1350 (EN AW-1350), and sometimes 1370, supplied fully annealed. These have at least 99.5 % aluminium and a conductivity of about 61 – 62 % IACS. General-purpose alloys are not used because their conductivity is much lower.

Can enamelled aluminium wire be soldered?

Not with an ordinary solder bath. Aluminium oxide prevents wetting, so you need special aluminium solder and flux, ultrasonic soldering, or a welded or crimped joint. Welding to a copper lead is the most common approach in transformers and motors.

What is the difference between aluminium and CCA winding wire?

CCA has a thin copper layer bonded to an aluminium core. It is slightly heavier and more expensive than solid aluminium but solders like copper and performs a little better at high frequency. For low-frequency power windings, solid aluminium usually offers better value.

What thermal class should I specify?

Choose a class above the coil hot-spot temperature: class 155 (F) for many appliance motors, class 180 (H) for industrial motors and dry-type transformers, and class 200 (N) or higher for hermetic compressors and heavy-duty service.
