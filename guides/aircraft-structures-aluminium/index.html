Aluminium Alloys for Aircraft Structures

Aircraft structures are built mainly from 2xxx (aluminium-copper) and 7xxx (aluminium-zinc) alloys: 2024 where fatigue and damage tolerance govern, 7075, 7050 and 7475 where strength and stress-corrosion resistance govern. This guide maps each major airframe part to the alloy, temper, product form and AMS specification normally used, and explains why.

Overview

Why aluminium still dominates airframes

Even with carbon-fibre fuselages on some new airliners, aluminium alloys make up most of the structural weight of the world's commercial, regional, business and military aircraft fleet, and large parts of composite aircraft too. They offer a well-understood combination of strength, stiffness, damage tolerance, repairability and cost that designers can certify with confidence.

No single alloy suits the whole aircraft. The lower wing skin and fuselage skin are loaded mainly in tension and cycle with every flight, so fatigue crack growth and fracture toughness matter most. The upper wing skin is loaded mainly in compression, so yield strength matters most. Thick machined parts such as bulkheads and spars need strength that holds through the full thickness and low residual stress so they do not distort during machining. These different demands are why airframes combine several alloy families and tempers.

Over the decades, alloy development has followed those needs: 2024-T3 for damage-tolerant skins, 7075-T6 for high strength, over-aged T73 and T76 tempers to fix the stress-corrosion weakness of 7075-T6, and then 7050 and 7475 for thick plate with better toughness and through-thickness properties. Newer aluminium-lithium alloys (such as 2050, 2060, 2099 and 2196) lower density and raise stiffness on the latest programmes.

Density matters too: 2024 is 2.78 g/cm³, 7075 2.81 g/cm³ and 7050 2.83 g/cm³, compared with 2.70 g/cm³ for 6061. Aluminium-lithium alloys reach around 2.6 g/cm³ with a higher elastic modulus.

Tempers

Aerospace tempers explained

T3 / T351

Solution heat-treated, cold worked and naturally aged; T351 plate is also stretched to relieve stress. The standard tempers for damage-tolerant 2024.

T6 / T651

Solution heat-treated and artificially aged to peak strength. T651 is stretched. Highest strength but lowest stress-corrosion resistance for 7075.

T73 / T7351

Over-aged beyond peak strength. Gives up about 10 – 15 % of strength to gain very high resistance to stress-corrosion cracking.

T76 / T7651

Over-aged less than T73: better exfoliation corrosion resistance than T6 with less strength loss than T73.

T74 / T7451

An intermediate over-age used mainly for 7050, balancing strength, toughness and stress-corrosion resistance in thick sections.

Tx511 / Tx510

The extrusion equivalents of stretched tempers; 511 allows minor straightening after stretching, 510 does not.

The single most common selection error on older designs was using 7075-T6 in thick parts loaded in the short-transverse direction, which caused stress-corrosion cracking in service. Modern practice specifies T73, T74 or T76 tempers for thick 7xxx parts unless the stresses are well understood.

Plate for primary structure is normally ultrasonically inspected to AMS-STD-2154 (formerly MIL-STD-2154), with the acceptance class set by thickness and part criticality. Forgings and thick plate also need checks on electrical conductivity and hardness to confirm the over-aged temper, since conductivity is a sensitive indicator of correct T73 or T74 ageing.

Quality and traceability

Certification and traceability for aerospace aluminium

  • Specification compliance: each lot is certified to the AMS, AMS-QQ-A or ASTM specification on the order, at the revision called out
  • Mill certificate: chemistry and mechanical test results per lot, typically to EN 10204 3.1, with heat and lot traceability
  • Non-destructive testing: ultrasonic inspection of plate and forgings; conductivity and hardness for over-aged tempers
  • Design data: structural allowables come from MMPDS (the successor to MIL-HDBK-5) for approved alloy, temper and form combinations
  • Flow-down: aircraft programmes add their own approved-source lists and process requirements, which the buyer must flow down

Selection

Choosing the right alloy for an aircraft part

  1. Identify the governing loadTension-dominated and cyclic parts favour 2024 or 7475; compression-dominated parts favour 7075 or 7055; thick machined parts favour 7050.
  2. Check the thicknessStrength of 7075 falls in thick sections; 7050 keeps its properties better through thick plate and forgings.
  3. Assess the environmentWhere stress corrosion or exfoliation is a risk, use T73, T74 or T76 tempers or Alclad sheet.
  4. Consider fabrication2024 and 7075 are not fusion welded on structure; plan for riveted, bolted or bonded joints, or friction stir welding where approved.
  5. Confirm allowablesUse MMPDS or programme design data for the exact alloy, temper, form and thickness.

Common mistakes

  • Substituting 7075-T6 for 7075-T73 to gain strength without checking stress-corrosion exposure
  • Ordering plate to ASTM B209 when the drawing calls for an AMS specification with extra testing
  • Ignoring the thickness range of a specification, which changes guaranteed properties
  • Forgetting that Alclad cladding lowers fatigue strength slightly and changes machining allowances

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) for aircraft structures: frequently asked questions

What aluminium alloy is used most in aircraft?

2024 and 7075 are the most widely used. 2024-T3 is the classic fuselage and lower-wing skin alloy for fatigue resistance, and 7075 is used where higher strength is needed. 7050 has largely replaced 7075 for thick machined parts.

Why is 7050 used instead of 7075 for thick parts?

7050 keeps its strength and toughness through thick sections much better than 7075 and, in the T7451 temper, combines high strength with good stress-corrosion resistance. That makes it the standard choice for bulkheads, spars and ribs machined from thick plate.

What is Alclad aluminium?

Alclad sheet has a thin layer of high-purity aluminium bonded to each side of a strong core such as 2024. The cladding corrodes sacrificially and protects the core, which is why fuselage skins are often Alclad.

Can aerospace aluminium alloys be welded?

2024 and 7075 are generally not fusion welded on aircraft structure because welding causes cracking and strength loss. Joints are riveted, bolted or bonded. Friction stir welding is used on some approved applications.

What does T7351 mean?

Solution heat-treated, stress relieved by stretching, and over-aged. The over-ageing trades some strength for very high resistance to stress-corrosion cracking.
