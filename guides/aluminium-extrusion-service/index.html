Custom Aluminium Extrusions: How the Service Works, from Drawing to Delivery

A custom aluminium extrusion is a profile made to your own cross-section using a die cut specifically for it, so the part arrives close to its final shape and needs little or no machining. This guide walks through the whole service: how extrusion works, which alloys and die types suit which profiles, the design rules that keep tooling simple and tolerances achievable, finishing options, what drives cost, and the common defects to guard against. Metline manages custom extrusion projects for customers worldwide, from drawing review to certified, export-packed delivery.

Overview

What is a custom aluminium extrusion?

Extrusion forms aluminium the way a piping bag forms icing: a heated billet is pushed through a shaped opening and comes out as a long bar with that exact cross-section. A custom extrusion simply means the opening (the die) has been designed for your profile rather than taken from a catalogue of standard shapes.

Because the cross-section is produced in one operation, features that would take hours to machine, such as screw bosses, snap-fit grooves, cooling fins, T-slots, internal ribs and hollow chambers, are built into the profile from the start. Dies for most profiles are relatively inexpensive compared with casting moulds or stamping tools, so custom extrusion becomes economical at modest volumes, and once the die exists the cost per metre is close to that of a standard shape.

The process suits aluminium better than almost any other metal. 6xxx alloys flow readily at 400 – 500 °C, can be quenched straight off the press, and reach their final strength by artificial ageing, so a complex, thin-walled profile can leave the line already in T5 or T6 temper. The resulting parts are light, corrosion-resistant, recyclable and easy to anodise or paint.

Custom profiles are everywhere: window and curtain-wall systems, solar mounting rails, LED and electronics housings, heat sinks, EV battery enclosures, truck and trailer floors, rail car body panels, machine frames, medical equipment and consumer products. The common thread is a part that is long compared with its cross-section and would otherwise be built up from several pieces or machined from solid.

What a custom extrusion can do for a design

  • Combine several parts into one profile, removing fasteners, welds and assembly time
  • Put material only where it carries load, cutting weight compared with machined plate or steel
  • Integrate functional features (fins, channels, screw ports, hinges, snap fits) directly into the section
  • Deliver consistent dimensions over thousands of metres once the die is approved
Process

How custom extrusion works, step by step

  1. Drawing reviewThe profile drawing is checked for extrudability: circumscribing circle size, wall thickness, tongue ratios, corner radii, tolerances and the alloy and temper requested. Suggested changes are agreed before the die is cut.
  2. Die design and manufactureA die maker designs the die (solid, semi-hollow or porthole) and machines it from hot-work tool steel such as H13, typically by CNC milling and wire EDM, followed by heat treatment and nitriding.
  3. Billet preparationCast billets of the chosen alloy, homogenised for good extrudability, are cut to length and heated, usually to about 420 – 500 °C depending on alloy.
  4. ExtrusionThe billet is loaded into the press container and the ram pushes it through the die. Metal flow, exit temperature and speed are controlled to protect surface quality and dimensions.
  5. QuenchingThe profile is cooled by air, mist or water as it leaves the die. The cooling rate needed depends on the alloy: 6063 can be air cooled, while 6061 and 6082 usually need water or intense mist quenching to reach T6 properties.
  6. Stretching and cuttingProfiles are stretched about 0.5 – 3 % to straighten them and relieve stress, then sawn to length.
  7. AgeingArtificial ageing in an oven (typically several hours at 160 – 200 °C) brings 6xxx alloys to T5 or T6 temper.
  8. First-article inspectionSamples from the first run are measured against the drawing and the tolerance standard and approved before volume production.
  9. Finishing and fabricationAnodising, powder coating, cutting to length, machining, bending and packing as specified.

The first two steps are where most of the value is created. A profile that is easy to extrude runs faster, scraps less and holds tolerance better, and a few small changes at the drawing stage often save more money than any later negotiation on price per kilogram.

Profile types

Solid, semi-hollow and hollow profiles

Profiles are classed by whether they enclose a void, because that determines the type of die and therefore the cost and difficulty of the tooling.

Profile typeDefinitionDie typeExamples
SolidNo enclosed or partly enclosed voidFlat (solid) die, the simplest and cheapestBars, angles, tees, channels, heat-sink bases, trims
Semi-hollowA partly enclosed void whose opening is narrow compared with its area (a high "tongue ratio")Semi-hollow or porthole-style die with a supported tongueDeep narrow channels, C-slots, some T-slot profiles
HollowOne fully enclosed voidPorthole (bridge) die: a mandrel held by bridges; metal splits and re-welds inside the dieRound, square and rectangular tube, frames, rails
Multi-void hollowTwo or more enclosed voidsPorthole die with several mandrelsWindow and door frames, battery tray sections, floor planks, microchannel tube

Porthole dies produce longitudinal weld seams where the metal streams rejoin. These seams are sound in properly run 6xxx alloys and are accepted for structural, architectural and most mechanical uses, but they are a concern for high-pressure tube and for hard alloys such as 2024 and 7075, which do not re-weld reliably. For those, hollows are made seamless on a press with a piercing mandrel. Our extruded tube guide covers the difference in detail.

Die design is a specialist subject in its own right; see our article on the aluminium extrusion die for die types, design features and what affects die life.

Materials

Choosing an alloy for a custom profile

Design for extrusion

Design rules for a cost-effective custom profile

Good extrusion design is mostly about helping the metal flow evenly through the die. Uneven flow causes distortion, poor surface and dimensional drift; very thin or deep features shorten die life. These rules are widely used across the industry, including in the Aluminum Extruders Council design guidance:

Keep the section compact

Size is measured by the circumscribing circle diameter (CCD), the smallest circle that encloses the profile. Smaller CCDs fit more presses, run faster and cost less. Profiles with a CCD under about 200 mm (8 in) and a modest weight per metre are the easiest to source.

Balance wall thickness

Aim for uniform walls. Where thickness must change, make the transition gradual. A thin wall next to a heavy one cools and flows differently and is a common cause of twist and wavy surfaces.

Respect minimum walls

Minimum practical wall depends on alloy and CCD: 6060 and 6063 allow the thinnest walls, 6061 and 6082 need slightly more, and 7xxx and 2xxx alloys need substantially thicker walls. Ask for a check rather than designing to the absolute limit.

Round the corners

Use radii of at least 0.5 – 1 mm on internal and external corners. Sharp corners slow metal flow, crack dies and act as stress raisers in service.

Limit tongue ratios

Deep, narrow channels (high depth-to-gap ratio) need a long, unsupported die tongue that can deflect or break. Widen the gap, reduce the depth or convert the feature to a hollow.

Use symmetry

Symmetrical profiles fill the die evenly, distort less and are easier to straighten. Where a profile is asymmetric, small balancing ribs can help.

Mark visible faces

Show which surfaces are decorative. The die can be arranged so those faces avoid weld lines, contact with the run-out table and handling marks.

Design in function

Screw bosses, grooves for gaskets, snap fits, interlocks, index marks and drill guide lines cost nothing extra in the die and save fabrication time.

Tolerance only what matters

Apply tight tolerances to functional dimensions only, and give datum features. Over-tolerancing every dimension raises scrap and price without improving the part.

Two further points help assembly-heavy designs. First, decide early whether a hollow is really needed: a well-designed open profile with a closing cover or an interlock can be cheaper than a multi-void hollow. Second, if the profile will be machined, cut, bent or anodised, tell the extruder at the start, because alloy, temper and surface specification all change accordingly. Size range

From miniature to large and thin-wall profiles

Custom extrusions span a very wide size range. Which part of it your profile falls into decides the class of press required, the achievable tolerances and how many sources can make it.

Tolerances, standards and inspection

Unless the drawing states otherwise, custom profiles are made to the general tolerance tables of a published standard. Name the standard on the drawing so everyone measures against the same rules.

StandardWhat it covers
EN 755-1 / 755-2Technical delivery conditions and mechanical properties of extruded profiles
EN 755-9Tolerances on dimensions and form of general extruded profiles (CCD up to 800 mm)
EN 12020-2Tighter tolerances for precision profiles in EN AW-6060 and EN AW-6063, including thermal-break profiles
ASTM B221 / B221MExtruded profiles, bar and tube: chemistry, tempers, mechanical properties
ANSI H35.2 / Aluminum Standards and DataStandard dimensional tolerances used with ASTM orders
EN 15088Aluminium structural products for construction works
JIS H4100 / GB/T 6892Japanese and Chinese standards for extruded profiles
EN 10204Inspection documents: type 3.1 mill test certificate

Tolerances widen with profile size and are tighter for 6060 and 6063 than for 6061, 6082 and harder alloys. Wall thickness tolerances in EN 755-9 also depend on whether the wall encloses a void, because walls around a hollow are harder to control. If a dimension must be tighter than standard, state it on the drawing as a special tolerance; it can usually be met by die design and process control, at some cost.

  • First-article inspection: full dimensional check of samples from the first run against the drawing
  • Production checks: regular measurement of critical dimensions, straightness, twist and angles
  • Material tests: chemistry, tensile tests or hardness and conductivity checks per lot
  • Surface inspection: visible faces checked for die lines, scratches and finish defects; coating thickness for anodised and painted profiles
  • Documentation: EN 10204 3.1 certificate, inspection report, and third-party inspection on request

A useful rule: decide the finish before cutting the die. Decorative anodising shows every die line and alloy variation, so it may call for 6060 or 6063 and particular attention to the visible face, whereas a powder-coated or hidden profile can tolerate a wider choice of alloy and surface.

Cost

What drives the cost of a custom extrusion

A custom extrusion has two cost components: a one-time tooling cost for the die, and a recurring price per kilogram or per metre for the profile.

Die (tooling) cost depends on

  • Profile type: solid dies cost least, porthole dies for hollows more, multi-void dies most
  • Size (CCD) and the press class it needs
  • Complexity: thin walls, fine details, high tongue ratios and tight tolerances
  • Alloy: dies for hard alloys work harder and wear faster
  • Number of die openings (cavities) for small profiles

Profile price depends on

  • Aluminium market price (LME plus regional premium) and the alloy
  • Weight per metre: heavier profiles usually cost less per kg to extrude
  • Extrusion speed and recovery, which fall with complexity and hard alloys
  • Temper, quench and testing requirements
  • Finish, machining, cutting, packing and freight
  • Order quantity and how often the die is set up

Ways to reduce cost without compromising the part:

  • Simplify the section and remove hollows that are not structurally needed
  • Use the leanest alloy that meets the strength requirement (6063 or 6005A before 6061 or 7075)
  • Even out wall thicknesses and add radii, which raise extrusion speed and yield
  • Ask whether an existing standard or catalogue die can be used, or adapted with light machining
  • Order in fewer, larger runs to reduce set-up and die changes
  • Tolerance only critical dimensions and specify finishes only where they are visible

Alternatives

When another process is the better choice

ProcessBetter than extrusion whenLess suitable when
CNC machining from plate or barVery small quantities, prototypes, parts with features that vary along the lengthLong parts or volumes where machining time and scrap dominate cost
Die castingComplex 3D shapes, closed ends, high volumesLong uniform parts; cast alloys are weaker and less ductile than wrought
Roll forming of sheet or coilThin, constant-thickness open sections in very high volumesSections need varying wall thickness, hollows or integrated features
Press-brake bending of sheetSimple angles and channels, short runs, no die budgetTight tolerances, complex shapes, long lengths

Extrusion and machining often work together: a near-net profile is extruded, then cut and machined to add holes, threads and end features. This combination is usually the cheapest route for parts that are mostly constant in section but need some local detail.

FAQ

Custom aluminium (aluminum) extrusions: frequently asked questions

How much does a custom aluminium extrusion die cost?

It depends mainly on the profile type, size and complexity. A die for a small solid profile is the cheapest tooling in metal forming; porthole dies for hollow and multi-void profiles cost more, and large-press dies more again. Send the drawing and we will quote the tooling and profile price together.

Who owns the extrusion die?

The customer normally pays for the die and it is reserved for their profile. Agree in writing how it is stored, maintained and replaced when worn, and whether it can be transferred.

Can a custom extrusion be supplied finished and machined?

Yes. Profiles can be anodised, powder coated or painted and then cut, drilled, milled, tapped, bent or punched to drawing, so the parts arrive ready to assemble.

What tolerances can custom extrusions hold?

General profiles are made to EN 755-9 or ANSI H35.2 tables; precision profiles in 6060 and 6063 can be made to the tighter EN 12020-2 tables. Specific critical dimensions can be held tighter by agreement, and machining can be used where extrusion alone cannot meet the drawing.

Which alloy is best for custom extrusions?

6063 for complex, thin-walled or decorative profiles, 6005A when more strength is needed in hollows, 6061 or 6082 for structural and machined parts, and 7075 or 2024 only where very high strength justifies simpler shapes and higher cost.

How can I reduce the cost of my extrusion?

Shrink the circumscribing circle, even out wall thickness, add corner radii, avoid unnecessary hollows, choose the leanest suitable alloy, tolerance only critical dimensions and order in larger, less frequent runs.

Do you supply custom extrusions outside India?

Yes. Metline ships custom profiles worldwide with export packing, EN 10204 3.1 certificates and EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP terms.

Ready to develop a custom profile?

Send your drawing, alloy, finish and quantities. Metline will review extrudability and come back with tooling, pricing and delivery options.
