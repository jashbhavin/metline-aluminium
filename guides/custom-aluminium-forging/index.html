Custom Aluminium Forgings: Process, Alloy and Specification Guide

A custom aluminium forging is a part shaped by pressing or hammering hot aluminium to a drawing, so that the metal's grain follows the part's contour and the finished component is stronger and more fatigue-resistant than one machined from plate or cast in a mould. This guide helps engineers and buyers choose between open-die, closed-die and ring-rolled forgings, pick the alloy and temper, design a part that forges well, and write a request for quotation that gets accurate answers. Metline supplies aluminium forgings to drawing, as forged or machined, to customers worldwide.

Overview

Why specify a forging instead of plate, bar or casting?

Forging works the metal under compressive force. It closes internal porosity, breaks up the cast structure of the starting billet and aligns the grain along the shape of the part. The result is the highest and most consistent combination of strength, ductility and fatigue resistance available in a given aluminium alloy.

Compared with machining the same shape from thick plate or bar, a forging puts the grain flow where the loads are. In a machined part, the grain of the plate runs straight and is cut through at every curve, so fillets and lugs end up loaded across the weakest (short-transverse) direction. In a forging the grain wraps around the curve. This matters most for parts loaded in fatigue, such as suspension arms, wheels, landing-gear fittings and rotating components.

Compared with a casting, a forging has no shrinkage porosity, a finer grain and much higher elongation. Castings win on complex internal cavities and on cost for low-stress parts; forgings win wherever the part is safety-critical or cyclically loaded. Forging also usually saves material compared with machining from a solid block, because the shape is close to final dimensions before machining starts.

Process selection

Which forging process suits your part?

Open-die (hand / free) forging

The billet is shaped between flat or simple dies, turned and worked by the operator. It produces blocks, discs, bars, shafts and hollow cylinders with generous machining allowance. No dedicated tooling, so it is the economical route for large parts, prototypes and small batches. See forged blocks and forged discs.

Closed-die (impression-die) forging

Hot metal fills a machined die cavity, often in several stages (blocker then finisher), and excess flows into a flash that is trimmed off. Produces near-net shapes such as control arms, brackets, knuckles and housings with repeatable dimensions. Die cost is paid back over volume. See aluminium die forgings.

Ring rolling

A pierced, forged doughnut is rolled between a driven roll and an idler, growing in diameter while the wall thins. Gives seamless rings with circumferential grain flow for flanges, bearing races, slewing rings and wheel rims. See rolled ring forgings.

Backward extrusion

A punch drives into a billet held in a container so metal flows back around it, forming cups, cylinders and thick-walled tubes with a closed end. Used for housings, valve bodies and pressure vessel parts.

Precision / no-draft forging

Closed-die forging with tighter tolerances, little or no draft and thin webs, reducing machining. Higher die and press cost; used for aerospace and high-volume automotive parts. See precision forgings.

Cold and warm forging

Forming at room temperature or moderately elevated temperature for small parts that need tight tolerances and good surface finish, typically in softer alloys such as 1xxx, 3xxx and 6xxx. See aluminium cold forging.

A useful rule of thumb: if you need fewer than a few dozen parts, or the part is large and simple, start with open-die forgings and machine to shape. Once quantities justify a die, closed-die forging reduces weight, machining time and material cost per part. Rings and annular parts above a few hundred millimetres in diameter are almost always ring-rolled.

Temperature routes

Hot, warm and cold forging of aluminium

Aluminium alloys have a narrow hot-working window compared with steel. Most wrought alloys are hot forged with the metal at roughly 350 – 500 °C, and dies are heated as well (often 150 – 450 °C depending on process) so that the thin aluminium sections do not chill and crack on contact. Too hot, and the alloy can melt at grain boundaries (incipient melting); too cold, and it cracks or fails to fill the die.

Hot forgingAbout 350 – 500 °C, alloy dependentLow forming force, large deformation, recrystallised grain; followed by heat treatmentLarge blocks, rings, shafts, structural die forgings

Exact forging and die temperatures are set by the forging shop for each alloy; 2xxx and 7xxx alloys have narrower windows than 6xxx alloys.

Material selection

Choosing the alloy for a custom forging

Most aluminium forgings are made from heat-treatable alloys, which are forged, solution heat-treated, quenched and aged to their final strength. Non-heat-treatable 5083 is used where weldability and seawater resistance matter more than strength. Heat treatment

Forging tempers and what they mean

TemperMeaningWhen to specify
FAs forged, no heat treatmentForging blanks that the buyer will heat-treat after rough machining
OAnnealedMaximum ductility for further forming; rarely the final condition
T4Solution treated and naturally agedIntermediate condition, for parts formed or straightened before ageing
T6 / T61Solution treated, quenched, artificially agedStandard high-strength condition for 2xxx, 4xxx, 6xxx and 7xxx forgings
T652T6 with compressive stress relief (1 – 5 % permanent set) before ageingHand forgings and blocks that will be heavily machined, to reduce distortion
T73 / T7352Over-aged for maximum stress-corrosion resistance; T7352 adds stress relief7075 parts in aerospace and other corrosive or sustained-load service
T74 / T7452Over-aged, between T6 strength and T73 corrosion resistance7050 and 7075 aerospace forgings

Quenching after solution treatment leaves residual stresses, especially in thick forgings, which can cause parts to move during machining. Stress-relieved tempers (T652, T7352, T7452) are produced by a controlled cold compression after quenching and are the right choice for blocks and hand forgings that will have large amounts of metal removed. Quench sensitivity also limits the section thickness that reaches full properties: 7050 and 2xxx alloys hold their strength in thicker sections better than 7075 or 6061.

Design guidance

Designing a part that forges well

Small design choices drive die life, material use and cost. Involve the forging supplier early, ideally while the drawing is still flexible.

Parting line

Place the parting line at the largest cross-section so the part releases from both die halves. A flat parting line is cheaper than a stepped one.

Draft angles

Conventional closed-die aluminium forgings typically use about 5 – 7° draft on external surfaces, more on deep internal pockets. Precision forgings can reduce this, at higher tooling cost.

Fillets and corner radii

Generous radii help metal flow and prevent laps and folds. Sharp internal corners concentrate stress and shorten die life.

Ribs and webs

Aluminium forges thinner ribs and webs than steel, but very thin webs over large areas cool quickly and need high press force. Keep rib height to width ratios moderate.

Machining allowance

Allow stock on surfaces that need tight tolerance or fine finish, typically a few millimetres per face on die forgings and more on open-die forgings. Leave forged surfaces as-forged where function allows.

Grain flow

Orient the part so the principal loads run along the grain. Ask for a macro-etch grain-flow check on first articles of critical parts.

Datums

Define machining datums on forged surfaces that are stable and easy to locate, and mark them on the forging drawing.

Tolerances

Use standard forging tolerances (for example EN 586-3 or the supplier's tables) unless a feature truly needs tighter control; then machine it.

Welding

If the forging will be welded, choose 6xxx or 5083 and keep welds away from the most highly stressed areas, because the heat-affected zone loses strength.

Supply scope

Choosing the delivery condition

As-forged blanks

Forgings delivered in the specified temper with forged surfaces, for the buyer's own machining. Lowest unit price and the most control for the buyer.

Rough machined

Forged, heat-treated and rough machined to remove the skin and establish datums, often after ultrasonic inspection. Reduces scrap risk on the buyer's finish-machining line.

Finish machined

Forged, heat-treated and fully CNC machined to the part drawing, with surface treatment such as anodising, hard anodising or painting if specified. Ready for assembly.

Machining forgings is a specialist job: residual stresses must be balanced by removing metal evenly from opposite faces, and datum choices affect final flatness. Our CNC aluminium machining guide covers what to specify.

Ordering

What to include in a forging RFQ

  1. Drawings2D drawing with tolerances and finish, plus a 3D model; mark critical features, datums and any areas that must be machined.
  2. Material and temperAlloy and temper (e.g. 6082-T6, 7075-T7352), acceptable alternatives, and the governing standard: ASTM B247, EN 586, AMS-A-22771 or a company specification.
  3. QuantitiesPrototype quantity, batch size and annual volume. These decide open-die versus closed-die and whether dies are worthwhile.
  4. Delivery conditionAs forged, rough machined or finish machined, and any surface treatment.
  5. TestingMechanical test locations and directions, UT class, penetrant testing, grain flow, first-article inspection.
  6. Service conditionsLoads, fatigue life targets, operating temperature and environment, so the supplier can flag risks.
  7. CommercialDestination, delivery terms (EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP), packing and documentation required.

What drives the cost of a custom forging

  • Alloy (7xxx and 2xxx cost more than 6xxx) and the forged weight
  • Die cost for closed-die parts, amortised over the order quantity
  • Heat treatment route, especially stress-relieved and over-aged tempers
  • NDT class and the volume of testing and documentation
  • Machining scope and surface treatment

FAQ

Custom aluminium (aluminum) forgings: frequently asked questions

Are forged aluminium parts stronger than machined ones?

In the same alloy and temper, a forging usually has better fatigue strength, toughness and ductility in critical areas because its grain follows the part shape and internal porosity has been closed. Static strength in the main direction is similar; the advantage is consistency and performance at fillets and lugs.

What is the difference between open-die and closed-die forging?

Open-die forging shapes metal between simple tools and suits large, simple shapes and small quantities. Closed-die forging fills a machined die cavity to produce near-net shapes, with tooling cost that pays back over larger quantities.

Which aluminium alloy is best for forging?

6061 and 6082 are the easiest and most common for general parts. 2014 and 7075 give higher strength, 7050 is preferred for thick aerospace sections, 5083 for marine and cryogenic parts, and 2618 or 4032 for high-temperature pistons.

Why choose 7075-T73 instead of 7075-T6 for a forging?

T73 is over-aged, which sacrifices some strength (typically around 10 – 15 %) for much better resistance to stress-corrosion cracking. It is the normal choice for aerospace fittings and parts under sustained load in humid or marine air.

Can you supply forgings machined to my drawing?

Yes. Forgings can be supplied as forged, rough machined or fully machined with surface treatment, together with mill test certificates and inspection reports as agreed in the order.

Which standards apply to aluminum forgings?

ASTM B247 is the general US standard for die, hand and rolled ring forgings; EN 586 covers forgings in Europe; AMS-A-22771 and alloy-specific AMS specifications apply to aerospace parts. Ultrasonic testing is often specified to ASTM B594 or AMS-STD-2154.

How long does it take to get custom forgings?

It depends on whether new dies are needed, the alloy and heat treatment, testing requirements and machining scope. Open-die parts are generally faster to start than closed-die parts that need tooling. Share your drawing and quantity for a confirmed schedule.
