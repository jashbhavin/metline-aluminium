Aluminium Insulation Jacketing Coil: Alloys, Finishes and Specification

Aluminium jacketing coil is thin sheet, usually 0.4 – 0.8 mm, that is wrapped over thermal insulation on pipes, vessels, ducts and tanks to keep out rain, protect the insulation from damage and give a clean finish. The usual specification is 3003 or 3105 in H14 temper with a factory-laminated moisture barrier, classified under ASTM C1729. This guide explains how to choose alloy, thickness, surface and barrier, and how to store the coils so they arrive and stay in good condition.

Overview

What insulation jacketing does

Insulation only works while it stays dry. Mineral wool, calcium silicate, cellular glass and foam insulations lose much of their thermal value when wet, and water trapped against a hot or cold steel pipe drives corrosion under insulation (CUI), one of the costliest maintenance problems in refineries, chemical plants and power stations.

The jacket is the weather barrier. It sheds rain and wash-down water, resists foot traffic, ladders and tools, blocks ultraviolet light that degrades foam, and gives the plant a uniform, easy-to-inspect surface. Aluminium is the most common jacketing metal because it is light, easy to cut and roll on site, resists atmospheric corrosion well and costs less than stainless steel. Stainless jacketing is generally reserved for fire-rated systems and areas with specific chemical exposure, because aluminium melts at about 660 °C.

Jacketing coil is bought in rolls and either cut and rolled on site, or pre-formed in a workshop into cylinders, elbow gores and end caps. Metline supplies the aluminium coil and sheet; insulation contractors carry out fabrication and installation.

Standard

How ASTM C1729 classifies aluminium jacketing

ASTM C1729 is the North American specification for aluminium jacketing used over thermal insulation, and it is increasingly quoted on international projects. It classifies jacketing by three choices, so a specification line reads like "Type I, Grade 1, Class A".

ClassificationWhat it definesExamples
TypeOuter surface treatment and emittanceBare (mill finish or embossed) or painted outer surface
GradeAlloy and temper, which must meet ASTM B209e.g. 3003 or 3105 in H14; heavier-duty H12 versions
ClassInterior moisture retardere.g. polyfilm moisture barrier, polykraft, or other retarders

A widely used default is bare 3003 or 3105-H14 with a 3 mil polyfilm moisture barrier at about 0.016 – 0.024 in (0.40 – 0.60 mm). Thicker jacketing, around 0.032 in (0.80 mm) and above, is used on large diameters, soft (non-rigid) insulation that cannot support the jacket, and areas exposed to impact or foot traffic. Check the thickness table in the current edition of the standard and in the project specification, which may be stricter.

ASTM C1729 also covers emittance, because the surface finish affects the jacket's surface temperature; see the emittance note below.

Options

Surface finishes and moisture barriers

Smooth (mill finish)

Clean, bright appearance, the lowest cost and easy to keep clean. Shows dents and scratches more than textured finishes.

Stucco embossed

An orange-peel pattern that hides minor handling marks, reduces glare and slightly stiffens the sheet. Very common on process pipework and equipment.

Corrugated

Small or deep corrugations add stiffness for large vessels, tanks and ducts, and allow for thermal movement around large diameters.

Painted

Coil-coated colours for identification, appearance or higher surface emittance. Specify colour, paint system and film thickness.

Polyfilm moisture barrier

A thin coextruded polyethylene / Surlyn film heat-laminated to the inside face. Blocks crevice and pitting corrosion on the jacket's inner surface and isolates it from the pipe; absorbs almost no water.

Polykraft barrier

Kraft paper with a polyethylene layer. Low cost and a long track record, but the paper can absorb water and deteriorate if the jacket leaks, so polyfilm is preferred outdoors.

Why a moisture barrier matters: condensation or rain that gets behind the jacket can sit against the bare inner face, especially on cold lines and at overlaps. Without a barrier, aluminium in contact with wet insulation containing chlorides, or with a steel pipe, can pit or corrode galvanically. Most specifications now call for a factory-applied polyfilm barrier on all outdoor aluminium jacketing. For more on textured finishes see our stucco embossed coil guide.

Design note

Surface emittance and touch temperature

Bare aluminium reflects most infrared radiation, which means it also radiates heat poorly: its emittance is low compared with painted surfaces. On a hot line, a bare aluminium jacket therefore runs at a higher surface temperature than a painted or dull jacket over the same insulation, while heat loss changes only slightly. This matters when insulation is sized for personnel protection (keeping touchable surfaces below a safe temperature) or for condensation control on cold lines.

Insulation thickness calculations, for example to ASTM C680, use the emittance of the jacket as an input. If the calculation assumed a painted jacket and the site installs bare aluminium, surface temperatures can be higher than designed. Stating the jacket finish in the insulation calculation avoids this.

Good practice

Installation points that protect the jacket

  • Overlap direction: longitudinal seams are placed to shed water, typically on the side or lower quadrant away from the weather, and circumferential laps face downhill like roof tiles.
  • Seal penetrations: supports, valves, flanges and nozzles are where water gets in; seal them with compatible sealant and properly fitted caps.
  • Fastening: secure with bands or screws compatible with aluminium; avoid fasteners or bands of dissimilar metals touching the jacket without isolation.
  • Barrier facing in: install the moisture barrier against the insulation, never facing outward.
  • Inspection: damaged or open jacketing should be repaired promptly, because wet insulation leads directly to corrosion under insulation.
Logistics

Packing, transport and storage

The main risk to aluminium jacketing in transit and storage is water staining ("white rust"): moisture trapped between tightly wound wraps forms a white or grey oxide stain that cannot be polished out. It is a surface defect, but it spoils the appearance of a new installation.

  • Rolls are wrapped in film and heavy paper, packed with desiccant and fixed on wooden pallets or in cases with eye-to-sky or eye-to-wall orientation as agreed.
  • Keep coils dry during loading; never leave them in rain or on wet ground, and avoid moving cold coils into warm, humid air where condensation forms.
  • Do not stack loads on coreless rolls; they deform easily and become hard to install.
  • Store indoors in a ventilated, dry area off the floor, and re-seal partly used rolls in their packing.

Price for jacketing is built up from the aluminium price (LME plus regional premium), conversion cost for the thickness and temper, any embossing, corrugating, painting and moisture barrier, packing, and freight to the destination.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) insulation jacketing: FAQ

What thickness of aluminium jacketing should I use?

About 0.40 – 0.60 mm (0.016 – 0.024 in) is typical for pipe and small equipment over rigid insulation. Use about 0.80 mm (0.032 in) or more for large diameters, soft insulation and areas with impact or foot traffic, and follow the project specification.

Which alloy is standard for insulation jacketing?

3003 or 3105 in H14 temper is the usual industrial choice. 1100 suits light-duty indoor work; 5005 and 5052 are used for coastal, marine or visible applications.

Do I need a moisture barrier?

For outdoor work and cold or hot lines, yes. A factory-laminated polyfilm barrier prevents crevice, pitting and galvanic corrosion on the jacket's inner face and is called for by most current specifications.

What is the difference between smooth and stucco embossed jacketing?

Smooth jacketing has a bright, flat finish. Stucco embossed has an orange-peel texture that hides handling marks, reduces glare and is slightly stiffer.

Can aluminium jacketing be used for fire protection?

No. Aluminium melts at about 660 °C, so where a jacket must survive a fire, stainless steel jacketing or a fire-rated system is normally specified instead.

How do I prevent white staining on stored rolls?

Keep rolls dry, sealed and ventilated, avoid condensation from temperature changes, and re-wrap opened rolls. Water staining forms when moisture is trapped between wraps.
