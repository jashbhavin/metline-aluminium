Cast Ceramic Aluminium Matrix Composites (Cast Al-MMCs)

Cast aluminium matrix composites are casting alloys such as A356 or A359 that contain fine ceramic particles, usually silicon carbide (SiC), alumina or titanium diboride (TiB2) grown in the melt. The particles raise stiffness, strength, wear resistance and dimensional stability, while the part is still made by sand, permanent-mould, low-pressure, squeeze or die casting. This guide explains the material families, casting routes, typical properties against A356, design and machining rules and where cast Al-MMCs pay off.

Overview

What a cast Al-MMC is

A metal matrix composite combines a metal with a second, non-metallic phase. In aluminium matrix composites (Al-MMCs) the matrix is an aluminium alloy and the reinforcement is a ceramic: particles, short fibres or whiskers of silicon carbide, aluminium oxide, boron carbide or titanium diboride. Cast Al-MMCs are the subset produced by melting and casting, rather than by powder metallurgy or infiltration of a ceramic preform.

The appeal is simple. Ordinary aluminium casting alloys have an elastic modulus of about 70 GPa, the same as wrought aluminium. Adding 10 – 20 % by volume of SiC particles lifts the modulus to roughly 85 – 100 GPa, raises yield strength, halves wear rates in many sliding applications and lowers thermal expansion, while density rises only slightly. Because the composite is still cast, designers keep the freedom of complex, near-net shapes.

Two broad production families exist. Ex-situ composites are made by stirring pre-made ceramic particles, typically SiC of around 10 – 20 µm, into the molten alloy. In-situ composites grow the ceramic inside the melt by chemical reaction, most commonly TiB2 particles from salts containing titanium and boron. In-situ particles are much finer, often 50 – 300 nm, clean and well bonded to the matrix, which tends to preserve ductility better.

Why engineers specify cast Al-MMCs

  • Higher stiffness than any conventional cast aluminium, for parts that must not deflect or vibrate
  • Higher strength and fatigue resistance, sometimes enough to replace a forging with a casting
  • Lower thermal expansion and better dimensional stability for precision structures
  • Much better wear resistance for brake, piston and sliding parts
  • Near-net-shape casting, reducing machining and assembly

Properties

Typical properties compared with A356-T6

The figures below are representative published values for permanent-mould castings in T6 temper. They show the trade-off clearly: stiffness and strength rise with SiC content, while elongation falls. In-situ TiB2 composites sit between these, usually with a smaller stiffness gain but better ductility. Two practical points follow. First, the biggest single gain is stiffness, roughly 20 – 40 % above unreinforced aluminium; if a design is stiffness-limited, this is where MMCs shine. Second, the very low elongation of high-SiC grades means they are poor choices for parts that must absorb impact or tolerate misalignment; in-situ or low-fraction grades with higher ductility are preferred there.

Processing

Casting routes and what they mean for the part

Sand casting

Suits large or low-volume parts and prototypes. Slower cooling gives a coarser structure; particle settling must be controlled by keeping the melt stirred until pouring.

Gravity permanent mould

Good surface and properties for medium volumes; the reference process for much published MMC data.

Low-pressure and differential-pressure casting

Calm, bottom-up filling reduces oxide entrapment and porosity; widely used for wheels, chassis and structural MMC parts, followed by T6.

Squeeze casting

High pressure during solidification closes porosity, giving the best ductility and fatigue strength; used for safety-critical and high-stress parts.

High-pressure die casting

Fast and economical for thin-walled parts; some composite grades are designed to be used without heat treatment.

Investment casting

Fine detail and tight tolerances for small, complex precision parts such as optical and instrument housings.

  • Keep the melt gently stirred so particles do not settle or float
  • Avoid overheating SiC composites for long periods: aluminium can react with SiC to form brittle aluminium carbide (Al4C3), which high-silicon matrices help suppress
  • Degas with care and minimise turbulence; oxide films and porosity are more damaging in low-ductility composites
  • Segregate MMC returns from normal alloy scrap; composite scrap needs its own recycling route
  • Use filters and gating designed for higher-viscosity composite melts

Design

Design rules for cast Al-MMC components

  • Design for stiffness first: the modulus gain lets walls and ribs be thinner for the same deflection, which is where most weight savings come from
  • Respect low elongation: avoid sharp notches, add generous fillets and keep stress concentrations away from critical sections
  • Fatigue: fatigue strength is generally better than unreinforced alloys, but porosity and inclusions dominate; specify radiographic quality levels
  • Thermal matching: lower CTE helps when the part is bolted to steel, ceramics or optics; check the actual value for the grade
  • Joining: mechanical fastening and adhesive bonding are simplest; fusion welding is possible for some grades but can degrade the reinforcement, so qualify procedures
  • Corrosion: generally similar to the matrix alloy; apply the same coatings and anodising as for A356, with some loss of anodic film uniformity at high SiC content

Common mistakes with cast Al-MMCs

  • Substituting an MMC into an existing A356 design without redesigning: the stiffness gain is wasted and the low ductility becomes a liability
  • Using property data from a laboratory bar for a thick-walled sand casting with very different cooling
  • Ignoring machining cost: coarse SiC grades can cost more to machine than the casting itself if large allowances are left
  • Mixing composite returns into standard aluminium scrap, which contaminates the foundry's normal alloys
  • Specifying fusion welding on safety parts without a qualified procedure for the exact grade

A useful way to justify an MMC is to compare the cost of a forged and machined part, or a steel or titanium part, against a near-net composite casting with minimal machining. The material costs more per kilogram than A356, but less material and fewer operations can reduce the total part cost.

Machining

Machining cast Al-MMCs

Ceramic particles are harder than tungsten carbide grains, so SiC-reinforced composites wear conventional tools very quickly. Plan machining from the start, keep stock allowances small and finish as much as possible in the casting.

OperationRecommended toolingNotes
Turning and millingPolycrystalline diamond (PCD) insertsPCD lasts far longer than carbide on SiC composites; use moderate speeds and steady feeds
DrillingPCD-tipped or diamond-coated drillsAvoid dwelling; peck drilling in deep holes
TappingThread milling preferred; form taps are unsuitableConsider helical inserts for repeated assembly
GrindingDiamond wheelsSilicon carbide or alumina wheels wear rapidly
Non-traditionalWire EDM, waterjet, laserUseful for complex profiles and difficult features

In-situ TiB2 composites with sub-micron particles are much kinder to tools than coarse SiC grades, and many can be machined with carbide tooling, though tool life is still shorter than for unreinforced alloys.

Applications

Where cast Al-MMCs are used

Automotive chassis and brakes

Steering knuckles, control arms, subframes and wheel hubs replacing forgings or cast iron; brake discs and drums where wear resistance matters.

Engines and drivetrain

Pistons, cylinder liners, connecting rods and drive shafts, using wear resistance and stiffness.

Aerospace and space

Brackets, housings, optical benches and mechanisms where stiffness, low weight and thermal stability are needed, sometimes replacing titanium.

Rail transit

Brake discs, gearbox housings and bogie parts that need wear resistance and reduced unsprung mass.

Electronics and semiconductor equipment

Precision frames, stages and wire-bonding or welding machine parts that must stay dimensionally stable; AlSiC for heat-spreader bases.

Industrial machinery

Robot arms, machine-tool slides and high-speed rotating parts where lower deflection improves accuracy and speed.

Ordering

How to specify and source cast Al-MMC parts

  1. Define targetsStiffness, strength, elongation, fatigue, wear, thermal expansion and operating temperature, plus the part's current material.
  2. Choose the systemSiC grades for stiffness and wear, in-situ TiB2 or low-fraction grades for ductility, and the matrix alloy.
  3. Select the casting routeBased on size, volume, wall thickness and integrity requirements.
  4. Agree testingTensile (Rm, Rp0.2, A, E), hardness, radiography or CT, penetrant inspection, metallography for particle distribution, fatigue if critical.
  5. Prototype and qualifyCast trial parts, test and adjust design before series supply.
  6. DocumentationMaterial certificates (EN 10204 3.1) and inspection reports with each delivery; protective packing with VCI film and desiccant for sea freight.

FAQ

Cast aluminium (aluminum) matrix composites: FAQ

What is a cast aluminium matrix composite?

An aluminium casting alloy containing fine ceramic particles such as SiC, Al2O3 or TiB2. The particles raise stiffness, strength and wear resistance while the part is still produced by conventional casting.

How much stiffer is an Al-SiC casting than A356?

With about 20 % SiC by volume the modulus rises from about 72 GPa to roughly 99 GPa, an increase of around 35 – 40 %. A 10 % SiC grade reaches about 86 GPa.

Can a cast Al-MMC replace a forged aluminium part?

Sometimes. In-situ and squeeze-cast composite grades can reach forging-like strength with adequate ductility, which allows near-net casting of parts such as steering knuckles. Each case must be validated by testing, especially fatigue and impact.

What is the difference between ex-situ and in-situ composites?

Ex-situ composites have pre-made ceramic particles, usually micron-sized SiC, stirred into the melt. In-situ composites grow fine ceramic particles such as TiB2 inside the melt by reaction, giving cleaner interfaces and usually better ductility.

Are Al-MMCs hard to machine?

SiC-reinforced grades are very abrasive and need PCD or diamond tooling. Fine in-situ TiB2 grades are easier and can often be cut with carbide tools. Keeping machining allowances small saves cost.

Can Al-MMC castings be welded or recycled?

Some grades can be welded with qualified procedures, but mechanical joining is simpler. Composite scrap must be kept separate from ordinary aluminium scrap so the ceramic does not contaminate standard alloys.
