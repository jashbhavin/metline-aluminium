Porous Aluminium Foam Sheet & Plate

Porous aluminium foam sheet is solid aluminium with 60–90 % of its volume replaced by gas cells, giving a rigid, non-combustible plate that weighs a fraction of solid metal. Open-cell foam absorbs sound and lets air or fluid pass; closed-cell foam insulates, blocks sound and soaks up impact energy. This guide explains how to choose between them, what the property data mean, and how to specify foam panels for supply from Metline.

Overview

What is porous aluminium foam?

Aluminium foam is a cellular metal: a continuous aluminium skeleton surrounding a network of pores. It keeps the things that make aluminium useful (stiffness, non-combustibility, corrosion resistance, recyclability) while adding properties solid metal does not have, such as sound absorption, low thermal conductivity and the ability to crush progressively and absorb a large amount of energy at a nearly constant force.

Two numbers describe any foam: porosity (the share of the volume that is empty, usually 60–90 %) and cell size (the average pore diameter, usually 0.5–10 mm). Density follows from porosity. A foam with 90 % porosity has a density of about 0.27 g/cm³, roughly one tenth of solid aluminium (2.70 g/cm³) and light enough to float on water. Most commercial plate falls between 0.2 and 0.7 g/cm³ for closed-cell grades, with denser open-cell grades used where strength or filtration matters.

The other key distinction is whether the cells are connected. In open-cell foam the pores link to each other and to the surface, so sound waves, air and liquids can enter. In closed-cell foam each pore is sealed by thin cell walls, so the plate is airtight, buoyant and a good insulator. The two behave so differently that they should be treated as separate materials when you specify them.

Why engineers specify aluminium foam plate

  • Very low weight for its stiffness, especially as the core of a sandwich panel
  • Non-combustible and gives off no toxic smoke, unlike polymer foams and mineral-fibre absorbers
  • Does not absorb moisture, shed fibres or rot, so it suits wet, clean and outdoor spaces
  • Crushes progressively under impact, absorbing energy at a controlled force level
  • Can be sawn, drilled, bonded and painted with ordinary workshop tools, and is fully recyclable

Open-cell foam is commonly offered in three cell-size classes (large, medium and small), and as panels open on one face or on both faces. A panel open on both faces absorbs sound from both sides and lets air through; a panel open on one face has a closed back skin and is used as a wall or ceiling lining. Closed-cell foam can be supplied with its natural cast skin, with the skin machined off to expose the cells, or perforated to add sound absorption.

Plateau strength scales roughly with relative density to the power 1.5, so a small change in density changes strength a lot. Always quote density with any strength figure.

The compressive stress–strain curve of a closed-cell foam is what makes it valuable for protection. After a short elastic region, the cells buckle and collapse one layer after another at a nearly flat "plateau" stress, until the foam is crushed to about 30–40 % of its original thickness and stiffens. The area under that plateau is the energy absorbed. Designers pick the density so that the plateau stress keeps the force on the protected structure, or on a vehicle occupant, below a safe limit while the stroke absorbs the required energy.

Production

How aluminium foam sheet and plate are produced

Melt foaming with blowing agent

A metal hydride (usually titanium hydride, TiH2) is stirred into molten aluminium thickened with calcium. The hydride releases hydrogen and the melt rises like bread. The block is cooled and sliced into plate. Produces fine, uniform closed cells; a slow-release blowing agent gives more time for even foaming.

Gas injection

Air or another gas is blown into an aluminium melt containing ceramic particles (such as SiC or Al2O3) that stabilise the bubbles. The foam is drawn off continuously as a slab. Cost-effective for large closed-cell panels with coarser cells.

Powder metallurgy

Aluminium powder and a blowing agent are compacted into a dense precursor, which expands when heated in a mould. Suited to shaped parts, foam-filled tubes and foam cores bonded metallurgically to aluminium skins.

Infiltration (space holder)

Molten aluminium is pressed into a bed of removable particles, typically salt grains, which are dissolved out afterwards. Gives open-cell foam with a cell size set by the particle size, from fine to coarse.

Investment casting and sintering

A polymer foam pattern is replicated in aluminium, or aluminium powder or fibres are sintered. Produces very regular open-cell structures for heat exchangers and filters.

Hollow spheres

Aluminium or ceramic hollow spheres are bonded or cast into a matrix to make a syntactic or sphere foam with very uniform cells.

Melt-foamed material shows a slight density gradient from top to bottom, because gravity drains liquid metal down the cell walls before the foam solidifies. Producers control this with melt viscosity and foaming time, and by slicing plate from the uniform middle of the block. If density uniformity matters for your design, ask for density measured on samples from several positions.

Foam panels are made to order. State the property you need (sound absorption, insulation, energy absorption) as well as the size, so the right type and density can be matched.

  1. Choose the typeOpen-cell for sound absorption, ventilation, filtration and heat dissipation; closed-cell for insulation, impact absorption and stiff light cores.
  2. Set density and cell sizeHigher density raises strength and stiffness; finer cells give more uniform behaviour and better acoustic absorption at high frequency.
  3. Fix thickness and sizeAcoustic absorption at low frequency improves with thickness and an air gap behind the panel.
  4. Specify surface and edgesNatural skin, open faces, perforation pattern, coating colour and edge trimming.
  5. Agree testsDensity, compression to ISO 13314, sound absorption to ISO 354 or ISO 10534-2, and fire classification where required.

Working with foam

Cutting, joining and finishing foam panels

Cutting

Band saws, circular saws with fine-tooth carbide blades, and waterjet all cut foam cleanly. Support the panel close to the cut so thin cell walls are not torn.

Drilling and fixing

Drill with sharp bits at moderate feed. Use large washers, inserts or bonded bushes, because cell walls cannot take high point loads from screws.

Sandwich panels

Bonding aluminium sheet to both faces of a closed-cell core produces a very stiff, light panel. Structural adhesives are common; powder-metallurgy foams can be bonded metallurgically to skins.

Coating

Spray paint and powder coating are both used. On acoustic panels, use a thin coat that does not close the open pores, or the absorption falls.

Corrosion

Aluminium foam resists normal indoor and outdoor atmospheres. In marine or polluted environments, choose a coated finish and avoid trapping water in open cells.

Recycling

Foam is aluminium plus a small amount of additive and can be remelted with aluminium scrap at end of life.

In most of these uses aluminium foam is chosen because it combines several functions in one panel. A rail car floor panel, for example, can be stiff, light, sound-insulating and non-combustible at the same time, where a conventional design might need a structural panel plus a separate mineral-wool layer. That combination, rather than any single property, is usually what justifies foam over cheaper single-purpose materials.

Buyer checklist

Common mistakes when buying foam plate

  • Quoting strength without density. A strength figure means nothing unless the density it was measured at is stated; compare grades at the same density.
  • Using closed-cell foam as a sound absorber. Unperforated closed-cell plate reflects most sound. For absorption, use open-cell foam or perforated closed-cell panels with an air gap behind.
  • Ignoring the density gradient. Melt-foamed blocks are slightly denser at the bottom. For energy-absorbing parts, ask where in the block the plate was cut and request density checks.
  • Screwing directly into the cells. Point loads crush the cell walls. Plan bonded inserts, through-bolts with large washers, or adhesive joints from the start.
  • Sealing open pores with heavy paint. A thick coating turns an absorber into a reflector. Specify the coating with the acoustic test.
  • Comparing price per square metre only. Foam often replaces two or three layers (structure, insulation, fire barrier). Compare the cost of the whole build-up, not one layer.

A short sample trial avoids most of these problems. Ask for a sample panel of the proposed density and finish, check it with your fixing method and, for acoustic or impact work, test it in the real mounting arrangement before committing to the full quantity.

FAQ

Porous aluminium (aluminum) foam plate: FAQ

What is the difference between open-cell and closed-cell aluminium foam?

Open-cell foam has interconnected pores, so it absorbs sound and lets air or liquid through. Closed-cell foam has sealed pores, so it is airtight and buoyant, insulates against heat and sound, and absorbs impact energy. Choose by function, not by appearance.

How light is aluminium foam?

Most closed-cell plate weighs 0.2–0.7 g/cm³, about one quarter to one tenth of solid aluminium. A 1 m² panel 20 mm thick at 0.4 g/cm³ weighs about 8 kg.

Is aluminium foam fireproof?

Aluminium foam is a non-combustible metal and does not give off toxic smoke. Like all aluminium it softens and melts at high temperature (around 600–660 °C), so for fire doors and partitions it is used within a tested system. Coated panels need their own fire classification.

How is aluminium foam cut and installed?

With ordinary saws, waterjet or CNC routers. Panels are fixed with bonded inserts, large washers, clips or adhesives, because thin cell walls cannot take concentrated screw loads.

Can aluminium foam be painted without losing sound absorption?

Yes, if the coating is thin enough not to close the open pores. Heavy paint layers seal the surface and reduce absorption, so acoustic panels use light spray coats or factory finishes.

How do I choose the right foam density?

Start from the job. For impact absorption, pick the density whose plateau stress keeps transmitted force below the limit while absorbing the required energy. For acoustics, cell size and thickness matter more than density. For sandwich cores, higher density gives more shear strength.
