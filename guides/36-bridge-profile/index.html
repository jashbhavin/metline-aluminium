36 Aluminium Bridge Extrusion Profiles: Section Types Explained

An aluminium bridge, whether a footbridge, a portable military bridge or a floating pontoon crossing, is built from a family of extruded sections: girders, chords and cross-beams that carry load, and deck planks, edge closures and handrails that complete it. This guide walks through 36 typical sections, what each does and which alloy and temper it is normally extruded in.

Overview

Why bridges are built from extrusions

Aluminium weighs about a third as much as steel, does not rust, and can be extruded into hollow multi-cell shapes that put material exactly where it is needed. For bridges, that means light spans that can be lifted in one piece, quick to install and close to maintenance-free.

The extrusion process lets designers combine several functions in one section: a girder with integral bolt channels, a deck plank with interlocking edges and an anti-slip top, or a pontoon wall with built-in stiffeners. Fewer parts and fewer welds reduce fabrication cost and fatigue risk. The sections below fall into two groups: primary members that carry the bridge loads, and secondary members that form the walking surface, parapets and finishes.

Load-bearing structure

Sections 1 – 17: main load-bearing members

These members are designed to EN 1999 (Eurocode 9) or the Aluminum Design Manual and AASHTO LRFD, and are extruded in structural 6xxx alloys in T6 temper. Multi-cell closed sections dominate because they resist torsion and local buckling far better than open shapes of the same weight.

Deck and finishes

Sections 18 – 36: decks, parapets and trims

Secondary sections still carry pedestrian or vehicle loads locally (deck planks and treads in particular), but many are architectural. 6063 is preferred for trims and handrails because it extrudes thin walls with a fine surface that anodises or powder-coats well.

Engineering

Design and fabrication considerations

  • Stiffness, not strength, often governs: aluminium's modulus (about 70 GPa) is a third of steel's, so deflection and vibration limits on footbridges usually set section depth
  • Fatigue: traffic and pedestrian-induced loading make weld details critical; EN 1999-1-3 gives detail categories, and bolted or friction-stir-welded joints often perform better
  • Friction stir welding: multi-cell deck planks are commonly joined edge to edge by FSW into wide, flat panels with low distortion
  • Thermal movement: about 23 mm per 10 m per 10 °C range; expansion joints (section 17) and sliding bearings accommodate it
  • Galvanic isolation: separate aluminium from steel bearings and concrete abutments with isolating pads
  • Execution: fabrication follows EN 1090-3 execution classes in Europe; welders and procedures are qualified to ISO 9606-2 and ISO 15614-2

Applications

Where these sections are used

Pedestrian and cycle bridges

Prefabricated truss or girder spans lifted in one piece over roads, rail and rivers.

Bridge deck replacement

Light aluminium decks on existing steel structures, raising load rating by cutting dead weight.

Floating and pontoon bridges

Modular pontoons with aluminium decks for marinas, temporary crossings and emergency use.

Military and portable bridges

Rapidly deployed gap-crossing bridges in high-strength weldable 7xxx and 6xxx alloys.

Access bridges and gangways

Ship-to-shore, offshore and industrial access gangways.

Stairs and ramps

Interlocking treads and handrails for bridge approaches and platforms.

FAQ

Aluminium bridge extrusions: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium alloy is best for bridge girders?

6082-T6 in Europe and 6061-T6 elsewhere are the usual choices for main girders; 6005A-T6 is preferred for complex multi-cell sections such as deck planks. 7020 is used where higher weldable strength is needed.

How long can aluminium bridge extrusions be?

Commonly 6 – 12 m per piece, limited by the extrusion press, ageing ovens and transport. Longer members are spliced by bolting or welding.

Why are bridge deck sections multi-cell?

Closed cells resist local buckling and torsion, carry wheel loads between webs and let wide planks be friction-stir welded into flat panels.

Do aluminium bridges need painting?

Usually not. Most are left mill finish or anodised; coatings are used for colour or appearance.

What design code applies to aluminium bridges?

EN 1999 (Eurocode 9) in Europe and many other regions, and AASHTO LRFD with the Aluminum Design Manual in North America.

Can you supply profiles to our own drawings?

Yes. Send the section drawings with alloy, temper, length and finish, and we will confirm die and supply options.
