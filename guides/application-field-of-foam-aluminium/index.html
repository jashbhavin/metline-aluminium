Aluminium Foam: Types, Properties and Applications

Aluminium foam is solid aluminium filled with gas pores, so that 60 – 90 % of its volume is empty space. It weighs a fraction of solid metal, crushes at a steady stress that soaks up impact energy, absorbs sound when its cells are open, and does not burn. This guide explains the main foam types, the properties that matter, where each type is used and how to specify it.

Overview

What is aluminium foam?

Aluminium foam (also called foamed aluminium, foam aluminium or porous aluminium) is a cellular metal: a network of thin aluminium walls or struts enclosing pores of air. Its bulk density is typically 0.2 – 0.8 g/cm³, against 2.70 g/cm³ for solid aluminium, so a foam panel can be a tenth to a third of the weight of a solid plate of the same thickness.

The pores change how the metal behaves. Solid aluminium is stiff and strong but reflects sound, conducts heat quickly and transmits impact loads almost unchanged. A foam bends and buckles cell by cell when compressed, so the stress stays nearly constant while the material shortens by half or more. That long, flat stress plateau is what makes foam an efficient energy absorber. If the cells are interconnected, sound waves enter the pores and lose energy to air friction, which makes open-cell foam a good sound absorber.

Foam is not a substitute for sheet, plate or extrusions in load-bearing members. It earns its place when combined with them: as the core of a sandwich panel, as a filler inside a thin-walled tube, as an acoustic lining behind a perforated skin, or as a crushable insert that limits the force reaching something fragile. Choosing the right type, density and cell size for that job is most of the specification work.

What aluminium foam offers

  • Low weight with useful stiffness when used as a sandwich core
  • Progressive crushing that absorbs impact and blast energy in every direction
  • Sound absorption (open and opened cells) and vibration damping
  • Non-combustible metallic structure with no toxic smoke from the foam itself
  • Does not rot, swell or absorb water into the metal; fully recyclable with aluminium scrap
Foam types

Types of aluminium foam and how they differ

Foams are grouped first by cell structure, because that decides what they are good at. The three families below cover almost every commercial product; a fourth, very thin "semi-transparent" grade is a sliced open-cell foam used mainly for decoration.

Open-cell foam

Made by casting aluminium around a removable salt or sand space-holder that is then leached out, or by infiltrating a pattern. Because the pores connect, it behaves like a rigid, non-combustible acoustic sponge. Strength is lower than closed-cell foam of the same density.

Closed-cell foam

Molten aluminium is thickened (for example with calcium or ceramic particles) and foamed with a gas-releasing agent or injected gas, then solidified as a block and sliced; alternatively a powder precursor is foamed inside a mould. Sealed cells give higher stiffness and plateau stress, so this is the structural and crash grade.

Spherical-pore foam

Produced by casting around hollow or removable spheres, giving round pores of very even size. The uniform structure produces a predictable crush response and the small windows between spheres add a measure of sound absorption.

Properties

Key properties of aluminium foam

Almost every property of a foam scales with its relative density: the foam density divided by the density of the solid alloy. Doubling density raises compressive strength roughly three- to four-fold, so small changes in density matter. The table summarises what to expect from commercial foams.

PropertyTypical rangeWhat it means in practice
Density0.2 – 0.8 g/cm³ (spherical-pore up to about 1.1)Specify a band, for example 0.40 ± 0.04 g/cm³
Porosityabout 60 – 92 %Higher porosity means lighter, weaker and (if open) more sound-absorbing
Compressive plateau stressabout 1 – 17 MPa, rising steeply with densitySets the force at which the foam crushes; choose it below the load the protected structure can take
Densification strainroughly 50 – 70 %Only this fraction of the thickness is usable crush stroke
Tensile and bending strengtha few MPaFoam alone is weak in tension; use it between skins or inside tubes
Sound absorption (open cells)Peak coefficients near 0.9 or more are reported for thick open-cell panelsPerformance improves with an air gap behind the panel, especially at low frequencies
Thermal conductivityA few W/m·K, far below solid aluminiumSlows heat flow, but foam is not an insulation material in the sense of mineral wool
Fire behaviourMetallic and non-combustibleSoftens and melts near the melting range of the parent alloy; adhesives and coatings must be assessed separately
CorrosionSimilar to the parent alloy, but with a large exposed surface on cut facesSeal or coat foam in wet or saline service

Values are drawn from published literature on commercial foams (see references). Foam properties scatter more than those of solid alloys, so design values should come from tests on the specified product, not from a range table.

Two property claims are often overstated in sales literature. First, closed-cell foam on its own is a poor sound absorber: sealed cells reflect sound much like a solid plate, and good absorption needs open cells, cracked cell walls or a perforated face. Second, electromagnetic shielding and thermal values vary widely with thickness and structure, so ask for measured data rather than relying on a headline figure.

Foam is produced as blocks and then sliced, machined or laminated into the form the application needs. Common forms and size ranges are listed below; sizes vary by production route, so treat them as a starting point for an enquiry.

Surface condition matters as much as size. A slab can be supplied with both faces cut (cells open on both sides), one face cut and one with the natural cast skin, or with the closed skin on both faces. Cut faces look more uniform and absorb more sound; skinned faces are smoother, easier to bond and better sealed against moisture.

Pore size classApproximate pore sizeTypical behaviour and use
Small pore0.5 – 3 mmHigh specific surface; best sound absorption without an air gap; fine, even appearance for interiors
Medium pore3 – 8 mmBalanced strength, energy absorption and acoustic performance; the most common general-purpose class
Large pore8 – 12 mm and aboveLowest density and highest permeability; used for filtration, shock absorption and bold decorative surfaces

Pore size and density are separate choices. Two foams of the same density can have very different pore sizes, and the finer one usually has thinner walls, a smoother cut surface and more uniform crushing. When appearance matters, approve a physical sample, because pore size varies across a cast block.

Typical configurations

How foam is typically built into a product

Traffic noise barrier

A barrier module usually combines a structural aluminium frame, a rear reflective panel and a front layer of open-cell or perforated closed-cell foam, 5 – 30 mm thick. Unlike mineral-wool infill, the foam does not sag, rot or lose absorption when wet, and it can be washed.

Rail vehicle floor

A common build-up is thin aluminium sheet on both faces of a closed-cell foam core 15 – 30 mm thick. The sandwich gives a stiff, quiet floor that does not absorb moisture and replaces plywood with a non-combustible panel.

Crash-box filler

A 6xxx extrusion is filled with closed-cell foam. The foam supports the tube walls as they fold, so more energy is absorbed per unit length. Useful where the crash zone is short.

Acoustic ceiling

Open-cell or thin sliced foam tiles hung with an air gap behind them. The air gap lowers the frequency at which absorption peaks, improving control of speech and machinery noise.

Equipment shock mount

A machined foam block placed under sensitive equipment crushes at a known stress, limiting deceleration during a drop or landing.

Decorative screen

Thin semi-transparent foam is framed in aluminium profiles to make light-diffusing partitions, lamp shades and facade features.

Working with foam

Design, cutting, joining and finishing

  • Choose density from the load, not the brochure: work out the force the protected structure can tolerate and select the density whose plateau stress sits below it.
  • Allow crush stroke: plan on using only about half to two-thirds of the foam thickness before it densifies and the force rises sharply.
  • Cutting: band saws, circular saws with fine teeth, waterjet and wire EDM all work. Use sharp tools and light feeds to avoid smearing cell walls; wire EDM gives the cleanest edges on precision parts.
  • Joining: structural adhesives, mechanical fasteners with inserts or through-bolts, and foaming in place inside tubes are the usual methods. Do not fusion weld directly onto foam; weld the skins, frames or brackets instead.
  • Fastening: foam crushes under point loads, so use large washers, bonded inserts or potted hard points where screws carry load.
  • Finishing: powder coating, liquid paint and anodising are possible; sealing cut faces is recommended outdoors and in saline air. Acoustic panels should be painted with a thin, non-bridging finish so the pores stay open.
  • Galvanic isolation: separate foam from carbon fibre, copper and steel with insulating layers, as with any aluminium part.

For sandwich panels, the face sheets carry bending stresses and the foam carries shear. Typical face materials are 3003, 5052 and 5754 sheet for general panels and 6061 or 6082 where higher strength is needed. Metline supplies these sheet and coil grades alongside foam, so the core and the faces can be sourced together.

Ordering

How to specify and order aluminium foam

  1. Define the functionAcoustic absorption, energy absorption, stiffness, decoration or a combination, with the operating environment (indoor, outdoor, wet, saline, hot).
  2. Select type and densityOpen, closed or spherical-pore; a density band; a pore-size range if appearance or acoustics depend on it.
  3. Give dimensions and surfaceThickness, length and width or a drawing; cut, skinned or perforated faces; coating and colour.
  4. Agree tests and documentsDensity and dimensional checks on every lot; compression tests to ISO 13314; sound absorption to ISO 354 or ISO 10534 where acoustic performance is specified; reaction-to-fire report where required.
  5. Packing and deliveryFoam faces are easily dented, so panels are interleaved and packed in cartons or wooden cases. Delivery on EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP terms to your port or site.

Prices depend mainly on density, thickness, panel size, surface treatment, quantity and any lamination. Because foam is often used in projects, it helps to share the total area or number of parts, the delivery schedule and any samples or approval steps your client requires.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) foam: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between open-cell and closed-cell aluminium foam?

In open-cell foam the pores connect, so air and sound pass through; it is used for sound absorption, filtration and heat exchange. In closed-cell foam each pore is sealed; it is stiffer and stronger at the same density and is used for energy absorption and sandwich cores.

How heavy is aluminium foam?

Most commercial foams weigh 0.2 – 0.8 g/cm³, so a 20 mm panel weighs about 4 – 16 kg per square metre, against 54 kg for a solid 20 mm aluminium plate.

Does aluminium foam burn?

No. The foam is metallic and non-combustible and gives off no smoke of its own. It softens and melts near the melting range of the aluminium alloy. Adhesives, coatings and laminated facings must be assessed separately for fire performance.

Is aluminium foam good for soundproofing?

It is good for sound absorption (reducing echo and reverberation) when its cells are open or the panel is perforated. Blocking sound transmission through a wall depends mainly on mass and construction, so foam is usually combined with a solid layer for that purpose.

Can aluminium foam be used outdoors?

Yes. It is widely used in road and rail noise barriers. It does not absorb water into the metal or lose absorption when wet; coating or sealing cut faces is recommended in coastal or polluted environments.

Can aluminium foam be welded?

Not directly in a practical way. Foam is joined by adhesives, fasteners with inserts, or by foaming it in place. In sandwich panels and filled tubes, the solid aluminium skins or tubes are welded instead.
