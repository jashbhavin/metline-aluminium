Aluminium Round Tube Dies: Types, Size Chart & Ordering Guide

Every extruded aluminium round tube is defined by its die: the outside diameter comes from the die aperture and the wall from the gap between the die and a mandrel. Choosing a diameter and wall that the industry already has tooling for avoids a die charge and a trial run, while a non-standard size means designing a new die. This guide explains porthole and seamless tube dies, gives metric and inch size charts with calculated weights, and shows how alloy, tolerance and press size decide what can be made.

Overview

What a round tube die is, and why the size you pick matters

A round tube die is a set of hardened hot-work tool steel parts, almost always H13 (1.2344) or a premium variant of it, that shapes a heated aluminium billet into a hollow circular section. Unlike a solid bar die, a tube die has two working surfaces: the outer die cap that sets the outside diameter (OD) and an inner mandrel that sets the inside diameter (ID). The gap between them is the wall thickness, and the accuracy of that gap, all the way round, decides whether the tube comes out concentric.

Because both diameter and wall are fixed by the tooling, every combination needs its own die. A Ø50 × 3 mm tube and a Ø50 × 4 mm tube are two different tools, and so are Ø50.8 mm (2 in) and Ø50 mm. Buyers who are new to extrusion often assume tube is cut from a continuous range, the way sheet is cut from coil; in reality the available sizes are a library of discrete dies built up over decades by extruders around the world.

That has a direct commercial consequence. When the OD and wall on your drawing match a die that already exists, production can start from proven tooling, with known straightness and tolerance behaviour and no tooling invoice. When they do not, a new die must be designed, machined, heat-treated, nitrided and trialled, and the first trial often needs correction before the tube is accepted. For small and medium orders, the die cost and the trial time can outweigh the metal cost, which is why it pays to check a standard size chart before freezing a design.

Tooling is only half the story. The same die can run several alloys, but not all of them equally well, and some alloys are better made as seamless tube on a different type of press. Tolerance class, temper, surface finish and length also influence which route is used. The sections below take each of these in turn.

Five checks before you fix a round tube size

  • Pick an OD and wall from the charts below, or from the inch chart if your drawing is in inches
  • Decide whether the tube must be seamless (pressure, hydroforming, some aerospace and pipe specifications) or whether porthole tube is acceptable
  • Check that the alloy suits the route: 6xxx alloys run well through porthole dies, while 2024 and 7075 are normally made seamless
  • State the tolerance standard (EN 755-8, EN 755-7, ASTM B221 or a drawing value) so the right die and process are chosen
  • If you need a tighter OD than extrusion gives, ask whether the nearest standard size can be cold drawn to your dimension
Tooling types

Porthole, bridge and die-and-mandrel tooling compared

Hollow aluminium tube is extruded by two fundamentally different methods. Which one is used depends on the alloy, the size, the end use and the specification on the purchase order.

Porthole die

The most common tube die. The billet is split by the bridges of a mandrel assembly into several streams that pass through ports, flow around the mandrel and weld back together in a welding chamber under high pressure before leaving the die. A solid billet is used, so standard presses can run it.

Bridge and spider dies

Variants of the same principle. In a bridge die the mandrel is supported by a bridge across the die; in a spider die by radial arms. Large-diameter tubes often use bridge-type tooling. All of them produce longitudinal seam welds where the streams rejoin.

Die-and-mandrel (seamless)

A hollow billet, cast hollow or pierced, is pushed over a mandrel that sits in the die aperture. The metal never separates, so there is no seam weld. This method needs a press with a piercing or mandrel system and is used for seamless tube and pipe.

Multi-cavity dies

Small tubes are often extruded through dies with two, four or more openings at once. This raises output and is one reason small standard sizes are economical; it also means small new sizes may be designed as multi-cavity tools.

Drawing dies and plugs

Cold drawing is a separate step after extrusion. The tube is pulled through a carbide or hardened steel die over a plug or mandrel to reduce OD and wall, tighten tolerances and improve the surface. Drawing tooling is much cheaper than an extrusion die.

Feeder and step dies

A feeder plate in front of the die controls metal flow and can allow a slightly larger tube than the container would otherwise permit. Step dies change section along the length and are a specialist product, not a stock item.

The seam welds in porthole tube are solid-state bonds formed by pressure and temperature, not fusion welds. In 6xxx alloys made with a well-designed die, they are sound and the tube meets the same tensile minimums as seamless material in most structural uses. Problems arise when the welding chamber pressure is too low, when the alloy is hard to bond (2xxx, high-strength 7xxx and high-magnesium 5xxx), or when the tube is later expanded, flared or hydroformed, which concentrates strain at the seams. That is why many specifications for pressure pipe and hydroformed parts require seamless tube, and why the purchase order should say so explicitly.

How dies are made

How a new round tube die is made, and what limits the size

  1. DesignThe die designer sets the number of ports, the bridge shape, the welding chamber height and the bearing lengths so that metal reaches the die gap at even speed all round. Uneven flow is what causes eccentric walls, wavy tube and poor seam welds.
  2. MachiningThe mandrel and cap are rough-machined from H13 bar, heat-treated to high hardness, then finish-machined and spark-eroded (EDM) to final dimensions. Bearing surfaces are polished.
  3. NitridingThe working surfaces are nitrided to give a hard, wear-resistant case. Dies are re-nitrided periodically during their life.
  4. TrialThe die is run on the press with production alloy. The first tube is measured for OD, wall at several points, ovality and straightness, and sectioned to check the seam welds.
  5. CorrectionIf the wall is uneven or the tube bows, the die maker adjusts bearings or flow paths and the die is trialled again. Simple round tubes usually settle quickly; very thin walls and large diameters take more correction.
  6. Production and maintenanceThe die is cleaned in caustic soda after each run, inspected, re-nitrided when needed and stored for repeat orders. Wear gradually enlarges the bearings, and the die is retired when it can no longer hold tolerance.

Die life varies with alloy, section and press practice, so it is quoted as an estimate rather than a fixed number. Soft alloys such as 6063 are kind to tooling; 6082, 6061 and 7xxx alloys are harder on bearings; thick walls with low extrusion ratios are easier than thin walls on large diameters.

What can be extruded is limited by the press. The OD must fit within the die diameter the press accepts, and the force needed rises with the cross-sectional reduction and the alloy's flow stress. Very large tubes, above about 300 mm OD, are made on large presses with big containers or by spreading techniques, and the range of available wall thicknesses narrows. At the other extreme, very small diameters below about 8 – 10 mm are often extruded slightly oversize and cold drawn to final dimension.

Wall-to-OD ratio

Very thin walls on a large diameter are hard to fill evenly and tend to go oval; very thick walls on a small diameter leave little room for a strong mandrel. Mid-range ratios run fastest and most consistently.

Minimum wall

For soft 6xxx alloys on small tubes, walls around 0.8 – 1 mm are practical by extrusion; thinner walls are normally achieved by cold drawing. Harder alloys need thicker minimum walls.

Mandrel strength

The mandrel is supported only by its bridges or by the press mandrel bar, so it deflects under load. That deflection is the main cause of wall eccentricity and the reason why designers keep bridges sturdy.

Extrusion ratio

If the reduction from container to tube is too small, the metal is under-worked and seam welds may be weak; if it is too large, pressure rises and speed falls. Extruders choose the press size to keep the ratio workable.

Length

Tube is typically cut to 3 m, 6 m or similar lengths at the press; longer lengths depend on the run-out table and stretcher. Coiled tube is a different product covered by its own standard.

Alloy behaviour

A die designed for 6063 may need different bearings for 6082 or 7075, so stating the alloy at the enquiry stage avoids surprises in trial.

Alloy selection

Which alloys run through round tube dies, and in which tempers

Most round tube is made from the 6xxx alloys because they flow easily, bond well in the welding chamber of a porthole die and reach good strength after press quenching and ageing. The table shows the usual route and tempers for each alloy family.

Typical tensile strengths illustrate the range: 6063-T6 is about 240 MPa, 6061-T6 about 310 MPa, 7005-T6 about 350 MPa and 7075-T6 about 570 MPa (Aluminum Association typical values). Specified minimum values for extruded tube are set by ASTM B221, ASTM B241 and EN 755-2, depend on wall thickness and are what the mill certificate reports. Design to the minimums, not the typical values.

Temper also affects tooling choice indirectly. T5 tube is quenched at the press exit by air or water mist, which suits thin-walled 6063 and 6060. Thick-walled 6061 and 6082 need an intense water quench to reach T6 through the wall, so they may be extruded on lines with water-wave quench or solution heat-treated separately. A tube that must be bent after delivery is often ordered in T4 or O and aged afterwards.

The "51" in T6511 means the tube was stretched about 1 – 3 % after quenching to relieve stress and straighten it, with minor straightening allowed afterwards. Stretched tempers move less when one end or side of the tube is machined.

Tolerances

Standards and tolerances for extruded and drawn round tube

Tolerance tables differ by process, so the standard on the order should match the route. In the European system, porthole and seamless tube have separate parts of EN 755, and drawn tube has its own parts of EN 754. In the American system, ASTM B221 covers extruded tube (including porthole tube), B241 covers seamless extruded tube and pipe, and B210 covers drawn seamless tube; dimensional tolerances for all of them are tabulated in ANSI H35.2.

The standards control OD (both the mean diameter and the diameter at any point, which together limit ovality), wall thickness (mean wall and wall at any point, which limit eccentricity), straightness, length and the squareness of cut ends. Tolerances widen as the diameter grows and are wider for the harder alloy group (2xxx, 5xxx with more than about 3 % Mg, and 7xxx) than for 6060, 6063 and 6082.

Drawn tube is markedly tighter than extruded tube and has a smoother surface, which is why hydraulic cylinders, telescopic tubes, precision sleeves and heat exchanger tubes are usually drawn. If a drawing gives an OD tolerance of a few hundredths of a millimetre, plan for cold drawing or machining rather than asking for it from the extrusion die.

When your size is not standard: drawing, machining or a new die

A size that is not in the charts is not necessarily a new die. There are four realistic routes, and the cheapest depends on quantity and tolerance.

Cold draw from a nearby size

If your OD is slightly smaller than a standard extruded size, or you need a thinner or more accurate wall, a standard tube can be drawn down. This is common for small diameters, heat exchanger tube and precision sleeves.

Machine the critical zone

For fit sections only a short length long, turning or honing a heavier standard tube can be cheaper than a die, especially for low quantities.

Use the nearest standard size

Many designs can accept 40 × 3 instead of 40 × 2.8, or a metric tube instead of an inch one. Asking the question early often removes the tooling cost entirely.

Open a new die

For repeat volume, a dedicated die is usually worth it. The buyer typically pays a one-time die charge; the die is then reserved for repeat orders. Terms for die ownership and any credit against volume are agreed in the quotation.

Secondary processing is often what turns a tube into a usable part. Common operations include precision cut-to-length with deburring and chamfering, CNC bending (single or multi-radius, which is easiest in 6063-T5 or T4 tempers), end forming such as flaring, swaging and expansion, drilling, tapping and slotting to drawing, and MIG, TIG or brazed joints. For porthole tube that will be expanded or flared heavily, discuss the seam position and the forming strain at the enquiry stage.

Applications

Typical round tube applications and the usual specification

  • Handrails and balustrades: 6063-T5 or T6 in sizes such as Ø38 × 2, Ø42.4 × 2.5 or Ø50 × 2, anodised or powder coated, made as porthole tube to EN 755-8.
  • HVAC and heat exchangers: 3003 or 6063 tube around Ø19.05 (3/4 in) or Ø12.7 (1/2 in), often cold drawn to tight OD for brazed joints and expansion into fins.
  • Fitness, furniture and medical equipment: 6061-T6 or 6063-T6 in Ø25 – 60 mm, with bending, drilling and powder coating.
  • Bicycle and sports frames: 6061 and 7005 tube, frequently drawn or butted after extrusion to vary the wall along the length.
  • Structures, scaffolding and trailers: 6082-T6 heavier-wall tube such as Ø48.3 × 4, designed to EN 1999 (Eurocode 9) or the Aluminum Design Manual.
  • Pressure lines and hydroformed parts: seamless 6061, 6082 or 5083 tube to EN 755-7 or ASTM B241.
  • Aerospace and defence: seamless 2024, 6061 and 7075 tube to AMS specifications, with ultrasonic inspection where required.
  • Large-diameter rollers and housings: 6061 or 6082 tube above 200 mm OD for printing rollers, conveyor drums and enclosures, usually machined after extrusion.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) round tube dies: frequently asked questions

Do I pay a die charge for a standard round tube size?

Usually not. Where the OD and wall match tooling that already exists for the alloy and route, the tube is priced on metal, processing and finish only. A die charge applies when a new size needs new tooling; it is quoted separately and agreed before the die is made.

What is the difference between porthole tube and seamless tube?

Porthole tube is extruded from a solid billet through a die that splits and rewelds the metal around a mandrel, leaving longitudinal seam welds. Seamless tube is extruded from a hollow billet over a mandrel, so it has no seams. Seamless tube is specified for pressure service, hydroforming and many aerospace uses; porthole tube is standard for structures, frames and architectural work.

Can a 6063 die be used for 6061 or 6082 tube?

Often, but not always. The aperture defines the size, but bearings and flow paths are tuned for an alloy. Harder alloys need more pressure and may fill the die differently, so the die may need adjustment or a separate tool. Always give the alloy when you ask whether a size is available.

How do I get a tighter tolerance than extrusion gives?

Choose cold-drawn tube (EN 754-7/-8 or ASTM B210), which is drawn through a carbide die over a plug after extrusion, or machine the critical length. Extrusion dies alone cannot reliably hold the few-hundredths tolerances used on hydraulic cylinders or precision sleeves.

What is the largest round tube that can be extruded?

Tubes of several hundred millimetres in diameter are extruded on very large presses, and the EN 755-7/-8 tolerance standards cover diameters up to 450 mm. Above roughly 300 mm the choice of walls and alloys narrows, and tubes are often machined after extrusion. Send the size and we will confirm feasibility.

Who owns a new tube die?

Practice varies. Commonly the buyer pays a one-time die charge and the die is reserved for that buyer's repeat orders, while the extruder stores and maintains it. Ownership, confidentiality and any credit of the die cost against volume should be written into the quotation.
