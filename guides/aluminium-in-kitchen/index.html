Aluminium in the Kitchen: Materials for Cookware, Foil, Cabinets & Appliances

Aluminium is used throughout the kitchen because it spreads heat quickly, weighs a third as much as steel, resists corrosion and is easy to form. Each use calls for a different alloy and product form: 3003 circles for pans, 8011 foil for wrapping and containers, 6063 extrusions for cabinet frames. This guide maps those choices and explains the food-contact rules, for manufacturers sourcing the aluminium stock.

Why aluminium

Why aluminium suits the kitchen

Three properties explain most kitchen uses. Aluminium conducts heat about ten times better than stainless steel, so a pan heats evenly without hot spots. It has a density of only 2.70 g/cm³, so large pots, trays and cabinet doors stay light. And it forms a thin, self-healing oxide film that protects it from moisture and steam.

MaterialThermal conductivity (W/m·K, approx.)Density (g/cm³)Kitchen implication
Aluminium 10502292.71Very even heating; soft, ideal for deep drawing
Aluminium 30031932.73Even heating with more strength and dent resistance
Copper3908.9Best conductor, but heavy and expensive
Cast ironabout 507.2Holds heat; slow and uneven to heat up
Stainless steel 304168.0Durable and inert, but poor heat spreading; often clad with aluminium

This is why many stainless steel pans have an aluminium disc bonded to the base, or an aluminium layer running through the whole body. The aluminium spreads the heat; the stainless steel gives a hard, inert cooking surface and, if it is a ferritic grade, makes the pan work on induction hobs.

Aluminium is also fully recyclable without losing its properties, and recycling uses only about 5 % of the energy needed to make primary metal. For packaging such as foil trays this is a practical advantage, provided the item is clean enough to enter the recycling stream.

Cookware and kitchenware

Aluminium for pots, pans and bakeware

Most wrought aluminium cookware starts as a circle (disc) cut from coil or sheet, which is then deep-drawn, spun or pressed into shape. Choosing the right alloy and temper is a balance between formability, strength and finish.

AlloyTypical temperPropertiesTypical products
1050 / 1060 / 1070O, H12, H14Purest and softest; best deep-drawing and spinning; brightest anodised finishPressure cookers, saucepans, kettles, lids
1100O, H14Slightly stronger than 1050 with similar formabilityPots, bakeware, utensils
3003O, H12, H14About 20 % stronger than 1100; good drawing; resists dentingFrying pans, woks, non-stick pans, baking trays
3004O, H32Higher strength from added magnesiumHeavy-duty pans, large stock pots
5052H32Higher strength and corrosion resistance; less formableCommercial trays, sheet pans, lids

Deep drawing

Circles must have a fine, even grain and low earing so the drawn wall height is uniform. Order the O or H12 temper and specify earing limits for deep shapes.

Non-stick coating

PTFE and ceramic coatings need a clean, degreased surface, often roughened by grit blasting or etching. 3003 is the usual base.

Hard anodising

Hard-anodised cookware has a thick, very hard oxide layer that seals the metal from food and resists scratching.

Induction bases

Aluminium is not magnetic, so induction pans have a stainless steel plate impact-bonded or brazed to the aluminium base.

Cast cookware

Heavy cast pans use aluminium casting alloys rather than circles; wrought circles give thinner, lighter walls.

Bakeware

Anodised or plain 3003 and 5052 sheet bakes evenly and does not rust in a dishwasher.

Our aluminium circles and deep-drawing sheet pages give sizes and tempers. Metline supplies circles, sheet and coil to cookware makers; we do not make or sell finished cookware.

8011 is the workhorse: its iron and silicon content gives it more strength than pure aluminium at the same gauge, so foil and trays resist tearing. 8079 is softer and very ductile, good for thin household foil. 1235 is a high-purity alloy used for thin foil and for laminating. Lacquered or colour-coated container stock is used when the tray is visible or when the lacquer is required as a barrier against acidic food.

Cabinets

Aluminium for kitchen cabinets and fittings

Aluminium cabinets and frames are popular in humid kitchens because they do not swell, rot or harbour termites the way particle board can. Cabinet makers use extrusions for frames, door profiles, handles and slots, and sheet or honeycomb panels for doors, back panels and shelves.

Extruded profiles are made to the cabinet maker's die drawing and can be cut, drilled and CNC-machined before finishing. Appliances

Aluminium in kitchen appliances

  • Refrigerators and freezers: stucco-embossed 1100 or 3003 sheet for inner liners and evaporator plates; aluminium fin stock and tube in the cooling circuit
  • Range hoods: 3003 coil for ducting and flexible foil duct; aluminium mesh grease filters
  • Ovens and microwaves: aluminium sheet for heat shields, reflectors and trim; extruded handles
  • Small appliances: deep-drawn 1xxx and 3xxx bowls for rice cookers and air fryers, often with non-stick coatings
  • Coffee machines and kettles: drawn or extruded housings and heating blocks
  • Dishwashers and sinks: trims and decorative panels in anodised or coated sheet

Appliance sheet is usually supplied as coil or cut sheet in 0.2 – 3 mm, mill finish, embossed, pre-painted or anodised, to ASTM B209 or EN 485. See 3003 sheet, 1050 sheet and anodised sheet.

Food contact

Is aluminium safe for food contact?

Aluminium cookware and packaging are permitted for food contact throughout the world, subject to the general rules for food-contact materials. A small amount of aluminium can migrate into food, and the amount depends strongly on the food and on whether the surface is protected.

Rule or referenceWhat it says
EN 602Lists the chemical composition limits for wrought aluminium semi-products used to make articles for food contact
Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004EU framework: materials must not transfer constituents to food in amounts that endanger health or change the food unacceptably
Council of Europe CM/Res(2013)9Technical guide for metals and alloys; sets a specific release limit for aluminium of 5 mg/kg food
EFSA (2008)Tolerable weekly intake of 1 mg aluminium per kg body weight from all sources
US FDAAluminium is widely used in food-contact articles under US food-contact rules

Practical guidance for users and manufacturers

  • Acidic or salty foods (tomato sauce, citrus, pickles, marinated fish) dissolve more aluminium from bare surfaces; store them in coated, lacquered or anodised containers
  • Anodised, hard-anodised and non-stick coated cookware greatly reduces contact between food and bare metal
  • Uncoated foil is fine for wrapping and short cooking; avoid long storage of acidic food in it
  • For food-contact articles, specify an EN 602-compliant alloy and ask for the mill certificate with the actual chemistry
Selection

Choosing aluminium stock for kitchen products

  1. Start from the forming routeDeep drawing and spinning need O or H12 temper with fine grain; pressing trays needs H22/H24 foil; profiles need an extrudable 6xxx alloy.
  2. Match strength to use1xxx for maximum formability, 3003 for everyday cookware, 3004 or 5052 for heavier or commercial items.
  3. Plan the finishNon-stick, anodising, enamel and powder coating each need a suitable alloy and surface condition; tell the supplier which finish follows.
  4. Confirm food-contact complianceSpecify EN 602 or your market's rule and the coating system for the food-contact surface.
  5. Define size and packingCircle diameter and thickness, coil width and ID, foil gauge and roll size, or profile die drawing and lengths.

Common mistake: ordering a hard temper for deep-drawn pans. H14 and harder tempers crack or earing becomes excessive in deep draws; use O or H12 unless the drawing ratio is shallow.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) in the kitchen: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium alloy is best for cookware?

3003 is the most common choice for pans because it combines good deep-drawing behaviour with more strength than pure aluminium. 1050 and 1060 are used for deep shapes such as pressure cookers, and 3004 or 5052 for heavier commercial items.

What alloy is household aluminium foil made from?

Mostly 8011, 8079 or 1235 in the soft O temper, about 9 – 25 µm thick. Food containers use thicker 8011, 3003 or 3004 foil in H22 or H24.

Is cooking with aluminium safe?

Yes, within normal use. Regulators set release limits and intake guidance, and migration is low for most foods. Acidic and salty foods pick up more aluminium from bare metal, so anodised, coated or lacquered surfaces are preferred for them.

Why do stainless steel pans have an aluminium base?

Stainless steel conducts heat poorly. An aluminium disc or core layer spreads heat across the base so the pan heats evenly, while the stainless surface stays hard and inert.

Are aluminium kitchen cabinets durable?

Yes. 6063 profile frames with anodised or powder-coated finishes do not swell, rot or rust in humid kitchens, and they can be recycled at end of life.

Does Metline make finished cookware or cabinets?

No. Metline supplies the aluminium materials: circles, sheet, coil, foil and extruded profiles, cut or processed to drawing on request, to manufacturers worldwide.
