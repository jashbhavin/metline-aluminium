Rivet wire selection guide

3003, 3004 and 3A21 Aluminium Rivet Wire

3003, 3004 and 3A21 are aluminium-manganese alloys drawn into wire for cold-headed rivets that need good corrosion resistance and easy upsetting rather than high shear strength. 3003 and 3A21 are near-identical, softer grades; 3004 adds about 1 % magnesium for roughly 25 % more shear strength. This guide compares the three and explains how to specify the wire.

Overview

Where 3xxx alloys fit among rivet materials

Aluminium rivets are made from wire that is cut to length and cold headed. The alloy decides two things that pull in opposite directions: how much shear load the finished rivet carries, and how easily the head forms without cracking.

At one end sit 1xxx grades such as 1100 and 1050A, soft and very ductile but low in strength. At the other end are heat-treatable 2xxx and 7xxx grades (2017, 2117, 2024, 7050) used for aircraft structure, which need solution treatment and often have to be driven within a time limit or stored refrigerated. The 3xxx manganese alloys sit between: stronger than pure aluminium, still easy to head cold, and they need no heat treatment at any stage.

That makes 3003, 3004 and 3A21 a practical choice for brake-lining rivets, blind and tubular rivets, hardware, appliance and furniture assembly, signage, HVAC ductwork and light transport bodies: joints that are lightly or moderately loaded, often outdoors or in damp conditions, and made in high volumes on automatic heading machines.

Quick selection rule

  • Need the easiest heading and lowest cost: 3003-H14 (or its Chinese counterpart 3A21-H14)
  • Need more shear strength but still no heat treatment: 3004-H14 or H34
  • Joint is structural or aircraft primary structure: look at 2117-T4, 2017-T4 or 7050 instead
  • Joining magnesium or needing seawater service at higher strength: 5056 or 5052 rivet wire

Manganese forms fine Al-Mn-Fe particles that strengthen the matrix and keep grain size small after annealing, which helps rivet heads form with a smooth surface. Magnesium in 3004 adds solid-solution strength; it also raises the work-hardening rate, so 3004 needs slightly more heading force than 3003.

Tempers

Choosing the temper: O, H12 or H14

H14 (half hard)

The standard rivet-wire temper. Enough cold work to give useful shear strength and a clean cut-off, while still leaving the ductility to form a full round, countersunk or flat head in one or two blows.

H12 (quarter hard)

A softer option for large heads, deep tubular rivets or heading machines with limited force. Slightly lower shear strength.

O (annealed)

Maximum ductility for semi-tubular and blind rivet bodies, or for very large upset ratios. The heading operation itself work-hardens the head.

H34 for 3004

Strain hardened and stabilised. Used where the extra strength of 3004 is the reason for choosing it, but harder to head than 3003-H14.

Production

Getting good heads from 3xxx wire

  1. Match temper to upset ratioHead volumes above roughly 2 × diameter of wire length favour H12 or O; normal round heads run well in H14.
  2. Specify a heading testAsk for an upset (compression) test on samples from each coil: a short length is compressed to a set fraction of its height and must show no cracks.
  3. Control surface defectsLongitudinal scratches and die lines open up as cracks on the head periphery. Specify a maximum defect depth, usually no more than half the diameter tolerance.
  4. Keep wire dryStore coils indoors and use them in the order received. Water staining between turns marks the head surface.
  5. Mind galvanic contactAluminium rivets in steel or copper assemblies need insulation or a coating in wet service; 3xxx alloys sit close to other aluminium alloys and pose little risk with aluminium sheet.
Applications

Typical uses of 3003, 3004 and 3A21 rivets

  • Brake and clutch linings: tubular and semi-tubular rivets that hold friction material to steel shoes
  • Blind rivets: rivet bodies for general fastening of sheet in buildings, signage and enclosures
  • HVAC and ductwork: joining 3003 duct and housing sheet with a matching-alloy fastener
  • Vehicle bodies and trailers: trim, panels and non-structural attachments
  • Appliances and electronics: housings, brackets and earthing points where some conductivity helps
  • Furniture, ladders and luggage: visible rivets that must resist corrosion and look clean

For aircraft, 3xxx rivets are limited to non-structural interiors and fairings; primary structure uses 2117-T4 (AD), 2017-T4 (D), 2024-T4 (DD) or 7050-T73 (E) rivets to NAS and MS standards.

FAQ

3xxx aluminium rivet wire: common questions

Is 3A21 the same as 3003?

For rivet purposes they are interchangeable. 3A21 is the Chinese designation with a manganese range of 1.0 – 1.6 % and a titanium limit; 3003 has 1.0 – 1.5 % manganese and a deliberate 0.05 – 0.20 % copper addition. Properties in H14 are practically the same.

Why choose 3004 over 3003 for rivets?

The magnesium in 3004 raises shear strength by roughly 20 – 30 % without heat treatment. The trade-off is slightly harder heading and a little lower ductility.

What temper should aluminium rivet wire be?

H14 is the usual temper for solid rivets. Choose H12 or O for large upsets, tubular and blind rivet bodies, or low-force heading machines.

Can 3003 rivets be used outdoors?

Yes. 3xxx alloys have excellent atmospheric corrosion resistance and are compatible with most aluminium sheet. Insulate them from steel or copper in wet service to avoid galvanic attack.

Are 3003 rivets strong enough for aircraft?

Only for non-structural parts such as interior fittings. Primary aircraft structure uses heat-treatable rivet alloys such as 2117-T4 or 7050-T73.

What documents come with the wire?

An EN 10204 3.1 mill test certificate showing chemistry, tensile strength and, where ordered, shear and upset test results for each lot.
