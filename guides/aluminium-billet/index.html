Aluminium Billet: Types, Alloys, Sizes and Quality

An aluminium billet is a long, round, cast log of alloy that is the starting stock for extrusion, forging and, after working, machined parts. Most billet is direct-chill (DC) cast, homogenised and cut or scalped to length, in diameters from about 6 to 20 inches. This guide explains how billet is made, which alloys suit which process, standard sizes and weights, and the quality checks that separate good billet from scrap-prone stock.

Overview

What is an aluminium billet?

In the aluminium industry, billet means a cast cylinder intended for further hot working. It sits between primary metal (ingot, sow or molten metal) and finished wrought products. Rolling mills use rectangular slabs or rolling ingots; extruders and forgers use round billet.

The word is used in two other ways that cause confusion. Machinists talk about "billet aluminium" parts, meaning parts CNC-machined from solid wrought bar, plate or block rather than cast to shape. And forgers sometimes call a sawn length of extruded or rolled bar a "forging billet" or "forging stock". This guide covers all three, but focuses on cast billet for extrusion and forging.

Billet quality matters more than its humble appearance suggests. Inclusions, porosity, coarse grains or poor homogenisation in the billet show up downstream as tearing and pick-up on extrusions, surface streaks after anodising, cracks during forging or ultrasonic rejects in finished parts. Buyers of aerospace forgings and high-finish architectural extrusions therefore specify billet as carefully as the final product.

Three kinds of billet buyers ask for

  • Extrusion billet: DC-cast, homogenised logs or cut lengths for extrusion presses, mostly 6xxx alloys
  • Forging billet / stock: cast-and-homogenised or pre-extruded bar in 2xxx, 6xxx and 7xxx alloys for closed-die and open-die forging
  • Machining billet: large wrought bar or block in 6061, 7075, 2024 and similar, sold for CNC machining of "billet" parts
Manufacturing

How aluminium billet is cast and processed

  1. Melting and alloyingPrimary aluminium and controlled scrap are melted, and alloying elements (Mg, Si, Cu, Zn, Mn) are added to hit the target chemistry, checked by spectrometer.
  2. Melt treatmentThe melt is degassed (usually with argon in an in-line unit) to remove hydrogen that would cause porosity, and filtered through ceramic foam filters to remove oxide inclusions. A grain refiner such as Al-Ti-B rod is added for a fine, even cast structure.
  3. Direct-chill (DC) castingMetal is poured into short water-cooled moulds on a casting table. As the shell freezes, the starting block is lowered and water sprays directly on the surface, solidifying the logs vertically. Hot-top and air-assisted moulds give a smooth surface with a thin segregated shell.
  4. HomogenisationLogs are soaked at high temperature (for 6063 roughly 560 – 580 °C for several hours; lower for 2xxx and 7xxx alloys), then cooled at a controlled rate. This dissolves coarse phases, evens out segregation and gives the right precipitate size for extrusion speed and surface finish.
  5. Sawing, scalping and inspectionLogs are sawn to length or to press-ready billets. Billet for forging and aerospace use is often scalped (peeled) to remove the cast surface. Inspection covers chemistry, dimensions, surface, macro-etch and ultrasonic tests as specified.

Older and smaller plants may use horizontal continuous casting for small diameters, and some forging stock is produced by extruding a cast billet into bar first, which refines the structure further. For critical aerospace forgings, the specification often defines which route is allowed.

The large majority of extrusion billet is 6xxx, because 6060 and 6063 extrude fast with an excellent surface and 6061, 6082 and 6005A give structural strength. 2xxx and 7xxx billets extrude several times more slowly and need tighter casting control, which is why they cost more and are often bought from specialist casthouses. For a single alloy in depth, see our 7075 billet guide.

Chemistry within the registered limits also matters. Extruders often specify a narrower window, for example controlled Mg and Si in 6063 for consistent anodising, or a defined Mg2Si balance for strength. Aerospace billet is typically cast to AMS or customer specifications with tighter impurity limits (especially Fe and Si) for better toughness.

Standard billet diameters, lengths and weights

Billet diameter is chosen to fit the extrusion press container or the forging operation. Common sizes are quoted in inches. Logs are typically cast around 4.8 – 5.8 m (about 190 – 230 in) long and sawn to press length, often 0.5 – 1.5 m. Weights below use 2.70 g/cm³; 7xxx and 2xxx alloys are 2 – 4 % heavier.

A good billet extrudes at high speed without tearing, gives a uniform anodised finish, and leaves little back-end scrap. A poor billet shows its problems as die lines, blisters, streaks, low press speeds or cracked forgings. If you see these, ask for the cast numbers and homogenisation records: most root causes trace to melt cleanliness, hydrogen or homogenising practice.

Applications

Where aluminium billet ends up

Extrusion

Window and door profiles, curtain wall, solar mounting rails, heat sinks, tubes, bars and automotive crash structures, mostly from 6xxx billet.

Closed-die forging

Aircraft fittings, landing-gear parts, automotive control arms and knuckles, wheels and pistons in 2xxx, 4032, 6061/6082 and 7xxx.

Open-die and ring forging

Large discs, rings and blocks for aerospace, defence and industrial equipment.

Machining from solid

Racing parts, enclosures, moulds and prototypes machined from bar or block that started as billet.

Impact extrusion

Small billet slices (slugs) for cans, tubes and housings, usually 1xxx, 3xxx or 6xxx.

Remelting and alloying

Some billet is traded as feedstock for other casthouses and foundries.

Buying advice

How to specify aluminium billet, and common mistakes

  • State alloy and any narrowed chemistry (for example 6063 with Mg 0.45 – 0.55 %), not only the registered grade.
  • Specify diameter, length (log or cut), tolerance and whether the billet is as-cast or scalped.
  • Require homogenisation and say whether a controlled cooling rate is needed for your press.
  • Define inspection: chemistry per cast, macro-etch, ultrasonic class for forging billet, and the certificate type (EN 10204 3.1).
  • Agree packing: banded bundles or lengths on dunnage, with cast numbers marked on each billet for traceability.

Common mistakes include buying unhomogenised billet to save cost (and then losing far more in press speed and scrap), choosing a diameter that does not match the container, and accepting billet without cast-number traceability, which makes it impossible to investigate downstream defects. FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) billet: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between aluminium billet and ingot?

Ingot is usually a small block or sow of primary or alloyed metal made for remelting. Billet is a round DC-cast log of a specific wrought alloy, homogenised and sized for direct use in extrusion presses or forging.

What does "billet aluminum" mean for machined parts?

It means the part was CNC-machined from a solid piece of wrought aluminium (bar, plate or block) rather than cast to shape. These parts have the higher strength and integrity of wrought metal, typically 6061-T6 or 7075-T6.

Why is homogenisation important?

As-cast billet has segregation and coarse intermetallic phases. Homogenising dissolves and redistributes them, which allows faster extrusion, a better surface and anodising response, and better forgeability. Unhomogenised billet usually costs more downstream than it saves.

What billet diameters are standard?

Common sizes are 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12 inches for extrusion, with 14 – 20 inches and larger for big presses and forgings. The right diameter is set by your press container or forging plan.

Which alloy should I choose for forging billet?

6061 or 6082 for general and automotive forgings, 2014 or 2618 for heat-resistant parts, 4032 for pistons, and 7075 or 7050 for high-strength aerospace parts. The forging specification usually names the alloy and inspection class.

Can billet be supplied cut to length and scalped?

Yes. Billet can be supplied as full logs or sawn to press length, as-cast or scalped, with chemistry, macro-etch and ultrasonic reports as required, and mill test certificates to EN 10204 3.1.
