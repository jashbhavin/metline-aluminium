Honeycomb overview

Honeycomb Core Materials & Sandwich Panels

A honeycomb sandwich panel bonds two thin facings to a lightweight cellular core, giving very high stiffness for its weight. The core material, cell geometry, facing and joining method together decide strength, temperature range, fire behaviour and cost. This overview compares the main core materials and panel types, explains how to choose between them, and shows where Metline's aluminium honeycomb core and panels fit.

Principles

How a honeycomb sandwich panel works

A sandwich panel behaves like an I-beam spread out into a sheet. The two facings act as the flanges, carrying bending as tension on one side and compression on the other. The core acts as the web: it keeps the facings apart, carries transverse shear and supports the facings against wrinkling and buckling. Because bending stiffness rises with the square of the distance between the facings, a thicker core adds a great deal of stiffness for very little weight.

ConstructionRelative bending stiffnessRelative bending strengthRelative weight

Classic illustration published by honeycomb producers for a lightweight core; actual gains depend on core shear stiffness and facing material.

Honeycomb is the most efficient core shape for flat panels because the hexagonal cell walls run straight through the thickness and give high compressive and shear strength at very low density. Foams, balsa and corrugated cores are alternatives, but at equal weight a well-chosen honeycomb is usually the stiffest and strongest option.

Key honeycomb terms

Cell size
Distance across the flats of a hexagonal cell
Foil or wall thickness
Thickness of the sheet material forming the walls
Density
Core mass per unit volume, kg/m³ or lb/ft³
L direction
Along the ribbon, parallel to the double-thickness node walls; the stronger shear direction
W direction
Across the ribbon, the direction of expansion; weaker in shear
T direction
Through the thickness, along the cell axis; carries compression

In weight-critical structures the comparison usually narrows to aluminium versus aramid. Aluminium gives higher stiffness and strength per kilogram, conducts heat and electricity and resists moisture once coated; aramid insulates, is transparent to radar and recovers better from small impacts. Our insight on aluminium vs aramid honeycomb core covers that choice in depth, and 3003 vs 5052 core covers the choice between aluminium grades.

Aluminium honeycomb

Aluminium honeycomb core: grades, geometry and supply range

3003 honeycomb core

Cost-effective, easy to bend and shape. Used for building panels, doors, partitions, signage, cleanroom walls and general industrial panels.

5052 honeycomb core

The standard structural core for aerospace, rail, marine and industrial panels: stronger and more corrosion-resistant than 3003.

5056 honeycomb core

Highest strength of the standard alloys and best salt-spray resistance; chosen for aerospace and naval structures and energy absorbers.

5A12 aerospace core

High-magnesium foil core supplied against drawings and specifications that call for it.

Density is the single best predictor of core strength. For the same alloy, compressive and shear strength rise faster than density, so choosing the right cell and foil combination is the main lever for optimising panel weight. Joining

How cores and facings are joined

Film adhesives

Epoxy film cured under heat and pressure, forming a fillet at every cell wall. Standard in aerospace for the highest peel and shear strength.

Liquid and paste adhesives

Two-part epoxies and polyurethanes, roller-coated or sprayed. Used for building, transport and industrial panels on press lines.

Brazing

Al-Si clad sheet and core joined in a furnace. No organic content, highest temperature tolerance and no outgassing.

Node bonding inside the core

The adhesive lines that hold expanded core together; their rating often sets the core temperature limit.

Surface preparation

Conversion coating or phosphoric-acid anodising of aluminium skins and core gives the most durable bond, especially in humid service.

Edge closure

Extruded aluminium edge profiles, folded skins or potting seal the cells against moisture and give fixing points.

Joining choice affects more than strength: it decides temperature range, fire classification and whether the panel can be recycled easily. Our insight on honeycomb core joining methods compares the options.

Durability in service depends mostly on the bond line. Panels that fail after years outdoors rarely fail because the aluminium core or skins have corroded through; they fail because moisture has reached an untreated bond surface and the adhesive has lost adhesion. Specify the surface treatment for both skin and core, require sealed edges and drainage paths where panels can collect water, and ask for peel or flatwise tension tests after humidity ageing if the panel will live outdoors or at sea.

Design checks

Sandwich panel failure modes to check

A sandwich panel can fail in several ways that a solid plate cannot, and each one points to a different design change. Run through all of them during design, not only the bending stress in the facings.

Failure modeWhat happensHow to prevent it
Facing yield or fractureSkin on the tension or compression side reaches its strengthThicker or stronger facing alloy, or deeper core
Core shear failureCore shears between the facings near supportsHigher-density core, L direction along the span, deeper core
Face wrinklingCompression skin buckles into the core over short wavelengthsStiffer, denser core; better bond
Intra-cell dimplingThin skin buckles into individual cellsSmaller cell size or thicker skin
Local indentationCore crushes under point loads, feet or wheelsHigher-density core locally, load-spreading plates
Insert pull-outFastener insert tears out of the corePotted inserts, reinforced core zones, through-bolts with spacers
Bond failure (delamination)Skin separates from coreCorrect surface treatment, adhesive and cure; sealed edges

Aluminium honeycomb's directional shear strength is the detail designers most often miss. Shear strength in the W direction is typically about 60 % of the L-direction value, so a panel that spans in two directions should be checked with the W value on the weaker axis. Deflection checks must include core shear deflection as well as bending deflection; for thick, soft cores on short spans, shear deflection can be the larger part.

Processing

Cutting, machining and finishing honeycomb panels

Cutting core

Band saws and reciprocating knives slice core cleanly; CNC ultrasonic knives profile complex shapes without crushing cell walls.

Cutting panels

Circular saws and CNC routers with carbide tools cut finished panels; support the panel close to the cut to avoid skin lifting.

Edge closing

Route a groove, fold the skin, fit an extruded aluminium edge profile or fill with potting compound to seal cells and give clean edges.

Inserts and fixings

Drill through one skin, remove a small volume of core and pot a threaded insert with epoxy filler; for heavy loads use through-panel inserts.

Bending

Route a V-groove in the back skin and core, then fold to create cassette panels for facades and ceilings.

Finishing

Pre-coated PVDF or polyester skins give the most durable architectural finish; anodised skins suit interiors and equipment.

Metline can supply core and panels cut to size or machined to drawing, with edge profiles and inserts specified on the order. For high-volume projects, supplying core as unexpanded slices plus coated aluminium coil for skins can be the most economical route for a fabricator with its own press line.

Selection

How to choose a core and panel: step by step

  1. Loads and stiffnessSpan, supports, design loads and allowable deflection give the required bending stiffness and core shear stress.
  2. EnvironmentTemperature range, moisture, salt, chemicals, UV and fire rules for the sector.
  3. Core material and densityPick aluminium grade (or another core where its property is needed), then the lowest density that passes shear, compression and local checks.
  4. Facings and finishAlloy, thickness and coating for the skins; appearance and corrosion protection.
  5. Joining and edgesAdhesive or brazing, edge closures, inserts and fixings.
  6. Testing and documentsAgree tests, certificates and inspection before production.
  • Common mistake: specifying core by cell size only. Always give density or foil thickness as well.
  • Common mistake: ignoring ribbon direction. Run the L direction along the main span for the highest shear capacity.
  • Common mistake: leaving edges open. Unsealed cells take in water, which corrodes core and lowers bond strength.
  • Common mistake: pairing bare aluminium core directly with carbon-fibre skins. Add an insulating glass ply or scrim to prevent galvanic corrosion.

FAQ

Honeycomb core and sandwich panels: frequently asked questions

What is the most common honeycomb core material?

Aluminium, mainly 3003 for commercial and building panels and 5052 for structural and aerospace panels. Aramid paper core is the main non-metallic alternative.

Which honeycomb core materials does Metline supply?

Aluminium honeycomb core in 3003, 5052, 5056 and 5A12, expanded or unexpanded, and aluminium-faced honeycomb panels including brazed panels. We also supply aluminium core and skins to fabricators building composite-faced panels.

What size do aluminium (aluminum) honeycomb panels come in?

The common format is 1220 × 2440 mm (4 × 8 ft), with longer 3050 and 3660 mm lengths and custom cut sizes. Total thickness typically runs from about 3 mm to 75 mm.

How do I choose cell size?

Smaller cells support thin facings better and give smoother surfaces and more bond area; larger cells save weight in lightly loaded panels. Choose density first for strength, then the cell size that suits facing thickness.

Is an aluminium honeycomb panel fire-safe?

Aluminium core and skins are non-combustible; the adhesive and coating decide the fire class. Panels for buildings, rail and ships are tested as complete products to EN 13501-1, EN 45545-2 or IMO rules.

Can honeycomb panels be curved?

Yes. Single curvature is achieved with over-expanded core or by roll-bending finished panels; compound curves use flexible-geometry core or pre-formed parts.

What is the difference between expanded and unexpanded core?

Unexpanded core is the bonded block or slice before the cells are opened; it ships compactly and is expanded by the user. Expanded core is supplied open and ready to bond.
