Aluminium Clad Stainless Steel

Aluminium clad stainless steel is a bimetal in which a layer of aluminium is metallurgically bonded to stainless steel, by roll bonding for thin sheet, strip and cookware discs, or by explosion bonding for thick plate and structural transition joints. It combines the heat conduction and light weight of aluminium with the hygiene, strength and corrosion resistance of stainless steel. This guide covers the bonding methods, material combinations, properties, standards, applications and how to specify it.

Overview

What is aluminium clad stainless steel?

Cladding joins two metals over their whole contact area so that they behave as one sheet or plate. Unlike a painted or plated coating, the bond is metallurgical: atoms of aluminium and steel are brought into direct contact under pressure, with no adhesive between them. The result can be bent, deep drawn, machined and welded on each face with the process that suits that metal.

Aluminium and stainless steel complement each other well. Stainless steel is strong, hard-wearing, hygienic and resistant to many chemicals, but conducts heat poorly: grade 304 has a thermal conductivity of only about 16 W/m·K. Commercially pure aluminium conducts about 220 – 235 W/m·K and weighs roughly one third as much, but is soft and attacked by strong acids and alkalis. A bonded laminate puts each metal where it does most good.

The two metals cannot be joined reliably by ordinary fusion welding: molten aluminium and iron form brittle iron-aluminide compounds. Cladding avoids this by bonding in the solid state. That same property makes clad plate valuable as a transition joint, a strip that is welded to steel on one side and to aluminium on the other.

Why engineers specify it

  • Even, fast heat spreading through a stainless surface (cookware, griddles, heat exchangers)
  • Joining aluminium superstructures to steel hulls and decks without bolts or galvanic problems
  • Lighter parts than solid stainless steel with a stainless working face
  • Magnetic 430 stainless outer layer makes aluminium cookware work on induction hobs
  • Thermal and electrical paths that solid stainless steel cannot provide

In explosion-bonded aluminium-steel transition material, a thin interlayer of commercially pure aluminium (for example 1050) is usually placed between the steel and a structural aluminium alloy such as 5083. Pure aluminium bonds more reliably than magnesium-bearing alloys and reduces the formation of brittle compounds at the interface. Some structural joints also use a titanium or other interlayer for higher bond strength.

Products

Forms of aluminium clad stainless steel

Explosion-bonded clad plate

Thick aluminium (1050A, 1060, 5083) bonded to stainless or carbon steel plate for pressure equipment, cryogenic transitions, electrolytic cells and structural joints.

Roll-bonded clad plate and sheet

Two- or three-layer laminates from a few millimetres to around 25 mm, with the cladding typically a defined percentage of total thickness. Used for griddles, steam kettles and heat-transfer surfaces.

Tri-ply cookware discs and sheet

Stainless 304 inside, aluminium core, magnetic 430 outside, supplied as circles for deep drawing into pans, pots and kadhais.

Clad strip and coil

Thin aluminium-stainless laminates, single- or double-sided, for stamping, bending and roll forming in appliances, heat exchangers and decorative trims.

Clad foil

Very thin laminates for shielding, heat spreading and specialist packaging.

Marine and pipe transition joints

Strips or rings cut from explosion-bonded plate, welded to the steel hull on one side and the aluminium superstructure or pipe on the other.

Ferritic grades such as 405, 410S, 429 and 430, and austenitic grades such as 304, 309, 316 and 321, can all be clad with aluminium. The choice depends on whether the face must be magnetic, how corrosive the medium is and whether the part will be deep drawn: 304 draws better than 430, while 430 is cheaper and magnetic.

A clad laminate does not have a single "alloy" property; its behaviour is set by the properties and thickness ratio of each layer. The table shows typical values for the individual metals.

The large difference in thermal expansion between aluminium and stainless steel means a two-layer laminate bows when heated. Tri-ply construction, with stainless on both faces, balances these stresses, which is one reason cookware is usually three-layer. For transition joints, the bond is commonly verified by through-thickness tensile and shear tests; supplier data for MIL-J-24445A-type joints often quotes minimums in the region of 75 MPa tensile and 55 MPa shear at the weakest interface. Long exposure above about 300 °C should be avoided, because brittle intermetallic compounds can grow at the interface.

Stainless values from ASTM A240 minimums and typical handbook data; aluminium values from Aluminum Association / ASM typical data. Bond strength must be confirmed by the test certificate of the actual lot.

ASTM A578Ultrasonic examination of plain and clad steel plates; often applied to check bond continuity

ASTM A263, A264 and A265 cover stainless and nickel cladding on carbon steel rather than aluminium cladding, so they are sometimes used only as a reference for test methods such as bond shear. For aluminium-stainless products the order should state the test plan directly: ultrasonic scanning of the bond area, shear and tensile tests of the interface, bend or peel tests, and thickness checks of each layer.

  • Ultrasonic testing: maps unbonded areas; transition joints are commonly specified with 100 % scanning
  • Shear and tensile tests: measure bond strength through and along the interface
  • Bend and peel tests: show whether the bond survives forming
  • Thickness and ratio checks: confirm each layer by micrometer or cross-section
  • Microstructure: cross-sections show the wavy explosive bond or the diffusion zone in roll-bonded material
Applications

Where aluminium clad stainless steel is used

Cookware

Tri-ply pans, pots, pressure cookers and kadhais: a stainless cooking face, an aluminium core that spreads heat and a 430 base for induction.

Shipbuilding

Transition joints let shipyards weld aluminium superstructures to steel hulls, cutting top weight while avoiding bolted joints that corrode.

Process equipment

Steam kettles, griddles, evaporator pans and heat-transfer plates in food and chemical plants.

Cryogenic plant

Aluminium-stainless pipe transitions connect aluminium cold boxes and heat exchangers to stainless piping in air separation and LNG equipment.

Electrochemistry

Current-carrying connections and anode assemblies where aluminium meets steel.

Appliances and trim

Clad strip for heat spreaders, stamped housings and decorative panels.

A typical example: a fast ferry uses a steel hull with an aluminium deckhouse. Strips of explosion-bonded steel/1050/5083 transition material are welded to the steel deck by normal steel welding, and the aluminium deckhouse is welded to the 5083 face with aluminium MIG. The bimetal interface stays below its temperature limit because the welds are made well away from it.

Working with clad material

Fabrication guidance

Cutting

Saw, waterjet or shear. Avoid thermal cutting close to the bond, which can overheat the interface.

Deep drawing

Roll-bonded tri-ply discs draw well when the layer ratio and annealing are correct; use lubricants suited to stainless.

Welding transition joints

Weld steel to steel and aluminium to aluminium only. Control heat input and interpass temperature so the interface stays below its temperature limit; follow the joint maker's and class society's procedure.

Machining

Machine each layer with tools and speeds suited to it; stainless work-hardens and aluminium smears.

Finishing

Stainless faces are polished or brushed; aluminium faces can be left plain, hard anodised or coated depending on the use.

Corrosion

Seal the exposed bimetal edge with paint or sealant in wet service to prevent galvanic attack at the interface.

Procurement

How to specify aluminium clad stainless steel

  1. Define the combinationStainless grade(s), aluminium alloy(s), number of layers and any interlayer.
  2. Set thicknessesEach layer thickness or the cladding ratio, plus total thickness and tolerance.
  3. Size and shapePlate size, coil width, disc diameter or strip dimensions for transition joints.
  4. Testing and approvalsUltrasonic coverage, bond shear and tensile tests, class society approval or MIL-J-24445A compliance where required.
  5. Surface and processingPolish, brushing, protective film, cutting or machining.
  6. Documents and deliveryEN 10204 3.1 or 3.2 certificates and delivery terms (EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP).

Common mistakes

  • Citing ASTM A264 as if it were an aluminium cladding standard instead of writing a direct test plan
  • Using two-layer laminates where differential expansion will cause bowing
  • Welding too close to the bond line of a transition joint
  • Leaving the cut bimetal edge unsealed in marine service
  • Not specifying the magnetic outer grade for induction cookware

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) clad stainless steel: frequently asked questions

What is tri-ply aluminium clad stainless steel?

A three-layer laminate with an aluminium core between two stainless layers, usually 304 on the cooking side and magnetic 430 outside. The aluminium spreads heat evenly while the stainless faces give a durable, hygienic surface and induction compatibility.

Can aluminium be welded directly to stainless steel?

Not reliably by fusion welding, because brittle iron-aluminium compounds form. Solid-state methods such as explosion bonding, roll bonding and friction welding, or a bonded transition joint, are used instead.

What is an aluminium-steel transition joint?

A strip cut from explosion-bonded plate, with steel on one face and aluminium (often a 1050 interlayer plus 5083) on the other. It is welded steel-to-steel and aluminium-to-aluminium, so the dissimilar joint is never fused.

What is the difference between roll-bonded and explosion-bonded clad plate?

Roll bonding produces thinner sheet, strip and discs with very consistent thickness over long lengths. Explosion bonding joins much thicker plates and suits structural transition joints and heavy process equipment.

How is the bond quality checked?

By ultrasonic scanning for unbonded areas, plus shear, tensile, bend or peel tests of samples from the same plate or coil. Results are reported on the inspection certificate.

What temperature can aluminium clad stainless steel withstand?

Normal cooking and process temperatures are fine. For structural transition joints, prolonged heating above about 300 °C should be avoided because brittle compounds can grow at the interface.
