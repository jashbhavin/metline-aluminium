Anodised Aluminium Extrusions and Profiles

Anodised aluminium extrusions are profiles, tubes, angles and channels whose surface has been converted into a hard, sealed layer of aluminium oxide by electrolytic treatment. The finish resists corrosion and wear, keeps a metallic look and cannot peel. Specify it by alloy, film class (for example AA15 or AA20 for architecture, or Type III hardcoat for wear parts), colour and sealing; Metline supplies anodised extrusions cut to length or machined to drawing for customers worldwide.

Overview

Why anodise aluminium extrusions?

Anodising makes the extrusion the anode in a sulphuric-acid bath. Current drives oxygen into the surface, growing a layer of aluminium oxide made of tiny hexagonal cells, each with a central pore. The pores can be filled with dye or metal salts for colour and are then sealed, leaving a dense, hard, transparent skin that is part of the metal.

Compared with paint or powder, anodising adds very little thickness, does not hide the metallic character of the aluminium, resists abrasion far better and does not chalk or peel. It is the traditional finish for window and curtain-wall profiles, shopfronts, handrails, machine-building frames, LED profiles and consumer products. Hard anodising (Type III) goes further, producing a thick, very hard coating for sliding and wearing parts.

The quality of the finish depends as much on the extrusion as on the anodiser. Alloy chemistry, die lines, press handling and the visible faces on the drawing all show through a transparent film. This guide covers the decisions that determine whether the finished profile looks and performs as intended.

What anodising gives an extrusion

  • Corrosion resistance matched to exposure by film class (AA10 to AA25)
  • Abrasion resistance well above organic coatings; hardcoat for heavy wear
  • Lightfast colours: clear, champagne, bronze shades and black
  • An electrically insulating, non-flaking surface that keeps tolerances tight

For decorative work, 6060 and 6063 are the safe choices: low iron and controlled magnesium and silicon give a clear film with consistent colour. Strength-driven alloys such as 6082 and 6005A anodise well for protection, but large visible faces may show streaks or grain. When a project needs both strength and appearance, discuss the visible faces with the extruder and approve samples before production.

Do not mix alloys or tempers on one visible assembly: the same anodising cycle gives different tones on different alloys.

Film growth affects dimensions. A conventional sulphuric film grows partly into the metal and partly outwards, so the part gains roughly one third of the film thickness on each surface. Hardcoat grows about half outward and half inward, so a 50 µm hardcoat adds about 25 µm per face, or 50 µm on a diameter. Machine critical fits allowing for this, or mask them.

Hardcoat is usually left natural (grey to bronze-black, depending on alloy and thickness) or dyed black. It is hard and wear-resistant but more brittle than Type II, so avoid it on parts that will be bent after anodising. For hardcoated bar stock, see our hard anodised bar guide.

Colour and sealing

Colouring and sealing anodised profiles

Clear (natural)

No colouring; the most durable and easiest to match. Satin-etched or brushed pre-treatment gives matt or directional looks.

Electrolytic (two-step) colour

Tin or other metal salts are deposited at the base of the pores to give champagne, light to dark bronze and black. Very lightfast; the standard for exterior architecture.

Organic dyes

Immersion dyes give black, gold, red, blue and many other colours. Excellent indoors; lightfastness varies outdoors, so check dye data.

Hot-water sealing

Boiling deionised water hydrates the pore walls and closes them. Robust and preferred by many specifiers for exterior and coastal work.

Mid-temperature sealing

Nickel acetate or additive-based seals at lower temperature, saving energy and reducing smut; widely used with good control.

Cold sealing

Nickel fluoride-based seals at near-room temperature followed by ageing or a warm-water rinse. Needs careful control to achieve full sealing.

Sealing is what turns a porous film into a durable one. An unsealed or poorly sealed film stains easily and loses corrosion resistance. Quality schemes verify sealing with admittance (ISO 2931), dye-spot (ISO 2143) or acid-dissolution (ISO 3210) tests. For colour, specify by named shade and approve limit samples showing the lightest and darkest acceptable tones; a ΔE tolerance measured by spectrophotometer is common on large projects.

Even a good anodised film needs simple care outdoors: periodic washing with water and a neutral detergent removes salt and pollutant deposits that would otherwise concentrate on the surface. Avoid alkaline cleaners and mortar or concrete splashes, which attack anodic films, and protect profiles on site until construction trades are finished.

Design for anodising

Designing and specifying extrusions for anodising

  • Mark visible faces on the drawing. Extruders orient die lines, handling and racking contacts away from them.
  • Allow for rack marks. Each profile needs an electrical contact point, which leaves a small unanodised mark; agree where it may be (usually an end that will be cut off or hidden).
  • Avoid deep blind slots and enclosed cavities where solutions are trapped and film cannot grow evenly.
  • Keep wall thicknesses as uniform as practical: thick-to-thin transitions can show as tone differences after etching.
  • Specify tolerances to EN 755-9 or EN 12020-2 (precision 6060/6063 profiles) or ANSI H35.2 for ASTM orders, and allow for film growth on critical fits.
  • Specify film thickness measurement: eddy-current per ISO 2360 or ASTM B244, measured on significant surfaces.
  • Plan fabrication order. Cut, drill and machine before anodising where the finish must cover everything; otherwise seal or accept bare cut edges.

Lengths are typically around 6 m, the common length for anodising racks and transport; other lengths are possible depending on the line. Profiles can be supplied cut to precise lengths and CNC-machined (drilled, tapped, milled) before or after anodising to suit the part.

FAQ

Anodised (anodized) aluminium extrusions: frequently asked questions

Which alloy is best for anodised extrusions?

6063 and 6060 give the most uniform, clear anodised finish and are the standard for architectural and decorative profiles. 6463 is used for bright, specular finishes; 6061 is a good choice for strength and hardcoat.

What anodising thickness do I need for outdoor profiles?

AA15 for normal exterior use, AA20 for polluted or moderate coastal sites and AA25 for severe marine exposure. In the US, AAMA 611 Class I (18 µm and above) is specified for commercial exteriors.

What is the difference between Type II and Type III anodising?

Type II is conventional sulphuric anodising, usually up to about 25 µm, used for decorative and protective finishes in many colours. Type III hardcoat is thicker (often around 50 µm) and much harder, used for wear resistance, with a limited colour range.

Does anodising change the dimensions of an extrusion?

Slightly. Conventional films add roughly one third of their thickness to each surface; hardcoat adds about half. A 50 µm hardcoat increases a diameter by about 50 µm, so critical fits should be machined to allow for it or masked.

Can anodised profiles be cut and drilled after finishing?

Yes, but cut ends and holes will be bare aluminium. Indoors this is usually acceptable; outdoors, hide or seal them, or machine before anodising.

Does Metline supply anodised extrusions internationally?

Yes. Metline supplies standard and custom anodised aluminium extrusions, cut to length or machined to drawing, with mill certificates, anodising test reports and export packing to customers worldwide.
