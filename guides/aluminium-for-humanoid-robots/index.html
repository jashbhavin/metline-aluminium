Aluminium for Humanoid Robots

Most humanoid robots are built around an aluminium skeleton: 6061 or 6082 for frames and long links, 7075 for compact, highly loaded joint parts, 5xxx sheet for covers and casting alloys for volume housings. This guide explains which alloy, temper and manufacturing route suits each part of the robot, what tolerances and finishes matter, and how to specify the aluminium stock for prototypes and production.

The design problem

Why humanoid robots are built mostly from aluminium

A humanoid robot is a chain of moving masses. Every gram in a forearm has to be accelerated by the elbow, shoulder, hip and ankle actuators below it, so mass far from the body centre costs motor torque, battery energy and control bandwidth. Aluminium is the default structural metal because it offers about one third the density of steel with strength that, in the 7xxx alloys, approaches that of many structural steels.

The important number is not strength alone but strength and stiffness per unit mass. Aluminium alloys have an elastic modulus of roughly 69–72 GPa, about one third of steel, but because density is also about one third, specific stiffness is almost identical. That means an aluminium link of the same mass as a steel link can be made larger in section and therefore stiffer in bending, which is exactly what a robot arm or leg needs to hold position accuracy under load.

Aluminium also machines several times faster than titanium or steel, conducts heat well enough to act as a heat spreader for motors and drives, takes hard anodic coatings for wear surfaces, and is available in every semi-finished form a robotics team needs: plate, block, bar, flat bar, tube, custom extrusions and forgings. Titanium, magnesium, carbon-fibre composites and engineering plastics all have a place in humanoid design, but they tend to be used selectively where aluminium is not enough, rather than as the main structure.

Part-by-part

Where aluminium goes in a humanoid robot

The loads, space and production volume differ from one part of the body to the next, so a single robot commonly uses four or five aluminium alloys and three or four manufacturing routes. The table below is a practical starting map; final selection always depends on your load cases and drawings.

Torso and spineMain frame, battery bay, spine plates, shoulder and hip mounts6061-T6/T651, 6082-T6, 7075-T651 at highly loaded nodesPlate or extrusion, then CNC machining
Pelvis and central nodesHip yoke, pelvis block, load-transfer brackets7075-T651 or 7050-T7451 plate, 6061-T6 forgingsMachined from plate or block; forging at volume
Thigh and shank linksLeg tubes, link beams, ankle struts6061-T6, 6082-T6, 7075-T6Custom extrusion or tube, machined ends

A useful rule is that anything long with a constant cross-section is a candidate for extrusion, anything compact and highly loaded is machined from 7xxx plate or bar (and later forged), and anything complex, hollow and produced in thousands is a candidate for die casting.

Alloy selection

Choosing the right aluminium alloy for each robot part

7075-T6 / T651

The high-strength choice: typical yield about 503 MPa, nearly twice 6061-T6, with excellent machinability and good fatigue strength. Used for compact joint parts, reducer housings, output flanges, finger links and pelvis nodes where section size is limited. Not weldable by fusion methods and less corrosion resistant, so it is normally anodised.

7050-T7451

Similar strength class to 7075 but with much better toughness and resistance to stress-corrosion cracking in thick sections. A good option for thick pelvis blocks and large load-carrying brackets machined from plate over about 50 mm.

6061-T6 / T651

The all-rounder: good strength, very good machinability, weldable, anodises well and available everywhere. Often the first choice for frames, brackets, torso plates and actuator housings that are not strength-critical.

6082-T6

The European structural equivalent of 6061 with slightly higher strength in thicker sections. Widely available as bar, tube and custom extrusion, so it suits leg and arm links.

6063-T5 / T6

Lower strength but excellent extrudability and surface finish. Used for thin-walled covers, cable trays, heat-sink profiles and lightly loaded internal frames.

5052-H32 / 5083

Non-heat-treatable sheet alloys that bend and weld well. Used for enclosures, shells, shields and battery covers where formability matters more than strength.

2024-T351

High fatigue strength and toughness. Occasionally used for highly cycled links and finger parts, but it has poor corrosion resistance and needs anodising or coating.

Casting alloys (A356, A380 and similar)

Dedicated foundry alloys for die-cast or sand-cast housings. They are not interchangeable with wrought grades: 7075 and 6061 are not die-casting alloys.

The common decision

Is 7075 always better than 6061 for robot parts?

No. 7075 is the obvious answer when a part is strength-limited in a small envelope, but in many robot parts the governing criterion is stiffness or deflection, and the modulus of 7075 is only about 4 % higher than 6061. If a link is deflecting too much, switching from 6061 to 7075 barely helps; increasing the section depth does.

7075 makes most sense where stress is high: thin-walled bearing seats that must not creep under preload, threads in small bosses, clevis lugs, finger links with small cross-sections and output flanges carrying impact loads from falls. It costs more per kilogram, cannot be fusion welded and is more sensitive to corrosion and stress-corrosion cracking, so it is used where it earns its place.

Choose 7075 or 7050 when

  • Stress, not stiffness, limits the design
  • The envelope is fixed and the section cannot grow
  • Threads or bearing seats are in thin walls
  • Impact loads from falls or contact must be absorbed without yielding

Choose 6061 or 6082 when

  • Deflection or vibration governs the design
  • Parts are long, welded or extruded
  • Cost and availability across suppliers matter
  • Decorative or coloured anodising is required
Manufacturing route

CNC, extrusion, forging or die casting: which route fits the part?

The right route depends on design maturity and volume as much as on the part itself. Humanoid programmes usually move through all four as the design stabilises, and choosing the stock form early can save a redesign later.

RouteStarting stockBest forStrengthsWatch-outs
CNC from solidPlate, block, round bar, flat barPrototypes, joint parts, brackets, low volumesNo tooling; any alloy; fast design changesHigh material removal; cost per part stays high at volume
Extrusion + machiningCustom 6061, 6082, 6063 or 7075 profileLinks, torso rails, beams, heat-sink housingsNear-net section, low cost per metre, integral featuresDie cost; tolerances per EN 755-9 or ANSI H35.2 need machining at datums
Forging + machining6061 or 7075 forging stock, billetPelvis nodes, hip and knee yokes, output flangesGrain flow follows the part, high fatigue and impact strengthDie cost; justified only once the design is frozen
Die casting + machiningCasting alloy ingotMotor, gearbox and joint housings in volumeComplex hollow shapes, ribs and bosses in one partPorosity, lower ductility; bearing seats must be machined
  1. Concept and prototypeMachine from 6061-T651 or 7075-T651 plate and bar. Keep stock sizes standard so material is available quickly.
  2. Design iterationIdentify parts with constant sections and redraw them as extrusions; identify compact highly loaded nodes as forging candidates.
  3. Pilot buildOrder the extrusion dies and forging tools; machine critical faces and bearing seats from the near-net blanks.
  4. ProductionMove complex housings to die casting where volumes justify the tooling, and keep 7xxx machined parts for the highest-stress locations.

Precision & stability

Tolerances, flatness and dimensional stability

Humanoid joints run preloaded bearings, strain-wave gears and encoders, so housing geometry directly affects backlash, noise, efficiency and position accuracy. The figures below are typical design targets engineers set for robot parts; actual requirements always come from your drawing and the bearing or reducer manufacturer's mounting specification.

Part typeTypical drawing requirements
Frame and torso platesFlatness of a few hundredths of a millimetre over mounting faces; hole position to ±0.01–0.02 mm at datum interfaces
Actuator housingsBearing seats to IT6 or similar fits; coaxiality of bores around 0.01–0.02 mm; bore finish Ra 0.8–1.6 µm
Strain-wave and RV reducer housingsTighter still: coaxiality and roundness in single microns, seat tolerances of a few microns, bore finish around Ra 0.4–0.8 µm
Links and armsStraightness and twist from the extrusion standard, with interfaces machined to datum after cutting

Achieving these numbers starts with the stock. Stress-relieved tempers, T651 for plate and T6511 for extruded bar, are stretched after quenching so that removing material does not release locked-in stress and distort the part. For thin-walled housings, rough machining, a rest period or low-temperature stabilisation, then finish machining is common practice. Unstretched T6 bar can move noticeably when deep pockets are cut.

Thermal expansion matters too. Aluminium expands about 23 µm/m·K, roughly twice steel, so a steel bearing in an aluminium seat loses interference as the motor warms up. Designers either specify the fit at operating temperature, use steel bearing liners, or choose bearing arrangements that tolerate the change.

Stock specifications that help precision parts

  • Order 6061-T651, 7075-T651 or 7050-T7451 plate rather than unstretched tempers
  • For bar, specify T6511 (extruded, stretched) or T651 (rolled or drawn, stretched)
  • Ask for ultrasonic inspection (for example to ASTM B594 or AMS-STD-2154) on thick plate for critical parts
  • Leave enough machining allowance to clean up the surface layer and any saw-cut edges

The most common mistakes we see in robot material specifications are easy to avoid: specifying 7075 everywhere when stiffness, not strength, is the limit; ordering unstretched T6 bar for thin-walled housings and then fighting distortion; forgetting the growth of hard anodising on bearing seats; and drawing parts around non-standard plate thicknesses that force expensive over-sized stock. Checking each drawing against standard stock sizes and stress-relieved tempers before the first prototype order usually removes these problems, and it makes the later switch to extrusion or forging far smoother because the datums and allowances are already defined.

Surface finishing

Anodising, hard anodising and other finishes

Finishing protects aluminium against corrosion and wear, provides electrical insulation where needed, and identifies parts. For robot parts it also changes dimensions, which must be designed in.

Alloy matters for the finish. 6061 and 6063 give the most uniform decorative anodising. 7075 anodises well but can look slightly yellow-grey in clear finishes. 2024 and high-silicon casting alloys anodise poorly and unevenly, so casting housings are often painted, powder coated or given a conversion coating instead.

Aluminium housings as heat paths for motors and drives

Frameless torque motors and their drives lose heat into the joint housing. Aluminium's thermal conductivity makes the housing itself the main heat path to the outer skin, and that affects alloy choice: 6061-T6 conducts about 167 W/m·K, 6063-T6 about 200 W/m·K, 7075-T6 about 130 W/m·K and typical die-casting alloys about 96–110 W/m·K.

For joints that run hot, a 6xxx housing or a 6063 heat-sink extrusion bonded to the motor can keep winding temperatures lower than a 7075 or cast housing of the same size. Where 7075 is needed for strength, designers often add fins, thermal pads or a 6063 heat spreader. Remember that sustained temperatures above about 100–150 °C gradually over-age 6xxx and 7xxx alloys and reduce their strength.

In battery packs and power electronics bays, the same logic applies: 6063 or 6061 extrusions with integral fins or cooling channels often double as structure and heat sink, which saves both mass and part count compared with a separate frame and cooler.

RFQ checklist

How to specify aluminium for a humanoid robot RFQ

  • Drawing or model: 2D drawing with GD&T, 3D model and revision number, or a stock list if you will machine in-house
  • Alloy and temper: e.g. 7075-T651 plate, 6061-T6511 bar; mark "to be advised" if you want a recommendation
  • Product form and size: plate thickness, bar diameter or section, extrusion drawing, forging blank drawing
  • Critical requirements: flatness, straightness, ultrasonic class, machining allowance
  • Finish: mill finish, anodising type and colour, masking areas
  • Documents: EN 10204 3.1 certificate, dimensional report, RoHS or REACH declarations if required
  • Quantities: prototype quantity, pilot batch and estimated annual volume
  • Delivery: destination, Incoterms (EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP) and packing requirements

Metline supplies the aluminium itself (plate, block, bar, flat bar, tube, custom extrusions and forgings) and can cut or machine it to your drawing. Teams that machine in-house often buy cut blanks; teams that outsource can send part drawings for review.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) for humanoid robots: FAQ

What is the best aluminum alloy for humanoid robots?

There is no single best alloy. 7075-T6/T651 suits compact, highly stressed joint parts and reducer housings; 6061 or 6082 suit frames, links and general brackets; 5052 suits formed covers and enclosures; and dedicated casting alloys suit die-cast housings at volume. Most robots use several of these.

Which humanoid robot parts are typically made from 7075?

Reducer and actuator housings with thin bearing walls, output flanges, finger and wrist links, hip and knee yokes, pelvis nodes and any small part carrying high or impact loads. For thick blocks, 7050-T7451 is often preferred for its toughness.

Can 7075 aluminium be die cast?

No. 7075 is a wrought alloy that is rolled, extruded or forged. Die-cast housings use foundry alloys such as A380 or A356-type alloys, which have lower strength and ductility, so bearing seats and highly loaded features are machined and designed accordingly.

Should robot joint parts be machined, forged or die cast?

Machine from plate or bar while the design is still changing. Once the design is frozen, forge compact high-load nodes for better fatigue and impact properties, and die cast complex housings when volumes justify tooling.

Why do robot parts distort after machining?

Usually because of residual quench stress in unstretched stock or heavy one-sided material removal. Ordering stress-relieved tempers (T651 plate, T6511 extruded bar), roughing and finishing in separate stages and keeping the part symmetrical all reduce distortion.

Does hard anodising change part dimensions?

Yes. A Type III hard anodic coating grows roughly half outward and half into the metal, so a 50 µm coating adds about 25 µm per surface. Finish-machine bores and fits with that growth in mind, or mask them.

Does Metline make robot parts?

Metline supplies the aluminium stock (plate, block, bar, tube, extrusions and forgings) and can cut or machine it to your drawing. We do not design or assemble robots or actuators.
