Aluminium Foam Noise Barrier Panels

Aluminium foam noise barrier panels absorb traffic and rail noise instead of simply reflecting it, while staying light, non-combustible and fully recyclable. Treated foam panels mounted with an air cavity behind them work as absorbers across the frequencies that matter for road and rail noise. This guide covers how they work, how they are tested to EN 1793, and how to specify them for highways, railways, metro stations and tunnels.

Overview

How an aluminium foam noise barrier works

A noise barrier does two jobs. It blocks the direct path from the source to the listener (sound insulation), and it should absorb the sound that strikes it so the energy is not reflected back across the road or railway to homes on the other side (sound absorption). Plain concrete, steel or transparent panels insulate well but reflect most sound.

Aluminium foam is produced with closed cells, which on their own reflect sound. For acoustic use the panel surface is opened by rolling, drilling or perforating so that sound waves can enter the cell structure, where friction and resonance inside the irregular pores convert acoustic energy into heat. Mounting the panel with an air gap behind it shifts the absorption peak towards lower frequencies, matching the dominant frequencies of tyre and engine noise.

The solid aluminium skeleton still provides mass and stiffness, so a foam panel also blocks transmitted sound. Combined with non-combustibility and weather resistance, this makes foam one of the few materials that absorbs sound without a fibrous infill that can sag, absorb water or shed fibres over the life of the barrier.

Why engineers choose foam panels

  • Absorbs sound without mineral wool or other fibrous infill
  • Light compared with concrete absorbers, which reduces foundation and post loads
  • Non-combustible and resistant to UV, moisture and freeze-thaw
  • Can be powder-coated in any colour for visual integration
  • Fully recyclable at end of life with other aluminium scrap

Standards

How noise barrier performance is tested

In Europe and in many countries that follow European practice, road traffic noise reducing devices are specified under EN 14388, with acoustic and non-acoustic performance proven by the EN 1793 and EN 1794 test series. Railway barriers follow the equivalent EN 16272 series.

StandardWhat it measuresHow it is reported
EN 1793-1Sound absorption in a reverberation roomSingle-number rating DLα (dB) and absorption category
EN 1793-2Airborne sound insulation in a laboratorySingle-number rating DLR (dB) and insulation category
EN 1793-5 / EN 1793-6In-situ sound reflection and insulationMeasured on installed barriers
EN 1794-1Mechanical performance: wind load, self-weight, impact of stonesPass / fail against design loads
EN 1794-2General safety and environmental requirements, including fire behaviourClassification of each property
ISO 354 / ISO 10534-2Absorption coefficients by reverberation room or impedance tubeFrequency-by-frequency absorption

When comparing quotations, compare DLα and DLR ratings of complete, tested panel systems rather than single absorption coefficients at one frequency. A panel that performs well at 1,000 Hz but poorly at 250 – 500 Hz may disappoint against low-frequency truck and rail noise.

Applications

Where foam noise barriers are used

Highways and urban expressways

Vertical, cantilevered or curved barriers along carriageways and viaducts, where light panels reduce load on bridge structures.

Railways and high-speed lines

Low trackside barriers close to the rail and taller barriers near homes. Panels must resist the repeated pressure pulses of passing trains.

Metro stations

Wall and ceiling linings that cut reverberation on platforms, improving speech intelligibility of announcements.

Road and rail tunnels

Absorptive linings on tunnel walls reduce the build-up of reflected noise and echo; non-combustibility is essential here.

Industrial sites

Barriers and enclosures around compressors, cooling towers, transformers and generators.

Fully and semi-enclosed sections

Gallery structures over roads in dense urban areas, combining foam panels with transparent sections for daylight.

Design

Design and installation considerations

  • Cavity: an air gap behind the foam (often several centimetres) improves low-frequency absorption; the panel frame and posts must hold this gap
  • Wind and dynamic loads: design posts and fixings to EN 1794-1 wind loads and, beside railways, to train-induced pressure pulses
  • Thermal movement: aluminium panels expand about twice as much as steel; use slotted fixings and elastomer seals
  • Galvanic isolation: separate foam panels from galvanised steel posts with EPDM gaskets or isolating washers
  • Sealing gaps: small gaps between panels and at ground level leak sound and reduce DLR; specify continuous seals and a base plinth
  • Coating: powder coating or plating plus coating for coastal, de-icing-salt and tunnel environments
  • Maintenance: open-surface panels can collect dust in tunnels; specify a surface that can be cleaned by washing

A typical highway module might use a foam panel of 10 – 20 mm in an aluminium frame, mounted with a cavity in front of a solid back sheet, slotted between steel H-posts at 2 – 2.5 m centres. Exact geometry should follow the tested system.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) foam noise barriers: FAQ

Does aluminium foam absorb sound or just block it?

Both, when it is treated. Opening the closed-cell surface by rolling or perforating lets sound enter the pores, where it is absorbed; the solid aluminium structure also blocks transmitted sound. Untreated closed-cell foam mostly reflects.

How is noise barrier performance rated?

Under EN 1793-1 for absorption (DLα) and EN 1793-2 for insulation (DLR), both expressed in decibels with performance categories. Compare ratings of complete tested systems.

Is aluminium foam suitable for tunnels?

Yes. It is non-combustible, contains no fibres and resists moisture, which suits tunnel linings. Choose a coating that can be washed and resists de-icing salts and exhaust deposits.

How long do foam noise barrier panels last?

Aluminium does not rot and resists UV. With a suitable coating and isolation from steel posts, panels are expected to last for decades; service life depends on environment and maintenance.

Can the panels be coloured?

Yes. Foam panels are commonly powder-coated in RAL colours, with coating thickness typically 40 µm or more for outdoor use.

Are aluminium foam barriers recyclable?

Yes. The panels are aluminium and can be remelted with other aluminium scrap at end of life, unlike barriers that contain mineral wool or plastics.
