Aluminium Oil Cooler Tube

Aluminium oil cooler tube is a thin-wall flat or rectangular tube, usually high-frequency welded from brazing-clad strip, that carries engine oil, transmission fluid or hydraulic oil through a brazed cooler core. Its job is to hold pressure, transfer heat to the fins and braze reliably to headers and turbulators. This guide covers tube types, clad materials, typical sizes, tolerances, brazing and inspection, so you can specify the tube with confidence.

Overview

What an oil cooler tube does

Oil coolers keep engine oil, automatic-transmission fluid, power-steering fluid and hydraulic oil within their working temperature range. Most modern coolers for vehicles and mobile machinery are all-aluminium brazed cores: rows of tubes carrying oil, separated by corrugated fins that carry air or coolant, joined to headers at each end in a single furnace brazing cycle.

The tube is the part that touches the oil, so it carries most of the engineering load. It must provide a defined flow path with an acceptable pressure drop for a viscous fluid; contain the operating pressure plus pressure spikes from cold starts, when thick oil can push pressure far above normal; transfer heat efficiently through a thin wall to the fins; and form a sealed, strong braze joint with fins, headers and any internal turbulator. It must also resist corrosion from the outside environment and keep enough strength after the brazing cycle, which anneals the metal.

Oil is far more viscous than water or refrigerant, and heat transfer on the oil side is usually the limiting factor in the cooler. That is why oil cooler tubes so often contain an inserted turbulator or a dimpled wall: both break up the slow, laminar oil flow near the wall and can raise heat transfer substantially, at the cost of a higher pressure drop.

What the tube must achieve

  • Hold operating pressure and survive pressure cycling and cold-start spikes
  • Braze leak-free to headers, fins and internal turbulators in one furnace cycle
  • Keep consistent geometry so automated core assembly runs without jams
  • Retain strength and corrosion resistance after the brazing heat cycle

The choice is mainly a trade-off between heat transfer, pressure drop, pressure capacity and cost. Inserted turbulators give the highest performance but need tube strip clad on the inside as well as outside so the insert brazes to the wall. Dimpled tubes avoid the insert and the extra assembly step. Plain tubes remain the low-cost choice where the cooler has enough frontal area.

Clad materials

Core alloys and brazing clad structures

Welded oil cooler tube is roll-formed from brazing sheet: a core alloy carrying the strength, with a thin layer of low-melting aluminium-silicon alloy bonded to one or both faces. During brazing the clad layer melts, flows into the joints by capillary action and freezes to form fillets, while the core stays solid. The clad thickness is expressed as a percentage of total strip thickness, commonly around 5 – 15 % per side.

Reference values frequently used for thin-wall welded oil cooler tube; final tolerances are agreed on the customer drawing.

Braze clearance is the most important reason for tight tolerances. Capillary flow of the molten clad works best in a narrow, even gap. If the tube varies in width or height, some joints are starved of filler while others pool it, which leads to leaks or blocked passages. Consistent tube geometry also keeps the core flat after brazing, which matters when the cooler is bolted into a tight space.

Joining

Designing the tube for reliable brazing

Controlled-atmosphere brazing (CAB)

The most common method for automotive coolers. Non-corrosive fluoride flux is applied to the core, which is brazed in a nitrogen atmosphere at a peak of roughly 595 – 610 °C. Works well with 3003 cores and 4343 or 4045 clad.

Vacuum brazing

No flux; magnesium in the clad or core helps break up oxide in vacuum. Common for stacked-plate and bar-and-plate coolers and for magnesium-bearing, higher-strength materials.

Clean surface

Low residual rolling oil and forming lubricant, so flux wets the surface and filler flows evenly. Excess oil leaves carbon and voids in joints.

Continuous weld seam

The HF weld seam must be fully fused, with the inside bead controlled so it does not obstruct turbulator insertion or trap flux.

Even joint gaps

Tube, header slot and fin dimensions are chosen together so gaps stay small and uniform across the core.

Post-braze strength

Brazing anneals the tube, so pressure capacity is calculated from post-braze (O-temper) properties of the core alloy, not its as-supplied strength.

Quality control

How oil cooler tube is inspected

  1. Eddy current testingIn-line eddy current inspection checks the wall and weld seam continuously for cracks, pinholes and incomplete fusion.
  2. Burst testingSample lengths are sealed and pressurised to failure to confirm pressure capacity and weld integrity.
  3. Dimensional measurementVision systems or gauges measure width, height, crown and corner radii against the drawing.
  4. Cross-section examinationProfile projection shows section shape and weld position; etched sections show clad thickness and weld fusion.
  5. Brazing trialSample cores brazed in the customer furnace confirm fillet formation and leak-tightness before volume release.

Ask for dimensional reports, burst test results and the mill test certificate (EN 10204 3.1) with each lot. For new programmes, a production part approval package agreed with your quality team avoids repeated qualification later.

For most oil coolers, welded clad tube wins because the cladding supplies filler for both the outer fins and the inner turbulator, and wall thickness can be chosen precisely for the pressure. Extruded tube is chosen when a seamless section or integral channels are preferred, and folded tube is more common in radiators and condensers. If you are unsure, send the operating conditions and core design and we will suggest the tube construction that fits.

Applications

Where aluminium oil cooler tubes are used

  • Engine oil coolers in cars, vans and motorcycles, often integrated with the radiator module
  • Transmission and gearbox oil coolers, including automatic and dual-clutch transmissions
  • Heavy trucks and buses, where wider tubes and stronger cores cope with higher heat loads and vibration
  • Construction, mining and agricultural machinery hydraulic oil coolers, typically using heavier rectangular tubes
  • Industrial lubrication systems, compressors, wind-turbine gearboxes and hydraulic power units
  • Power-steering and retarder coolers on commercial vehicles

A typical passenger-car transmission cooler might use 16 × 4 mm double-clad flat tube with a wall near 0.4 mm and an inserted turbulator, while a typical excavator hydraulic cooler uses rectangular tube of around 27 × 19 mm with a 1.5 mm wall, chosen for pressure capacity and resistance to rough service.

  1. DimensionsWidth, height, wall thickness, corner radii, length and tolerances, or a drawing.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) oil cooler tube: frequently asked questions

What material are aluminium oil cooler tubes made from?

Usually brazing sheet with a 3003 or modified 3xxx core and a 4343 or 4045 aluminium-silicon clad layer on one or both sides, roll-formed and high-frequency welded into a flat or rectangular tube.

Why is the tube clad on the inside as well as the outside?

Inner cladding provides filler metal to braze an inserted turbulator or touching dimples to the tube wall. Plain tubes with nothing inside need only outer cladding.

Turbulator or dimpled tube: which is better?

Inserted turbulators usually give higher heat transfer and add pressure strength but cost more and add an assembly step. Dimpled tubes are simpler and cheaper with a smaller heat-transfer gain. The best choice depends on oil viscosity, space and allowable pressure drop.

Can oil cooler tube be made to my drawing?

Yes. Width, height, wall, corner radii, weld position and internal structure can be made to drawing. New sizes usually need new forming rolls, so share the drawing early.

How is aluminum oil cooler tube tested?

In-line eddy current testing of the wall and weld seam, burst tests on samples, dimensional measurement, cross-section examination and, for new programmes, brazing trials in the customer furnace.

Is the tube suitable for vacuum brazing?

Yes, with the right material. Vacuum brazing uses magnesium-bearing clad or core alloys; CAB uses flux and low-magnesium materials. Tell us your process so the clad structure is matched.
