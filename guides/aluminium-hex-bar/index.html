Aluminium Hex Bar: Alloys, Sizes and Selection

Aluminium hex bar is solid six-sided bar used mainly as feedstock for nuts, fittings, standoffs, couplings and turned parts that need flats for a spanner or anti-rotation. The right choice depends on three things: the alloy (6061 for general use, 2011 or 6262 for high-speed turning, 2024 or 7075 for strength), the process (extruded or cold-finished) and the across-flats size. This guide compares them and gives weights for metric and imperial sizes.

Overview

What aluminium hex bar is used for

A hexagon gives a bar six flat faces at 120°, so a finished part can be gripped by a standard spanner or socket, cannot rotate in a hexagonal recess and sits accurately in a collet. Starting from hex stock means those flats need no machining: an automatic lathe simply drills, turns, threads and parts off.

This makes hex bar the standard raw material for nuts, coupling nuts, hydraulic and pneumatic fittings, hose adaptors, spacers and standoffs, valve bodies, bolt heads and electrical connectors. It also appears as a structural or decorative section in furniture, railings and instruments, and in tool handles where the flats give grip.

Because the part is usually turned from the bar, the selection questions are about machining as much as strength: how well the alloy breaks chips, how good a surface it leaves, whether threads are clean, whether it will anodise to the required colour, and whether it must meet RoHS limits on lead.

Advantages of starting from hex stock

  • Flats are ready-made, saving milling time and fixturing
  • Less material removed than turning a hexagon from round bar
  • Consistent across-flats size suits standard spanners and sockets
  • Feeds directly into multi-spindle and Swiss-type automatic lathes

6061 hex bar

The default choice. Strong, weldable, corrosion resistant and an excellent base for clear and coloured anodising. Supplied extruded (T6511) or cold-finished (T6/T651). Good for fittings, standoffs, structural nuts and general machined parts.

2011 hex bar

The classic free-machining alloy: lead and bismuth additions make chips break into tiny pieces, allowing very high spindle speeds and clean threads. Poor corrosion resistance, so parts are usually used indoors or coated. Lead-free 2011B-type and similar grades exist for RoHS-sensitive work.

6262 hex bar

A free-machining 6xxx alloy with strength similar to or above 6061-T6 and much better corrosion resistance and anodising than 2011. Widely used for fittings, valve parts and fasteners. T9 temper is cold-worked after heat treatment for extra strength.

2024 hex bar

High strength and fatigue resistance for aerospace fasteners and highly loaded fittings. Needs anodising or coating for corrosion protection.

7075 hex bar

The strongest common hex bar. T6 for maximum strength; T73 or T7351 when resistance to stress-corrosion cracking matters, as it often does in aerospace fittings and fasteners.

6063 hex bar

Lower strength but excellent surface finish and anodising. Used for decorative rods, furniture fittings and light-duty spacers rather than threaded load-bearing parts.

For marine service, 5xxx hexagon bar (5052, 5083, 5086) is also produced; see our 5083 marine hexagon bar page for those grades.

Bar-fed automatics work best with cold-finished bar because consistent size and straightness keep the bar running smoothly through the feeder and collet. For larger sizes, saw-cut blanks or structural uses, extruded bar is usually the more economical choice. Stress-relieved tempers (T6511, T651) are worth specifying whenever parts are long or asymmetrically machined.

Dimensions

Hex bar sizes and weight per metre

Hex bar is ordered by the distance across flats (AF). The distance across corners is AF × 1.155, which matters when checking whether bar will pass through a collet or spindle bore. Cross-sectional area is 0.866 × AF², so weight in kg/m is about 0.00234 × AF² (AF in mm) at a density of 2.70 g/cm³. 7075 and 2011 are slightly denser (about 2.81 and 2.83 g/cm³), so add roughly 4–5 % for those alloys.

Working with hex bar

Machining, threading and finishing tips

Chip control

2011 and 6262 produce small broken chips and suit unattended automatics. 6061 and 7075 give longer chips; use chip-breaker inserts, high-pressure coolant and correct feeds. 6063 tends to be gummy and is better kept for decorative parts.

Threading

All these alloys thread well with sharp, polished tools. For repeatedly assembled threads, 2024, 7075 or 6262-T9 resist thread wear better than 6061 or 6063; thread inserts are another option.

Collets and feeders

Use hex collets matched to the across-flats size. Check the across-corners dimension against spindle bore and feeder tube.

Anodising

6061, 6063 and 6262 give bright, even anodised finishes in clear and colour. 2011 and 2024 anodise with a duller, uneven appearance, so specify them only for protective anodising.

Corrosion

Avoid unprotected 2011 or 2024 parts outdoors or in wet service. Use 6061 or 6262 instead, or anodise. In contact with stainless or carbon steel, isolate to limit galvanic corrosion.

RoHS and lead

2011 and 6262 traditionally contain lead. Where RoHS or similar limits apply, specify lead-free free-machining grades and confirm compliance on the certificate.

Selection

How to choose the right hex bar

  1. Define the part dutyIs the part load-bearing, threaded, decorative, outdoor, or conductive? This decides between strength, machinability and corrosion resistance.
  2. Pick the alloy familyGeneral: 6061. High-volume turned parts: 6262 or 2011. High strength: 2024 or 7075 (T73 for stress-corrosion resistance). Decorative: 6063.
  3. Choose the processCold-finished for automatic lathes and tight tolerances; extruded for larger sizes and structural uses.
  4. Set size and lengthPick a standard AF size close to the finished flats. Specify random or fixed lengths to suit your bar feeder.
  5. Add requirementsStandard and temper, certification (EN 10204 3.1), RoHS declaration, packing and Incoterms.

Common mistakes when buying hex bar

  • Ordering 2011 for outdoor or marine parts, where it corrodes
  • Forgetting that across-corners size, not across-flats, must clear the spindle bore
  • Specifying non-stretched tempers for long parts that then bend after machining
  • Assuming every free-machining grade is lead-free

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) hex bar: frequently asked questions

Which aluminum alloy is best for hex bar?

6061 is the best general choice: strong, corrosion resistant and easy to anodise. For high-volume turned parts choose 6262 or 2011, for high strength choose 2024 or 7075, and for decorative parts choose 6063.

How do I calculate the weight of aluminium hex bar?

Area is 0.866 × AF². For 6061 at 2.70 g/cm³, weight in kg/m is about 0.00234 × AF² with AF in mm, so 20 mm AF weighs about 0.94 kg/m. In imperial units, weight is about 1.01 lb/ft × AF² with AF in inches.

What is the difference between extruded and cold-finished hex bar?

Extruded bar comes straight from the extrusion die and is heat treated and stretched. Cold-finished bar is drawn through a die afterwards, giving tighter tolerances, sharper corners and a brighter surface, which suits automatic lathes.

Is hex bar better than round bar for turned parts?

If the finished part needs flats, yes: starting from hex saves milling, reduces material removed and gives consistent flats. If the part is round all over, round bar is cheaper and easier to feed.

Is 2011 hex bar RoHS compliant?

Traditional 2011 contains lead and bismuth, so compliance depends on lead content and the applicable exemption. Lead-free free-machining grades are available; ask for a RoHS declaration with the certificate.

What tolerances apply to aluminium hex bar?

ANSI H35.2 for ASTM B211 and B221 orders, and EN 754-6 (drawn) or EN 755-6 (extruded) for EN orders. Cold-finished bar has tighter tolerances than extruded bar of the same size.
