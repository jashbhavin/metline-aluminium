Aluminium Rectangular Tube: Standard Die Sizes and Custom Tooling

Every extruded rectangular tube is pushed through a hollow (porthole) die made for its exact width, height and wall, so the sizes that already have tooling are the fastest and cheapest to buy. This guide lists common sharp-corner rectangular and square tube sizes with their weight per metre, explains when a new die is needed, and sets out the design rules, alloys, tolerances and finishes that apply to custom rectangular tube.

Overview

Why the die matters when buying rectangular tube

Rectangular tube is a hollow extrusion, so it needs a two-part porthole die: a mandrel that forms the inside and a die plate that forms the outside. The die is machined from hot-work tool steel for one specific section, and its cost and trial time are the main reasons a custom size takes longer than a standard one.

Hundreds of rectangular sizes already have tooling in the extrusion supply chain because they are used every day in frames, furniture, machinery, vehicles and buildings. Choosing one of those sizes means no tooling charge, no die trial and a proven section that the press runs consistently. When a design truly needs a different size, wall or corner shape, a new die is made and the tube is produced from your drawing.

This page is a practical reference. The tables give standard sharp-corner rectangular and square sizes with calculated weights; the later sections explain how dies are designed, which features need new tooling, and how to specify a custom section so that it extrudes well the first time.

Benefits of choosing an existing die size

  • No tooling cost and no die trial, so the first delivery is faster
  • The section has a production history, so wall and corner quality are known
  • Easy repeat orders and substitution between alloys on the same die
  • A near-standard size often meets the design without custom tooling
Tooling basics

How a porthole die forms a rectangular tube

A heated aluminium billet is pushed into the die under high pressure. The metal divides into several streams as it flows through ports around the mandrel support bridges, then re-joins in a welding chamber before it passes through the gap between mandrel and die plate. That gap is the tube wall.

Seam (extrusion) welds

Where the streams re-join, solid-state seams form along the tube length. In a correctly run die they are as strong as the parent metal and invisible after finishing, but they are why porthole tube is not treated as "seamless" pipe for code pressure parts.

Bearing length

The land that controls metal speed. Die makers vary bearing length around the section so thick and thin parts of the profile exit at the same speed, which keeps the tube straight and the walls flat.

Corner radius

Sharp outside corners are standard on architectural tube, typically with a small internal radius. Rounded-corner tube needs different tooling and has a specified outside radius.

Die size and press

The circumscribing circle diameter (CCD), the smallest circle that encloses the section, decides the die and press size. A 100 × 45 mm tube has a CCD of about 110 mm.

Die life

Dies wear and are periodically re-nitrided or corrected. A die in regular use is a known quantity; a new die needs a trial and sometimes correction runs.

Multi-cavity dies

Small tubes are often run two or more at a time from one die, improving output and price for small sections.

Design for extrusion

Design rules for a custom rectangular tube

  • Keep walls uniform. Where walls must differ, keep the ratio of thickest to thinnest wall within about 2 : 1 to avoid uneven metal flow, distortion and surface streaks after anodising.
  • Respect minimum wall for the size. Minimum practical wall rises with CCD and with harder alloys: 6063 allows the thinnest walls, 6061 and 6082 need more.
  • Add small internal radii. An internal corner radius of about 0.5 – 1 mm improves die life and metal flow without changing the outside look.
  • Avoid very wide, thin flat walls. They sag or concave during extrusion; add a slight crown, a rib or more thickness.
  • Specify only the tolerances you need. Standard EN 755-9 or ANSI H35.2 tolerances are cheaper and faster than special ones.
  • Mark decorative faces. Tell us which faces will be visible after anodising so the die can be designed to keep seam welds and die lines away from them.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) rectangular tube dies: frequently asked questions

Do I pay for a die if my rectangular tube size is in the standard list?

No. If the width, height, wall and corner style match an existing die, the tube is produced directly with no tooling charge. A tooling cost applies only when a new die has to be made.

Can the same die make a tube with a thicker wall?

Generally not. The wall is the gap between mandrel and die plate, so a different wall needs a new mandrel or a new die. Ask us, because a nearby existing size may meet your load requirement.

Is extruded rectangular tube seamless?

No. It is made in a porthole die, which forms longitudinal seam welds where metal streams re-join. They are sound for structural and general use, but code pressure piping normally requires seamless tube made over a mandrel.

What alloy should I choose for rectangular tube?

6063-T5 or T6 for architectural, furniture and anodised parts; 6061-T6 for structural frames and welded assemblies; 6005A or 6082 for thin-wall structural sections and European specifications.

How do I calculate the weight of aluminum rectangular tube?

Multiply 2 × wall × (width + height − 2 × wall), all in mm, by 0.0027 to get kg per metre. For example, 50 × 25 × 2 mm tube weighs about 0.77 kg/m.

What tolerances apply to rectangular tube?

EN 755-9 for general profiles, EN 12020-2 for precision 6060/6063 sections, and ANSI H35.2 for orders to ASTM B221. Tighter tolerances can be agreed for a custom die.

Have a rectangular tube size in mind?

Send the dimensions or drawing, alloy, finish and quantity. We will check existing tooling and reply with a price and delivery.
