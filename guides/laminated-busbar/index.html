Laminated Busbar: Structure, Materials and Aluminium Conductor Layers

A laminated busbar is a stack of thin, flat conductor plates separated by insulating film and bonded into one rigid part, so that the positive and negative current paths sit a fraction of a millimetre apart. That geometry cancels most of the magnetic field, cuts stray inductance to a few nanohenries and keeps switching overvoltage under control in inverters, converters and battery systems. This guide explains how the design works and how to specify the aluminium conductor layers, which Metline supplies as cut-to-drawing sheet, plate and strip.

Overview

What is a laminated busbar?

A laminated busbar replaces a bundle of cables or separate bars with a single engineered sandwich: conductor, insulation, conductor, insulation, sealed at the edges and fitted with tabs, holes or press-in terminals where devices and capacitors connect.

The reason for building it this way is inductance. Every conductor loop stores magnetic energy, and when a power transistor (IGBT, SiC or GaN MOSFET) switches off a large current in tens of nanoseconds, that stored energy appears as a voltage spike: V = L × di/dt. Switching 1,000 A in 100 ns gives di/dt of 10 kA/µs; with 20 nH of loop inductance the spike is about 200 V, with 5 nH it falls to about 50 V. A smaller spike lets the designer use devices closer to their voltage rating, run faster switching edges and reduce snubber components.

Putting the DC+ and DC− plates face to face, separated only by insulation film, makes currents flow in opposite directions over a wide area. Their magnetic fields largely cancel, which is why laminated busbars are standard in traction inverters, wind and solar converters, industrial drives, UPS systems, rail propulsion and battery energy storage.

What a laminated busbar delivers

  • Low and repeatable stray inductance, often in the single-digit to low tens of nanohenries
  • Lower EMI, because the current loop area is small
  • Compact layout with several circuits integrated into one part and fewer assembly errors
  • Large flat surfaces that spread heat and can be bonded to a cold plate
  • Rigid construction that tolerates vibration better than cable harnesses
How it is built

Layers of a laminated busbar

Most designs follow the same build-up, whatever the conductor metal. The number of layers depends on the circuit: a two-level DC link needs two conductors, a three-level converter usually needs three or four, and multi-phase assemblies can exceed six.

LayerTypical materialFunction and design notes
Conductor platesAluminium 1350, 1060, 1070 or 6101; copper C11000 or C10200Carry current. Thickness usually 0.5 – 3 mm; width set by current density and temperature rise.
Inter-layer insulationPolyester (PET) film, polyimide (PI) film, aramid paper, epoxy-glass laminateIsolates potentials. Film thickness of roughly 0.1 – 0.5 mm per layer, chosen for voltage, partial discharge and temperature.
Adhesive systemThermoset or hot-melt adhesive coated on the filmBonds the stack under heat and pressure without voids, which would cause partial discharge.
Edge treatmentFilm overhang, edge fill, epoxy powder coating or encapsulationProvides creepage distance around the plate edges and keeps out moisture and dust.
TerminalsTabs formed from the plate, press-in nuts or studs, bushingsConnect to modules, capacitors and cables; usually plated for stable contact resistance.
Outer insulationPET, PI or powder coatProtects personnel and adjacent parts; often colour-coded by potential.

Inductance of two parallel plates is roughly proportional to the plate separation divided by the plate width (L ≈ μ0 × length × separation / width). That simple relationship explains the design rules: keep the insulation as thin as voltage and partial-discharge limits allow, make the plates wide, and keep the DC+ and DC− plates overlapping all the way to the device terminals. As an example, a 300 mm long, 100 mm wide plate pair separated by 0.25 mm has a calculated loop inductance of about 1 nH before terminal and via effects are added; real assemblies land higher because of holes, cut-outs and terminal geometry.

Conductor choice

Aluminium or copper conductor layers?

Copper has been the default because it packs the most conductivity into the smallest volume. Aluminium has become common in battery packs, energy storage systems and EV drive units because it removes about half the conductor mass for the same resistance, costs less per ampere and is easy to cut, punch and form.

The practical consequence is that an aluminium layer is thicker or wider than the copper layer it replaces. Because loop inductance depends on separation and width rather than conductor thickness, a wider aluminium plate can actually help inductance; a thicker one increases the overall stack height slightly. Where space is very tight and currents are high, copper still wins; where weight and cost dominate, as in most battery and storage systems, aluminium is usually the better engineering choice.

1350 / 1370

The electrical conductor grade, 99.5 % aluminium or better, with controlled trace elements for high conductivity. Best choice for flat plates that are only lightly formed.

1060 / 1070

Commercially pure grades with conductivity close to 1350, widely available as sheet and coil. Common for punched and bent laminated plates.

6101 (T6, T61, T63, T64)

6060 / 6063

Dielectric layers

Choosing the insulation system

Insulation selection balances three things: dielectric strength (and partial-discharge inception voltage), operating temperature, and thickness, because every extra micrometre raises inductance. Voids, wrinkles and sharp plate edges are where failures start, so the lamination process and edge design matter as much as the film itself.

MaterialTypical useStrengthsLimits
Polyester (PET) filmMost low- and medium-voltage laminated busbarsLow cost, good dielectric strength, easy to laminateLower temperature ceiling than PI
Polyimide (PI) filmHigh-temperature zones near power modules, aerospaceHigh temperature rating, thin for its strengthHigher cost; absorbs some moisture
Aramid paperEdge wraps, high-temperature insulationThermal endurance, conformabilityThicker than films for the same voltage
Epoxy-glass laminate (FR4 / G10 type)Rigid spacers and thick insulation between sub-assembliesMechanical stiffness, good machinabilityThick; raises inductance
Epoxy powder coatingEdge sealing and full encapsulationSeals edges, adds creepageCoating thickness must be controlled

A widely quoted design rule in the industry is about 1 mm of rigid insulation per kilovolt between sub-assemblies, but the governing requirement is the partial-discharge limit: the electric field (voltage divided by distance) at the worst point must stay below the level at which discharges start. Designs for altitude, such as aircraft or high-altitude solar sites, need extra margin because partial-discharge inception voltage falls as air pressure drops. Creepage and clearance at exposed edges follow the insulation-coordination rules in IEC 60664-1 for the relevant pollution degree and overvoltage category.

Engineering

Design rules engineers use

  1. Size the conductor for temperature riseStart from RMS current and allowable temperature rise (often 30 – 50 K above ambient). Aluminium plates need about 1.6 × the cross-section of copper for the same DC resistance; at high frequencies, skin and proximity effects favour wide, thin plates.
  2. Maximise overlapKeep DC+ and DC− plates overlapping right up to the device and capacitor terminals. Every gap, slot or detour adds inductance.
  3. Minimise insulation thickness safelyUse the thinnest film that meets dielectric, partial-discharge and lifetime targets, with margin for altitude and temperature.
  4. Design the edgesUse film overhang, edge fill or encapsulation to achieve creepage. Radius the plate corners and deburr all holes; sharp edges concentrate electric field.
  5. Plan terminals and platingAluminium forms an oxide film instantly, so contact areas are normally tin- or nickel-plated, or fitted with bimetallic inserts where they bolt to copper.
  6. Control joint pressureAluminium creeps under sustained bolt load more than copper. Use larger washers or conical spring washers and the torque values specified for aluminium joints.
  7. Model and verifyExtract inductance with 3D field simulation, then measure it on the prototype with a double-pulse test before releasing the design.

For the aluminium layers, the most useful document is the mill test certificate. It should show the alloy and temper, chemical analysis and, for conductor grades, the measured electrical conductivity or resistivity. Ask for conductivity to be reported in % IACS or MS/m so it can be compared directly with your thermal model.

Practical advice

Common mistakes when specifying aluminium layers

  • Copying copper thicknesses: an aluminium plate of the same size has about 1.6 × the resistance. Re-run the thermal calculation rather than swapping the metal.
  • Ignoring the oxide film: bare aluminium contact faces without plating or proper joint preparation lead to rising contact resistance over time.
  • Choosing a structural alloy by habit: 6061 or 6082 are strong but have noticeably lower conductivity than 6101 or 1350. Use conductor grades unless strength is the governing requirement.
  • Leaving burrs on punched holes: burrs pierce thin insulation films and create partial-discharge sites. Specify deburring on the drawing.
  • Forming hard tempers too tightly: 1xxx grades in H14/H18 or 6101-T6 crack on tight bends. Order a softer temper (O or H111 for 1xxx grades; T61 or T64 rather than T6 for 6101) for parts with tight tabs, or allow a larger bend radius.
  • Overlooking galvanic contact: bolting bare aluminium directly to copper in a humid environment invites corrosion; use plated surfaces or bimetallic transition pieces.

FAQ

Laminated busbar: frequently asked questions

Can a laminated busbar use aluminum instead of copper?

Yes. Aluminium conductor layers (1350, 1060 or 6101) are widely used in battery packs, energy storage and inverters. They need roughly 1.6 times the cross-section of copper for the same resistance but weigh about half as much. Contact areas are normally plated, and joints are designed for aluminium.

How low is the inductance of a laminated busbar?

Well-designed laminated busbars typically reach single-digit to a few tens of nanohenries at the device terminals, depending on plate width, insulation thickness and terminal layout. Cable or separated-bar connections are usually far higher.

Which aluminium grade is best for laminated busbar plates?

1350 or 1060/1070 for flat or lightly formed plates where conductivity is the priority; 6101 in T6, T61 or T64 where plates or tabs must be stiffer. Avoid general structural alloys unless strength governs, because their conductivity is lower.

What insulation is used between the layers?

Polyester (PET) film is the most common; polyimide (PI) is used for higher temperatures; aramid paper, epoxy-glass laminate and epoxy powder coating are used for edges, spacers and encapsulation.

How are laminated busbars tested?

Typical tests are dielectric withstand (hi-pot), partial discharge to the IEC 60270 method, insulation resistance, stray inductance by double-pulse test and temperature rise. The aluminium layers are checked against their mill certificate for alloy, temper and conductivity.

Does Metline make finished laminated busbars?

No. Metline supplies the aluminium conductor stock (sheet, plate, strip, flat bar and copper-clad aluminium), cut or machined to your flat pattern with mill certificates, to busbar makers and OEMs worldwide. Lamination and insulation are done by the assembler.
