Brazed Aluminium Honeycomb Panel

A brazed aluminium honeycomb panel is a sandwich of two aluminium skins and an aluminium honeycomb core joined by a brazing alloy instead of glue. Because every joint is metal to metal, the panel contains no organic adhesive, tolerates far higher temperatures than bonded panels, can be welded at its edges and is fully recyclable. That makes it a strong candidate for rail, marine, building facade and other uses where fire performance and long-term durability matter.

Overview

What makes a brazed honeycomb panel different

Most aluminium honeycomb panels are made by laminating skins to a core with epoxy or polyurethane film adhesive. The adhesive is the weak link: it softens with heat, can age in humidity and burns in a fire. A brazed panel replaces it with an aluminium-silicon filler metal that melts at around 577 – 600 °C, flows into the contact lines between core cell walls and skins, and solidifies as a continuous metallic fillet.

The result is a single all-aluminium structure. The core no longer depends on a polymer to transfer shear, so strength is maintained at temperatures where a bonded panel would delaminate, and there is nothing in the panel to emit smoke or toxic gases. The same metallurgical joint lets fabricators weld edge profiles, inserts and frames directly to the panel, something that would destroy an adhesive bond.

The trade-offs are cost and size. Brazing needs a large furnace, careful fixturing and tight control of temperature, so brazed panels are more expensive per square metre than bonded panels and are limited by furnace dimensions. They are chosen when fire, heat, outgassing or weldability requirements rule out adhesives.

In short, bonded panels remain the economical choice for interior partitions, signage and general cladding. Brazed panels earn their premium in rail car floors and walls, ship accommodation and fire divisions, high-temperature enclosures, clean and vacuum equipment, and facades where a non-combustible panel with a long life is required.

Materials

Alloys used in brazed honeycomb panels

ComponentCommon alloysWhy
Honeycomb core foil3003 (Al-Mn)Good brazeability, corrosion resistance and strength after the brazing cycle; melts well above the filler
Brazing filler4004 / 4104 (vacuum brazing), 4343 / 4045 / 4047 (flux brazing)Al-Si alloys with a melting range below the core and skins
SkinsBrazing sheet with a 3003 or 6xxx core and Al-Si cladding, or plain skins with separate filler foilCladding places filler exactly at the joint and simplifies assembly
Edge profiles and inserts6xxx extrusions or 3003 sectionsBrazed in the same cycle or welded afterwards

Vacuum brazing uses magnesium-bearing filler such as 4004 or 4104; the magnesium evaporates and breaks up the oxide film without flux, leaving a clean surface with no residue. Controlled-atmosphere brazing with a non-corrosive fluoroaluminate flux uses 4343 or 4045 filler and is less sensitive to furnace vacuum but leaves a thin flux residue. High-magnesium 5xxx alloys are generally avoided for brazed panels because magnesium interferes with wetting and lowers the solidus close to the brazing temperature.

Brazing heats the whole panel close to 600 °C, which fully anneals work-hardened skins. Design calculations should use annealed (O-temper) properties for non-heat-treatable skins.

Process

How brazed honeycomb panels are produced

  1. Core preparationAluminium foil is printed, stacked and expanded or corrugated into honeycomb, then cut to the panel thickness with flat, square faces so every cell wall touches the skins.
  2. AssemblySkins with Al-Si cladding, or plain skins with filler foil, are placed on each side of the core with edge members. Surfaces are cleaned and degreased.
  3. FixturingThe stack is held flat under light, even pressure in a fixture that compensates for thermal expansion during heating.
  4. BrazingThe assembly is heated in a vacuum or controlled-atmosphere furnace to just above the filler liquidus, typically in the 590 – 610 °C range, held briefly so the filler flows into the fillets, and cooled.
  5. FinishingPanels are inspected, trimmed, machined and, where required, welded to frames, then painted, anodised or powder coated.

Joint quality depends on flatness of the core faces, cleanliness, uniform temperature across a large panel and correct filler quantity. Too little filler leaves unbonded cell walls; too much can erode thin core foil. Ultrasonic or tap testing and destructive peel tests on sample coupons are used to confirm bonding.

Performance

Performance characteristics

Stiffness to weight

Like all sandwich panels, a brazed panel separates two thin skins with a light core, giving very high bending stiffness for its weight.

Peel and shear

Metallurgical fillets typically make the joint stronger than the core foil, so peel tests usually tear the core rather than the joint.

Heat and fire

With no organic adhesive, the panel keeps its integrity at temperatures far above bonded panels and adds no fuel to a fire. Fire classification still depends on the full system tested.

Impact and indentation

Thicker skins and smaller cells resist point loads such as heel and trolley traffic on floors.

Acoustic and thermal

Sound and heat transmission depend on thickness, cell size and any infill; values must come from tests on the specific build.

Corrosion

3003 core and skins resist atmospheric and humid exposure well; flux-free vacuum brazing leaves no residue to cause corrosion.

Published figures for brazed panels vary widely with skin thickness, core density and test standard, so buyers should ask for test reports on the exact construction (panel weight, flexural strength, flatwise compression and tension, climbing drum peel, and the relevant fire test) rather than relying on generic numbers.

Applications

Where brazed aluminium honeycomb panels are used

  • Rail vehicles: floors, side walls, ceilings and equipment covers that must meet rail fire rules and carry heavy, repeated loads
  • Shipbuilding: accommodation partitions, decks and fire divisions on fast ferries, cruise ships and naval craft
  • Building facades and soffits: large flat panels where a non-combustible, long-life cladding is required
  • Aerospace and ground equipment: panels near heat sources, and structures that must not outgas
  • Industrial equipment: oven walls, heat shields, clean-room partitions and vacuum or semiconductor enclosures
  • Electronics cooling: brazed honeycomb is also used as a lightweight structural heat exchanger element
Ordering

How to specify a brazed honeycomb panel

  • Overall panel size and thickness, with flatness tolerance
  • Skin alloy and thickness for each face, and surface finish
  • Core alloy, cell size and foil thickness, or a target core density
  • Edge closures, inserts, cut-outs and any welding to frames
  • Performance targets: load case, deflection limit, fire classification, acoustic rating
  • Required test reports and inspection, and the delivery destination

FAQ

Brazed aluminium (aluminum) honeycomb panels: FAQ

What is the main advantage of a brazed honeycomb panel?

It has no adhesive. The core and skins are joined by an aluminium-silicon braze, so the panel is all metal: it tolerates high temperatures, adds no fuel to a fire, does not age like a polymer and can be welded at its edges.

Can brazed aluminium honeycomb panels be welded?

Yes. Edge profiles, frames and inserts can be TIG or MIG welded with controlled heat input, because there is no adhesive bond to damage. Welding very close to thin core foil still needs care.

Are brazed panels non-combustible?

They contain no organic adhesive, which is the main fuel source in bonded panels. A formal classification such as EN 13501-1 A1 or an IMO non-combustibility rating depends on testing the full product, including any coating.

Why are brazed panels more expensive than bonded panels?

Brazing needs large vacuum or atmosphere furnaces, precise fixtures and careful temperature control, and panel size is limited by the furnace. Bonded panels use simpler presses and adhesives.

Which alloys are used?

The core is usually 3003 honeycomb. Skins are typically brazing sheet with an Al-Si (4xxx) cladding, or plain skins with a separate filler foil. High-magnesium 5xxx alloys are generally avoided because magnesium hinders brazing.
