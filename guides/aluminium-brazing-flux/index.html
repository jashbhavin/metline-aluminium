Aluminium Brazing Flux: Types, Use & Selection

Aluminium brazing flux removes and holds back the tough oxide film on aluminium so that molten aluminium-silicon filler can wet and flow into the joint. The main choice is between non-corrosive potassium fluoroaluminate flux, used in controlled-atmosphere furnace brazing of heat exchangers, and corrosive chloride flux, used mainly for torch and repair work. This guide explains how each works, which suits which alloy and process, and how to apply, store and buy it.

Principle

Why aluminium needs a brazing flux

Aluminium forms an oxide skin within moments of exposure to air. The film is only nanometres thick, but it melts above 2,000 °C and is chemically stable, so molten filler metal will simply bead up on it instead of wetting the surface.

Aluminium brazing is done with aluminium-silicon fillers (4343, 4045, 4047) that melt between about 577 °C and 615 °C, only a few tens of degrees below the solidus of the parent metal. There is no time or temperature margin to "burn through" the oxide. The flux must melt just before the filler, dissolve or loosen the oxide, protect the cleaned surface from re-oxidising and then get out of the way as the filler flows.

Two routes avoid flux entirely: vacuum brazing, which uses magnesium in the filler to disrupt the oxide under high vacuum, and some newer flux-free controlled-atmosphere processes. Everything else, from automotive radiators made by the million to a technician repairing an AC line with a torch, relies on flux.

What a good aluminium flux must do

  • Melt a little below the filler metal’s melting range
  • Break up and dissolve the aluminium oxide film
  • Wet the joint and protect it from re-oxidation until the filler flows
  • Leave a residue that is either harmless or easy to wash off

Under ISO 18496 aluminium fluxes form class FL, which separates hygroscopic, corrosive chloride-based fluxes from non-hygroscopic fluoride-based fluxes whose residues are not corrosive. In North America, AWS A5.31 classifies aluminium brazing fluxes in its FB1 group by form and activity range. Check the classification on the supplier’s data sheet against your procedure.

Process

How fluoride flux works in CAB furnace brazing

Most aluminium brazing by volume is controlled-atmosphere brazing (CAB) of heat exchangers: radiators, condensers, evaporators, charge-air coolers and battery cooling plates. Parts are made from brazing sheet clad with 4343 or 4045 filler, assembled, fluxed, dried and passed through a continuous nitrogen furnace.

  1. DegreaseRemove forming oils, by thermal degreasing or washing. Residual oil prevents flux from adhering and causes poor joints.
  2. FluxApply flux as a water-based slurry by spraying or dipping, or electrostatically as dry powder. The target is a uniform coat, commonly in the region of 3 – 5 g/m² on the surfaces to be joined, adjusted by part design and trials.
  3. DryDrive off water in a drying oven before the brazing furnace, so moisture does not enter the nitrogen atmosphere.
  4. HeatIn nitrogen with low oxygen and dew point, the flux melts at about 560 – 575 °C, dissolves the oxide and spreads over the joint.
  5. BrazeAt around 590 – 610 °C the Al-Si cladding melts and flows into joints by capillary action. Time at peak temperature is kept short to limit silicon diffusion into the core.
  6. CoolThe filler solidifies; a thin, adherent flux residue remains on the surface and is normally not removed.

Alloy compatibility

Magnesium: the main limit on fluoride flux

Magnesium in the parent alloy diffuses to the surface during heating and reacts with potassium fluoroaluminate flux, forming high-melting magnesium fluoride compounds that poison the flux. Above roughly 0.3 – 0.5 % Mg, standard non-corrosive flux struggles to produce sound joints in CAB. This is why typical CAB core alloys (3003, 3005 and modified 3xxx grades) are low in magnesium.

Product forms

Powder, slurry, paste and flux-cored filler

Dry powder

For electrostatic application on CAB lines, or mixed in-house into slurry. Particle size affects how the powder sprays and adheres.

Water-based slurry

Flux suspended in water, sometimes with a binder, for spray or dip fluxing of whole cores.

Flux paste

Flux in a binder for brushing, dispensing or screen printing onto specific joints. Useful for cold plates and local brazing.

Filler + flux paste

Al-Si filler powder premixed with flux, applied directly to joints that have no clad filler.

Flux-cored or flux-coated rod

Filler rod or wire with flux inside or on the surface, for flame and induction brazing of tubes and repairs.

Pre-fluxed brazing sheet

Clad sheet with flux applied at the mill, removing the fluxing step from the customer’s line.

For torch and repair work, combine flux with a suitable filler: see our aluminium brazing rods and flux-core aluminium brazing rods.

Handling

Storage, handling and safety

  • Keep containers sealed and dry. Chloride fluxes absorb moisture readily; fluoride powders cake if damp.
  • Re-mix slurries before use, because flux settles out of suspension.
  • Fluoride fluxes release fumes during brazing; use local exhaust ventilation and follow the supplier’s safety data sheet.
  • Use dust masks or respirators when handling dry powder, and avoid skin and eye contact.
  • Observe the shelf life on the label, and treat brazing wastes and washing water according to local regulations.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) brazing flux: frequently asked questions

What flux is used for brazing aluminium?

For production furnace brazing, a non-corrosive potassium fluoroaluminate flux. For torch repairs, either a non-corrosive fluoride flux or a more active chloride flux whose residue must be washed off. Caesium-modified fluoride flux is used when the alloy contains magnesium.

Does non-corrosive flux residue need to be removed?

Normally no. The residue is a thin, insoluble, non-hygroscopic layer that does not attack aluminium and is left on most heat exchangers. Chloride flux residue, by contrast, must always be removed.

Can I braze 6061 with standard flux?

Usually not reliably in CAB, because the magnesium in 6061 poisons standard fluoroaluminate flux. Use a caesium-modified flux and confirm by trial, or consider vacuum brazing.

At what temperature does aluminium brazing flux melt?

Potassium fluoroaluminate fluxes melt at about 560 – 575 °C depending on their exact composition, just below the 577 °C eutectic at which Al-Si fillers begin to melt. Caesium-modified fluxes melt somewhat lower.

How much flux should be applied in CAB brazing?

Loads commonly fall in the region of 3 – 5 g/m² on joint surfaces, but the right value depends on part design, alloy and furnace; establish it by brazing trials and control it with regular weight checks.

Can flux be used for aluminium-to-copper joints?

Yes, with suitable fillers and fluxes, but the joint behaves differently from aluminium-to-aluminium brazing. See our aluminium-to-copper brazing guidance before choosing materials.
