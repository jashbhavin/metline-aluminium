Aluminium Foam for Construction

Aluminium foam is a porous metal, typically 70 – 90 % air, that gives architects a non-combustible, moisture-proof and recyclable panel for facades, ceilings, partitions and acoustic linings. Closed-cell foam brings stiffness, impact absorption and a distinctive metallic texture; open-cell foam brings sound absorption and airflow. This guide explains the types, the properties that matter on a building project and how to specify foam panels correctly.

Overview

What is aluminium foam and why use it in buildings?

Aluminium foam is aluminium or an aluminium alloy expanded into a cellular structure, like a metal sponge. A typical construction-grade foam weighs roughly a tenth to a third of solid aluminium, so a 20 mm foam panel can weigh less than a 3 mm aluminium sheet of the same area while being far stiffer in bending.

For construction, the attraction is the combination of properties rather than any single one. The material is metallic all the way through, so it does not burn, rot, absorb water, shed fibres or support mould. It can be cut, drilled and fixed with ordinary metalworking tools. It reflects light in a way no other cladding does, and it can be fully recycled with other aluminium scrap at the end of the building's life.

Those advantages come at a higher material cost than mineral wool, gypsum or aluminium composite panels, so foam is normally chosen where it does more than one job at once: a facade that is also an acoustic absorber, a ceiling that must be non-combustible and washable, or a feature wall that must survive impact in a public space.

Where aluminium foam earns its place

  • Non-combustible linings in escape routes, tunnels, stations and plant rooms
  • Acoustic panels in wet, outdoor or hygiene-sensitive spaces where fibrous absorbers fail
  • Lightweight decorative facades and soffits with a metallic, textured face
  • Stiff, light cores for sandwich panels, doors and modular partitions
Foam types

Open-cell and closed-cell aluminium foam

Every aluminium foam belongs to one of two families. The difference is whether the pores connect to each other, and it decides almost everything about how the material behaves in a building.

PropertyClosed-cell foamOpen-cell foam
Pore structureSealed cells separated by thin aluminium wallsInterconnected pores, a continuous metal network
Typical densityabout 0.25 – 0.85 g/cm³about 0.2 – 0.6 g/cm³
Typical pore sizeabout 1 – 10 mm (often 3 – 8 mm)about 0.5 – 5 mm, set by the process
Stiffness and strengthHigher for the same densityLower; better protected behind a face
Sound absorptionLow as cast; good once cells are opened by rolling, drilling or slicingGood without treatment
Air and water permeabilityNone; does not take up waterAllows air and liquid to flow through
Main building usesFacades, cladding, sandwich cores, partitions, impact liningsAcoustic linings, ventilation grilles, heat exchange, filtration

Closed-cell foam is usually made by the melt route: molten aluminium is thickened with fine ceramic or calcium particles so that bubbles stay stable, then foamed with a gas-releasing agent or with injected gas, solidified into large blocks and sliced into slabs. Open-cell foam is normally made by casting or infiltrating aluminium around a removable space-holder (for example salt granules) that is later dissolved out, which lets the maker control pore size closely.

Pore size

How pore size changes performance

Large pores (over about 3 mm)

A bold, open texture that reads well on facades from a distance. Good structural stiffness for the weight and, in open-cell form, high air permeability for vents and grilles. Weaker as an absorber at speech frequencies unless backed by an air gap.

Medium pores (about 1 – 3 mm)

The general-purpose range. Balanced appearance, workable stiffness and useful absorption once cells are opened. Common for wall linings, ceilings and sandwich-panel cores.

Small pores (under about 1 mm)

Fine, uniform surface for interior feature panels and furniture. In open-cell form the narrow pores give strong viscous damping of sound, at the price of lower permeability and higher cost.

Pore size is never perfectly uniform in a melt-route foam: a slab described as "5 mm" will contain smaller and larger cells. For visible applications, approve a physical sample from the actual production lot and agree how much variation in appearance is acceptable across a run of panels.

Performance data

Mechanical and physical properties

Mechanical behaviour of a metal foam depends mainly on its relative density (foam density divided by solid aluminium density). Strength rises roughly with the 1.5th power of relative density, so a small increase in density gives a large increase in strength. Physical behaviour

Water absorption
Negligible for closed-cell foam; the metal does not take up moisture
Reaction to fire
The aluminium foam itself is non-combustible; panels with adhesives, films or coatings must be classified as a complete build (EN 13501-1, ASTM E84)
Thermal conductivity
Much lower than solid aluminium but still a few W/m·K, far above insulation materials; foam is not a substitute for thermal insulation
Thermal expansion
Close to that of the parent aluminium alloy (about 21 – 24 µm/m·°C); allow for movement at joints on long facades
Electromagnetic shielding
A continuous conductive metal network; closed-cell slabs give useful EMI attenuation that rises with thickness and density
Durability
Forms a protective oxide like other aluminium; coastal and industrial sites may call for a coated or anodised finish
Applications

Where aluminium foam is used in construction

Facades and rainscreens

Foam slabs or foam-core panels as a ventilated rainscreen layer on aluminium sub-frames. The textured face creates depth and changes with light; the material does not add fuel to a facade fire.

Ceilings and soffits

Lay-in or suspended panels where light weight reduces grid loads and where washability or moisture resistance matters, such as pools, kitchens, car parks and transport hubs.

Interior partitions

Foam cores bonded between aluminium or steel faces give stiff, light, dry-assembled partitions. Combined with dense layers they can meet sound-insulation targets.

Acoustic linings

Opened closed-cell or open-cell foam over an air gap absorbs reverberant sound in halls, atriums, corridors and plant rooms. See the acoustic panel guide for test data.

Road and rail noise barriers

Outdoor barriers that resist rain, UV and vehicle spray without losing absorption. Covered in detail on the foam noise barrier page.

Impact and blast linings

Thick closed-cell slabs crush progressively at nearly constant stress, absorbing energy from impacts. Used in protective linings and as fillers in security doors.

Ventilation and screening

Open-cell or perforated foam as decorative vents, louvre infills and light-diffusing screens that let air through.

Roofs and terraces

Light, non-combustible decorative elements and protection layers; foam is not waterproof by itself, so it sits above a separate membrane.

Lighting and feature walls

Thin slices back-lit to create a translucent "froth" effect, or front-lit to show texture in lobbies, retail and hospitality interiors.

Some suppliers describe foam panels that "withstand 1,000 °C". Aluminium melts at about 660 °C, so claims of this kind refer to a protected or composite build tested in a specific way, not to the foam itself. Ask for the exact test standard, the specimen build and the classification report before relying on a fire claim.

Design

Design and installation considerations

  • Fixing: foam does not hold self-tapping screws well near edges. Use through-bolts with large washers, bonded inserts, edge channels or clip systems on extruded aluminium sub-frames.
  • Movement: leave joints for thermal expansion on long exterior runs, and avoid locking panels rigidly at both ends.
  • Galvanic contact: isolate foam from copper, carbon steel and treated timber in wet locations, as with any aluminium product.
  • Edges: sliced edges expose open cells; cover them with trims or fill them where dirt traps or sharp cell walls would be a problem.
  • Water management: foam is not a waterproof layer. Rainscreen facades still need a cavity, a membrane and drainage behind the panels.
  • Fire classification: classify the complete panel and fixing system, not just the foam, to the code that applies (EN 13501-1, NFPA 285, local rules).
  • Appearance: agree a reference sample and a visual acceptance range for cell size and colour before production.

If the brief is simply a flat, coloured, lightweight facade, an aluminium composite or honeycomb panel is usually cheaper. Aluminium foam is the stronger choice when the panel must also absorb sound, survive impact, stay non-combustible throughout or show a cellular metal face.

Ordering

How to specify and order aluminium foam

  1. Define the functionDecorative, acoustic, structural core, impact absorption or a combination; state the target values (NRC, Rw, fire class, impact energy).
  2. Choose type and densityClosed or open cell, density band and nominal pore size.
  3. Set sizes and finishPanel length, width and thickness (slabs are commonly up to about 2.4 m long), finish and edge treatment.
  4. Agree testingCompression test to ISO 13314, fire classification for the complete build, acoustic tests to ISO 354 or ASTM C423 where relevant.
  5. Approve samplesReference sample from the production lot for appearance and density.
  6. ShipPanels are interleaved, edge-protected and packed in crates for export; delivery terms EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) foam for construction: FAQs

Is aluminium foam fireproof?

The foam is metal and does not burn, so it adds no fuel to a fire. It still melts at about 660 °C, and any adhesive, coating or composite layer affects the result, so classify the complete panel to EN 13501-1 or the local code.

Is aluminium foam a good thermal insulator?

Not by itself. Its conductivity is far lower than solid aluminium but still around a few W/m·K, much higher than mineral wool or PIR boards. Use a separate insulation layer behind foam cladding.

Can aluminium foam be used outdoors?

Yes. Closed-cell foam does not absorb water and resists weathering like other aluminium. On coastal or polluted sites, an anodised or coated face extends its life and keeps the appearance even.

How heavy is an aluminium foam panel?

Weight equals density times volume. A 1 m² panel 20 mm thick at 0.4 g/cm³ weighs about 8 kg; at 0.7 g/cm³ it weighs about 14 kg.

What is the difference between open-cell and closed-cell aluminium foam?

Closed-cell foam has sealed pores and is stiffer and waterproof; it suits cladding, cores and impact linings. Open-cell foam has connected pores, lets air through and absorbs sound without treatment.

Can aluminium foam be cut and drilled on site?

Yes, with fine-tooth saws, band saws, waterjet or CNC routers. Use through-fixings or inserts rather than relying on screws biting into the foam.

Planning aluminium foam for a building?

Tell us the application, panel sizes, finish and quantity. We will recommend a foam type and supply the panels with matching aluminium profiles and sheet.
