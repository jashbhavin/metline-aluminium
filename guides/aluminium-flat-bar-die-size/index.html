Extrusion sizing guide

Aluminium Flat Bar Die Sizes & Size Chart

Extruded aluminium flat bar is made by pushing a heated billet through a flat die with a rectangular opening, so every thickness and width combination needs its own die. Ordering a size for which dies already exist across the industry avoids a tooling charge and a die-making step, which is why designers check a standard size chart before fixing a dimension. This guide lists common metric and inch flat bar sizes with their weights, explains how die size, press capacity and alloy limit what can be extruded, and shows how to specify tolerances and finishes.

Overview

What a flat bar die is and why size choice matters

A flat bar die is a hardened tool-steel disc, usually H13 hot-work steel, with a precisely machined rectangular aperture. The billet, heated to roughly 450 – 500 °C, is forced through it by the press ram and emerges as a continuous bar with the cross-section of the opening. Because the opening defines both thickness and width, a 6 × 40 mm bar and a 6 × 45 mm bar need two different dies.

Flat bar dies are "solid" dies: they have no mandrel or bridge, unlike the porthole dies used for hollow tube. That makes them among the simplest and cheapest extrusion tools. Even so, a new die has to be designed, machined, heat-treated, nitrided and trial-run before production metal is accepted, and the first trial frequently needs correction of the bearing lengths to get the bar flat and straight. That process adds cost and calendar time to a first order.

Extruders therefore keep libraries of existing flat bar dies in the sizes the market asks for most. When a designer picks a thickness and width that is already in a library, the order can go straight to production. When the size is new, the buyer typically pays a one-time die charge, and the die then becomes reusable for repeat orders. Small differences, such as 38 mm instead of 40 mm, can decide whether a size runs from an existing tool or needs a new one.

Practical rules before you fix a flat bar size

  • Start from a standard metric or inch size in the chart below, then adjust the design to it if you can
  • If a non-standard width is essential, ask whether it can be machined or sawn from the next wider standard bar more cheaply than a new die
  • Keep the width-to-thickness ratio moderate; very thin, very wide flats are harder to extrude flat and straight
  • State the alloy and temper together with the size: a die that runs 6063 easily may not suit 7075
  • For large volumes, a dedicated die is often worth it, because it removes machining from every part
Process

How flat bar is extruded, straightened and aged

  1. Billet preparationHomogenised logs of the chosen alloy are cut to billet length and heated in an induction or gas furnace. 6063 and 6060 run at the lower end of the temperature range; 6061, 6082 and the hard alloys need tighter temperature control.
  2. Die heating and loadingThe flat die is pre-heated so that it does not chill the metal, then locked into the die stack in front of the container.
  3. ExtrusionThe ram pushes the billet through the die. Exit speed depends on alloy and section: soft 6063 flats can run quickly, while 2024 and 7075 are extruded far more slowly to avoid surface tearing.
  4. QuenchingHeat-treatable alloys are quenched at the press exit by air or water spray so that they can later reach full T5 or T6 strength. Thick flats in 6061 and 6082 need water quenching to reach T6 properties through the section.
  5. StretchingEach length is gripped at both ends and stretched by about 1 – 3 %. This straightens the bar, removes twist and relieves residual stress, which is what the "51" in T6511 or T651 indicates.
  6. Cutting and ageingBars are sawn to length and artificially aged in a furnace to reach T5 or T6. Samples from the batch are tested for hardness or tensile properties before release.
  7. Finishing and packingMill-finish bars are interleaved and bundled; anodised or powder-coated bars are finished after ageing and then wrapped to protect the surface.

Cold-drawn flat bar is a separate product: an extruded bar is drawn through a carbide die at room temperature to tighten tolerances and improve surface finish. Cold-drawn bar is covered by EN 754-5 in Europe and is normally limited to smaller sections; most structural and general-purpose flats are supplied as-extruded.

Weight calculation

How to calculate flat bar weight

Flat bar weight is simple geometry. Multiply the cross-sectional area by the density and the length. Corner radii reduce weight slightly, but on most flats the effect is well under 1 % and is ignored for ordering purposes.

Weight formulas

Metric
kg/m = thickness (mm) × width (mm) × 0.0027
Imperial
lb/ft = thickness (in) × width (in) × 1.17
Worked example, metric
10 × 50 mm: 10 × 50 × 0.0027 = 1.35 kg/m; a 6 m length weighs 8.1 kg
Worked example, inch
1/2 × 2 in: 0.5 × 2 × 1.17 = 1.17 lb/ft; a 12 ft length weighs about 14 lb
Alloy correction
2024 ≈ 2.78 g/cm³; 7075 ≈ 2.81 g/cm³; 5083 ≈ 2.66 g/cm³

For any other size, use the aluminium bar weight calculator. When estimating freight, add the weight of packing: wooden cases or pallets and interleaving typically add a noticeable margin to the net metal weight.

What can be extruded

Size limits: circle size, press capacity and width-to-thickness ratio

Three things decide whether a flat bar size can be extruded and how easily: the circumscribing circle diameter (CCD), the press force and the shape factor.

Circumscribing circle

The smallest circle that encloses the cross-section. For a flat bar it is the diagonal of the rectangle. A 10 × 200 mm flat needs a CCD of just over 200 mm, so it must run on a press whose container and die stack accept that size. Very wide flats move to larger presses, which cost more per kilogram.

Extrusion ratio

The ratio of the container cross-section to the bar cross-section. If the ratio is too low, the metal is not worked enough and properties can suffer; if it is too high, pressure rises and speed falls. Extruders choose the press and the number of die openings to keep the ratio in a workable band.

Width-to-thickness ratio

Thin, wide flats (for example 3 × 150 mm) tend to bow and wave because metal flows faster in the centre than at the edges. Die designers correct this with bearing lengths, but tolerances on flatness are wider for these sections.

Multi-hole dies

Small flats are often extruded through dies with two, four or more openings at once. This raises productivity and is one reason small standard sizes are economical.

Alloy group

Soft alloys (1xxx, 3xxx, 6060, 6063) extrude easily into thin, sharp-cornered sections. Hard alloys (2024, 5083, 7075) need more pressure, slower speeds and more generous minimum thicknesses.

Corner radius

An as-extruded flat has slightly rounded corners from the die. Sharp corners can be specified but may need a dedicated die or machining; full-round edges also need a specific die.

As a rule, flats up to around 150 mm wide are easy to source in all common alloys. Between about 150 mm and 300 mm, choice narrows and larger presses are needed. Beyond that, buyers often compare a wide extruded flat with a strip sawn from 6061 plate, which may be faster and offers wider thickness choice.

Typical tensile strengths give a feel for the range: 6063-T6 is about 240 MPa, 6061-T6 about 310 MPa, 2024-T3 about 480 MPa and 7075-T6 about 570 MPa (Aluminum Association typical values). Specified minimum values for extruded bar are lower and vary with thickness; they are set by ASTM B221 and EN 755-2 and are reported on the mill certificate.

T5 means cooled from the extrusion temperature and artificially aged; T6 means solution heat-treated (or press-quenched to an equivalent condition) and artificially aged. T6511 adds controlled stretching and minor straightening, which makes the bar more stable when machined.

Tolerances

Tolerance standards for extruded flat bar

Flat bar tolerances are not a single number: they depend on the width band, the thickness band and the alloy group. Harder alloys such as 2024 and 7075 (Group II in the EN system) have wider tolerances than 6060, 6063 and 6082 (Group I), because they are harder to control at the press.

The standards control thickness, width, corner radius, flatness (concavity and convexity across the width), straightness along the length, twist and squareness of the cut ends. Where a drawing needs tighter values than the standard gives, state them explicitly on the order: for example a ground or milled thickness, a precision-sawn length, or a straightness limit per metre. Tighter limits are achievable by machining or by selecting bars, at extra cost.

Two practical points avoid disputes. First, measure thickness away from the corners, because the corner radius is not part of the flat dimension. Second, check straightness and twist with the bar lying under its own weight on a flat table, which is how the standards define them.

Surface finishes

Surface finishes for flat bar

Mill finish

As extruded. Light die lines are normal. The usual choice for bars that will be machined, welded or painted later.

Anodised

Clear, black, bronze, champagne and other colours. 6063 and 6060 give the most uniform result; 6061 and 6082 anodise well but with a slightly greyer tone. Architectural coatings are typically 15 – 25 µm, hard anodising thicker.

Powder coated

Any RAL colour and gloss, applied after ageing. Architectural work is often specified to Qualicoat or AAMA 2603/2604/2605 classes.

Brushed or polished

Mechanical finishing for visible trim; brushed finishes are usually followed by anodising to protect the surface.

Electrophoretic coating

A thin, even lacquer film over anodising, used for metallic-look architectural sections.

Colour matching

For visible trim, order all bars for one project in a single anodising or coating batch so colour and gloss match.

Cutting & fabrication

Cutting, bending, punching and machining flat bar

  • Cut to length: standard lengths are often 3 m, 4 m or 6 m (or 12 ft and 20 ft); precision sawing to a tighter length tolerance is available on request.
  • Bending: easiest in 6063-T5 and in T4 tempers; 6061-T6 and 6082-T6 need larger radii. Bending the flat the "easy way" (about the thin axis) is far simpler than edgewise bending.
  • Punching and slotting: holes and slots should keep at least about one thickness, and preferably more, from the edge to avoid bulging or tearing.
  • Welding: MIG or TIG with ER4043 or ER5356 filler for 6xxx flats; heat-treated tempers lose strength in the heat-affected zone. 2024 and 7075 are not fusion-welded in structural work.
  • Machining: allow a machining margin on sawn faces if a finished dimension matters; T6511 bar moves less when one face is machined.
  • Anodising after fabrication: anodise after bending, drilling and welding where possible; weld zones in 6xxx may anodise to a different shade.

FAQ

Aluminium flat bar die sizes: frequently asked questions

Do I have to pay for a die to buy aluminium flat bar?

Not if the thickness and width match a die the extruder already holds. Standard sizes run from existing tools. A new size normally carries a one-time die charge, after which repeat orders use the same die.

What is the largest aluminium flat bar that can be extruded?

It depends on the press. Most extruders handle widths up to about 150 – 250 mm comfortably; wider flats need large presses with big containers. For very wide or thick sections, a strip sawn from plate is often a practical alternative.

Which standard covers tolerances for extruded aluminum flat bar?

In Europe, EN 755-5 covers extruded rectangular bar tolerances, with properties in EN 755-2. In North America, ASTM B221 applies with the dimensional tolerances of ANSI H35.2. Cold-drawn bar falls under EN 754-5 or ASTM B211.

How do I calculate the weight of an aluminium flat bar?

Multiply thickness by width in millimetres and by 0.0027 to get kilograms per metre. A 6 × 50 mm bar weighs 0.81 kg/m. In inches, thickness × width × 1.17 gives pounds per foot.

Why does my anodised flat bar look different from batch to batch?

Anodised colour depends on alloy chemistry, temper and the anodising bath. Order visible parts in one batch, from one alloy, and finished in one anodising run to keep the colour consistent.

Is 6061 or 6063 better for flat bar?

6063 gives a better anodised finish and sharper detail and is preferred for trim and architectural use. 6061 is about 30 % stronger and machines better, so it suits brackets, fixtures and structural parts.
