High-End Aluminium Forgings: Alloys, Processes and Quality

A high-end aluminium forging is a part shaped by plastic deformation from a certified heat-treatable alloy, with controlled grain flow, a specified temper and full inspection records. This guide explains which alloys, forging routes, tempers and tests separate aerospace, defence and energy-grade forgings from commodity parts, and what to put on your enquiry so the supplier quotes the right product.

Overview

What makes an aluminium forging "high-end"?

Every forging starts as a cast ingot or extruded billet that is heated and squeezed, hammered or rolled into shape. What lifts a forging into the high-end category is not the press but the control around it: a registered alloy with tight chemistry, a forging plan that puts the grain flow where the loads are, a defined heat treatment, and inspection that proves the inside of the part is sound.

Forging closes the porosity left from casting, breaks up coarse intermetallic particles and stretches the grains into a fibrous structure that follows the outline of the part. When that grain flow runs along the main stress paths, fatigue life, fracture toughness and resistance to stress corrosion are noticeably better than in the same shape machined from plate or bar, where the grain runs in one direction regardless of geometry.

That is why landing-gear components, wing and fuselage fittings, rocket rings and bulkheads, missile cases, high-pressure flanges, forged wheels and suspension arms are forged rather than cast or hogged from a block. The trade-off is cost and lead time: dies, forging passes and heat treatment cycles all add up, so forging pays off where failure is expensive or weight reduction is valuable.

Four features of a high-end forging

  • Certified alloy and temper to a named standard (ASTM B247, SAE AMS, EN 586) rather than a generic "6061"
  • Grain flow designed around the part, confirmed by macroetch on a sacrificial or first-article piece
  • Volumetric ultrasonic inspection to a stated class, plus hardness and conductivity checks
  • Full traceability: heat number, forging lot and heat-treatment batch on an EN 10204 3.1 certificate

Alloy selection

Forging alloys, tempers and typical properties

Forging alloys come from four families. Copper-bearing 2xxx alloys keep strength at elevated temperature; 5xxx alloys are non-heat-treatable and chosen for corrosion and weldability; 6xxx alloys are the general-engineering workhorses; 7xxx alloys give the highest strength. The values below are typical room-temperature properties from published Aluminum Association and ASM data, not specified minimums. Design to the minimums in the governing specification, which fall as section thickness grows and differ between longitudinal and transverse directions.

Two further alloys deserve mention. 7050 in T74 and T7452 tempers is the standard choice for thick aerospace forgings, because its lower quench sensitivity keeps strength in the centre of heavy sections while the over-aged temper resists stress-corrosion cracking. 6082 is the European counterpart of 6061 for forged automotive suspension parts and structural fittings. For both, use the specification minimums and the mill certificate rather than generic figures, because properties depend strongly on section size.

Processes

Open-die, closed-die, ring rolling and precision forging

Open-die (free) forging

The workpiece is shaped between flat or simple dies by successive blows or press strokes. No part-specific tooling, so it suits prototypes, one-off large parts and blocks, bars and discs. Grain refinement comes from upsetting and drawing in several directions. Shapes are simple and the part needs generous machining stock.

Closed-die (impression) forging

Heated billet is pressed into matched dies; excess metal escapes as flash and is trimmed. Dies cost money but give near-shape parts, consistent grain flow and less machining, so the cost per part falls quickly with volume.

Ring rolling

A pierced preform is rolled between a driven roll and an idler mandrel while axial rolls control height. The grain runs around the circumference, which is exactly where hoop stresses act in rings, flanges and bearing races.

Back extrusion and piercing

A punch drives into a contained billet so metal flows backwards around it, forming a cup or tube with a sound, worked wall. Used for large hollow parts and thick-wall cylinders.

Precision (near-net) forging

Tighter die design, controlled die temperature and close-tolerance billets produce thin webs and small draft angles, cutting machining time on high-volume parts such as wheels and suspension arms.

Cold and warm forging

Lower-temperature forging of softer alloys (1xxx, 3xxx, 6xxx) for small parts with excellent surface and tolerance, such as electrical connectors and fasteners. See our cold forging guide.

Tempers

Heat treatment and tempers for forgings

Heat-treatable forgings are solution treated, quenched and aged. The quench locks in residual stress, which matters because high-end forgings are usually machined heavily afterwards. Forging-specific tempers deal with this:

  • T6: solution treated and artificially aged to peak strength. Standard for 6061, 2014 and 7075 die forgings.
  • T652: T6 plus a controlled 1 – 5 % cold compression after quenching to relieve residual stress. The forging equivalent of stretched plate; ask for it when the part will be machined deeply.
  • T73, T7352, T74, T7452: over-aged tempers for 7xxx alloys that trade some strength for much better resistance to stress-corrosion cracking and exfoliation. Thick aerospace forgings are normally specified in one of these.
  • T61: the usual temper of 2618 for elevated-temperature service.
  • T4 and T352: naturally aged tempers, common for 2024 where toughness and fatigue matter more than peak yield strength.
  • O, H111, H112: annealed or as-fabricated conditions for non-heat-treatable 5xxx forgings.

Ask for heat treatment to AMS 2770 or AMS 2772, with furnace uniformity surveys and quench-delay control, when the part is for aerospace or defence. For 7xxx forgings, electrical conductivity measured by eddy current is used alongside hardness to confirm that the over-ageing step actually happened.

Compared with other metals, aluminium forgings weigh roughly a third of an equivalent steel forging (2.70 versus 7.85 g/cm³) and about 60 % of a titanium-alloy forging, while giving good corrosion resistance and easy machining. Steel and titanium win where strength at high temperature or very high absolute load is the priority; magnesium is lighter still but less resistant to corrosion. Metline supplies the aluminium forgings in such comparisons, not the steel or titanium alternatives.

Applications

Where high-end aluminium forgings are used

Aerospace and space

Wing and fuselage fittings, landing-gear parts, engine mounts, rotorcraft transmission housings and swash plates, launcher separation rings, adapter rings, domes and bulkheads, mostly in 2014, 2219, 7050 and 7075.

Defence

Armoured-vehicle wheels and track parts, missile cases and interstage rings, weapon-mount brackets, often in 7xxx and 5083 for weldable protection.

Energy and process plant

Cryogenic and LNG flanges in 5083 and 6061, gas-storage cylinder shells, large rings and forged tubes for power equipment where conductivity and corrosion resistance matter.

Automotive and motorsport

Forged wheels, control arms, steering knuckles, brake calipers, pistons in 4032 and 2618, mostly closed-die forged 6061 and 6082.

Bicycles and motorcycles

Cranks, triple clamps, stems, hubs and suspension parts where weight and fatigue life drive design.

Industrial hardware

Hinges, pulleys, couplings, gear blanks and hydraulic manifolds that need forged toughness without the weight of steel.

Ordering

How to specify and order a high-end forging

  1. Define the partSend the finished-part drawing or the forging envelope, with material, temper and the governing specification (for example ASTM B247 6061-T6, AMS 4133, EN 586).
  2. Agree the routeWe confirm open-die, ring-rolled or closed-die forging, machining allowance and stress-relieved temper (T652, T7352) where machining is heavy.
  3. Set inspectionSpecify test directions, ultrasonic class, grain-flow requirements and any first-article inspection or third-party witness.
  4. ProcessForging, heat treatment, testing and optional rough or finish machining, shot peening or anodising to your drawing.
  5. Document and shipEN 10204 3.1 certificate, inspection reports and export packing; delivery on EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP terms worldwide.

FAQ

High-end aluminium (aluminum) forgings: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium alloy is strongest for forgings?

7075-T6 has the highest typical strength of the common forging alloys, around 570 MPa tensile. For thick aerospace parts, 7050-T74 or T7452 is usually preferred because it keeps strength through heavy sections and resists stress-corrosion cracking much better than 7075-T6.

Are forged aluminium parts stronger than machined parts?

For the same alloy and temper the static strength is similar, but a forging has grain flow that follows the shape, closed porosity and better short-transverse properties. That gives longer fatigue life and higher toughness, which is why safety-critical parts are forged.

What is the difference between T6 and T652 for forgings?

Both reach peak-aged strength. T652 adds a small cold compression after quenching that relieves residual stress, so the part moves much less during heavy machining. Choose T652 (or T7352 for 7xxx) for parts with deep pockets or thin walls.

When should I choose open-die over closed-die forging?

Open-die suits simple shapes, large sizes and small quantities because it needs no part-specific dies. Closed-die forging costs more to set up but produces near-shape parts with consistent grain flow and less machining, so it wins at medium to high volumes.

What inspection is normal for aerospace aluminium forgings?

Chemical analysis, tensile tests in the specified directions, ultrasonic inspection to AMS-STD-2154 at the agreed class, hardness and conductivity for heat-treated tempers, grain-flow macroetch on first articles and a dimensional report, all recorded on an EN 10204 3.1 certificate.

Can Metline machine forgings to a finished drawing?

Yes. Forgings can be supplied as forged, rough machined or machined to drawing, with optional shot peening, sand blasting or anodising, and shipped worldwide with full certification.

Planning a critical forged part?

Send the drawing, alloy, temper and inspection class. Our team will recommend the forging route and reply with certification details and a delivered quote.
