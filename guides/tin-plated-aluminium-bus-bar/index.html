Tin-Plated Aluminium Bus Bar

Tin-plated aluminium bus bar is an aluminium conductor, usually 1350, 1060 or 6101, with a thin electroplated tin layer that keeps bolted and clamped joints stable. The tin replaces aluminium's hard, insulating oxide film at the contact face with a soft, conductive surface, reducing contact-resistance drift and making joints to copper terminals far more reliable. This guide explains how the plating is applied, how thick it should be, which alloys to choose and how to specify a tinned aluminium busbar that performs for decades.

The problem tin solves

Why aluminium bus bars are tin plated

Aluminium conducts about 61 % as well as copper by volume but weighs only about 30 % as much, so for the same current an aluminium bar is roughly half the weight of a copper one. Its weakness is the joint. Freshly cut aluminium forms an oxide film within seconds; the film is thin but hard and electrically insulating, and it regrows wherever it is scratched away.

In a bolted joint, current flows only through the small spots where the two surfaces actually touch. On bare aluminium those spots must break through the oxide. Over years of heating and cooling, aluminium also creeps slightly under bolt pressure and expands more than steel bolts, so contact pressure falls, the oxide regrows and resistance rises. Higher resistance means more heat, which accelerates the process. This is why poorly made bare aluminium joints develop hot spots.

Tin breaks this cycle. It is soft, so it deforms under bolt load and makes a large true contact area. Its own oxide is thin and easily broken. And because tin sits between aluminium and copper in the galvanic series, a tinned aluminium bar bolted to a tinned copper terminal has a much smaller galvanic difference at the interface than bare aluminium on bare copper, which reduces corrosion of the joint in humid air.

What tin plating does for an aluminium busbar

  • Gives a stable, low contact resistance at bolted, clamped and terminal joints
  • Protects the contact face from oxidation and from humid or mildly industrial atmospheres
  • Reduces galvanic attack when the bar is joined to copper or tinned copper parts
  • Provides a solderable surface where soldered connections are needed
  • Keeps the weight and cost advantage of aluminium over copper
Conductor alloys

Which aluminium alloy is used under the tin?

The plating only affects the surface; current flows through the aluminium. Choose the alloy for conductivity and mechanical needs, then specify the tin. Conductivity values below are typical; minimums are set by the conductor standard and reported on the certificate.

For most switchgear, UPS and battery applications, 6101 is the practical choice because the higher strength keeps bolted joints tight and resists bending under short-circuit forces. 1350 is used where every percent of conductivity counts, such as long straight runs and smelter or rectifier bus, and where the bar is supported along its length.

Copper is about 100 % IACS. To carry the same current at the same temperature rise, an aluminium bar needs roughly 1.6 times the cross-section of a copper bar, yet still weighs about half as much.

Process

How tin is plated onto aluminium

Tin cannot be plated directly onto aluminium with ordinary methods, because the oxide film reforms instantly and the deposit would peel. Aluminium is prepared with an immersion zinc (zincate) or stannate treatment, then usually given a copper or nickel strike before tin. ASTM B253 describes these preparation routes.

  1. Machining firstCutting, punching, drilling, bending and deburring are done before plating, so hole walls, edges and cut ends are fully covered.
  2. Cleaning and etchingNon-etching alkaline cleaning removes oils, then a light etch and desmut remove the existing oxide and alloy smut.
  3. Zincate immersionThe bar is dipped in a zincate solution that replaces the oxide with a thin zinc film. A double zincate (strip and repeat) gives a finer, more adherent layer and is standard for high-reliability parts.
  4. Copper or nickel strikeA thin strike layer of copper or nickel is deposited over the zinc. It protects the zinc from the tin bath and acts as a diffusion barrier between the aluminium substrate and the tin.
  5. Tin electroplatingTin is electroplated from an acid sulphate or alkaline stannate bath to the specified thickness, as matte or bright tin.
  6. Rinse, dry and inspectThe bars are rinsed and dried quickly to prevent staining, then checked for coverage, adhesion and thickness, usually by X-ray fluorescence (XRF).

Specifying the coating

How thick should the tin be?

Thickness is a balance between protection and cost. Very thin tin (under about 3 µm) can be porous and is consumed by diffusion over time; very thick tin adds cost and gives little extra benefit at a bolted joint. ASTM B545 groups electrodeposited tin into service classes by minimum thickness, from thin solderable coatings up to heavy deposits for severe service, and the choice is usually made by the environment.

Service conditionTypical tin thicknessNotes
Dry indoor switchgear and panels5 – 8 µmCommon default for bolted joints
Humid indoor, data centres, battery rooms8 – 12 µmMore margin against pores and wear from repeated assembly
Outdoor enclosures, coastal or industrial air12 – 20 µmCombine with joint compound and good sealing
Solderability only2 – 5 µmNot intended for long-term bolted contact

State whether the thickness is a minimum at the contact areas or an average over the part, and where it is measured. On a long bar the contact pads matter most; edge build-up during plating means corners will always be thicker than the centre of a flat face. Ask for an XRF thickness report against agreed measurement points.

Tin whiskers and how to manage them

  • Pure tin can grow fine conductive filaments (whiskers) under compressive stress over months or years
  • Matte tin with low carbon content, a nickel underlayer and adequate thickness all reduce the risk
  • For fine-pitch electronics, consider a reflowed tin finish or a different coating; for heavy power busbars with wide spacings the risk is usually low

Installation

Design and installation tips for tinned aluminium joints

Keep contact pressure

Use plated steel bolts with Belleville (conical spring) washers and wide flat washers. The spring washer maintains clamping force as aluminium creeps and as the joint heats and cools.

Torque correctly

Follow the torque recommended for the bolt size and joint design; overtightening can extrude soft tin and aluminium and lose preload over time.

Clean but do not abrade the tin

Wipe contact faces clean. Do not wire-brush tinned surfaces as you would bare aluminium; that removes the coating you paid for.

Joint compound where needed

In humid or outdoor service, a suitable oxide-inhibiting compound keeps moisture out of the joint.

Copper connections

Bolt to tinned copper terminals, or use aluminium-copper bimetallic transition plates or lugs. Avoid bare copper on bare aluminium in damp conditions.

Watch for fretting

Under vibration, tin-to-tin contacts can suffer fretting wear. Secure bars against movement and keep joints tight.

Tin melts at 232 °C, well above normal busbar operating temperatures, but it softens and diffuses faster at high temperature. Keep joints within the temperature-rise limits of your switchgear standard (for example IEC 61439-1 for low-voltage assemblies) and treat discoloured or hot joints as a maintenance warning.

Tin-plated aluminium is the middle path many panel builders now take: the bar is aluminium for weight and cost, while the joint behaves much more like a plated copper joint. For applications needing a copper surface over the whole bar, see copper-clad aluminium bus bar, which bonds a copper layer metallurgically to an aluminium core.

Applications

Where tin-plated aluminium busbars are used

  • Low-voltage switchgear and panel boards: main and distribution bars, where bolted joints must stay stable for decades
  • UPS systems and data centres: power distribution where temperature rise and energy loss must be kept low
  • Energy storage and battery packs: many connection points and frequent thermal cycling; tin stabilises contact resistance at module and rack connections
  • EV charging and power electronics: inverter, converter and charger bus, sometimes as laminated assemblies
  • Bus duct (busway): plated contact areas at joints and tap-off points
  • Earthing and neutral bars: tinned bars resist corrosion and accept copper earth conductors through suitable lugs
  • Rectifiers and electrochemical plants: plated contact faces on heavy aluminium bus

A typical example: a panel builder replaces 10 × 100 mm copper main bars with 6101-T6 aluminium bars of about 1.6 times the cross-section, tin-plated over the joint areas. The bars are larger in section but roughly half the weight for the same rating, and the tinned contact faces bolt directly to tinned copper breaker terminals with spring washers.

FAQ

Tin-plated aluminium (aluminum) bus bar: FAQ

Why are aluminium busbars tin plated?

To make joints reliable. Bare aluminium forms a hard insulating oxide and creeps under bolt load, so contact resistance can rise over time. Tin gives a soft, conductive contact surface that resists oxidation and reduces galvanic attack when the bar is joined to copper.

Does tin plating reduce the conductivity of the busbar?

No meaningful change. The current flows through the aluminium; a 5 – 12 µm tin layer only affects the contact surface, where it lowers and stabilises contact resistance.

Can tin be plated directly onto aluminium?

Not reliably. The aluminium is first given a zincate (or stannate) immersion treatment and normally a copper or nickel strike, then tin is electroplated. Skipping these steps leads to blistering and peeling.

Should holes be drilled before or after tin plating?

Before. Machining first means hole walls, edges and cut ends are covered by tin. Drilling after plating leaves bare aluminium exactly where the bolt joint is made.

Is tin-plated aluminum bus bar as good as copper?

Joint performance is close to plated copper when correct hardware is used, and the bar is about half the weight for the same current. It needs about 1.6 times the cross-section, so copper remains preferable where space is very tight.

Can I bolt a tinned aluminium bar to a copper terminal?

Yes. Preferably bolt to a tinned copper terminal, use spring washers and, in damp service, an oxide-inhibiting compound. For bare copper in harsh environments, use an aluminium-copper bimetallic transition piece.
