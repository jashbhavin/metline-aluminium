Bonding Ribbon for Semiconductor Packaging: Choosing the Material

Bonding ribbon is a flat metal strip, wedge-bonded between a chip and its substrate, leads or terminals. Aluminium ribbon dominates power modules and battery interconnects, gold ribbon is the standard for RF and microwave circuits, and copper and silver ribbon are used where higher conductivity or thermal performance is needed. This guide compares the four materials, explains when ribbon beats round wire and lists what to specify when ordering. Metline supplies the aluminium ribbon; the other metals are covered for comparison.

Overview

What is ribbon bonding?

Ribbon bonding is a form of wedge bonding in which a rectangular strip, rather than a round wire, is pressed onto a bond pad by a grooved tool and welded in the solid state by ultrasonic energy, sometimes with heat. The bonder forms the first bond, loops the ribbon to the second pad, bonds it and cuts it. No solder or adhesive is involved.

A flat conductor has two useful properties. For the same cross-section it has much more surface area than a round wire, which lowers inductance and resistance at high frequencies, where current flows near the surface. And it lies low over the chip, so loops are short and stable. Wedge bonds can also be compact: published comparisons suggest the bond footprint can be only slightly larger than the ribbon width, which helps pad layout.

The material choice is driven by the application. Power modules switch tens to hundreds of amps and need low DC resistance and long life under thermal cycling; RF and microwave circuits need low inductance and stable, corrosion-free contacts at gigahertz frequencies; battery packs need fast, reliable connections between cells and busbars. No single metal is best for all three.

Ribbon at a glance

  • Aluminium: power modules and battery interconnects; room-temperature ultrasonic bonding
  • Gold: RF, microwave and hybrid circuits; corrosion-free and easy to bond thermosonically
  • Copper: higher conductivity than gold or aluminium at lower cost than gold; harder to bond
  • Silver: highest electrical and thermal conductivity of any metal; used in niche high-performance designs

The thermal expansion row explains much of the reliability story. Silicon expands at about 2.6 ppm/K and silicon carbide at roughly 4 ppm/K, so every temperature swing shears the bond between the chip and an aluminium ribbon that expands six to nine times as much. Copper's lower expansion and higher strength are why copper interconnects survive many more power cycles, provided the chip has a copper-compatible top metallisation.

Aluminium

Aluminium ribbon for power modules and battery packs

Aluminium ribbon, usually 99.99 % or 99.999 % pure and sometimes micro-alloyed, is the workhorse interconnect in IGBT, SiC MOSFET and diode power modules. Typical bonds link chip emitters or sources to the DBC or AMB substrate, and the substrate to terminals. It bonds at room temperature by ultrasound alone, so no heated stage is needed, and it is fully compatible with the aluminium top metallisation used on most power chips.

In battery packs, wide aluminium ribbon connects cylindrical or prismatic cells to busbars, and in some designs the ribbon also acts as a fusible link. Its low density keeps pack weight down and its softness protects cell terminals during bonding.

Gold

Gold ribbon for RF and microwave circuits

Gold ribbon is the established interconnect in RF, microwave and millimetre-wave modules, hybrid circuits and optoelectronic packages. At high frequency, current is confined to a thin surface layer: the skin depth in gold is under 1 µm at 10 GHz (about 0.8 µm, calculated from its resistivity). A wide, thin ribbon offers far more surface than a round wire of the same mass, so its resistance and inductance are lower, and its flat shape gives more predictable impedance between a chip and a transmission line.

Gold does not oxidise, so it bonds reliably to gold-plated pads and stays stable for decades in hermetic and non-hermetic packages. Gold ribbon is usually bonded thermosonically, with the substrate heated to moderate temperatures, often in the range of about 100 – 150 °C, to help bond formation. Typical sizes are very thin, in the order of 13 – 50 µm (0.5 – 2 mil) thick and from about 50 µm to over 1 mm wide.

  • Watch gold-aluminium contacts: gold ribbon on aluminium pads forms Au-Al intermetallics that grow with temperature and time and can weaken the bond; gold pads are preferred.
  • Keep loops short and flat: loop length directly adds inductance in RF paths, so ribbon is often bonded with a very low, controlled loop.
  • Specify hardness and elongation: soft, annealed gold ribbon bonds more consistently and is less likely to damage GaAs or GaN chips.
Copper and silver

Copper and silver bonding ribbon

Copper ribbon carries more current than aluminium of the same size and has higher thermal conductivity, so it spreads heat away from hot spots on the chip. Its lower expansion and higher strength give much longer power-cycling life. The trade-offs are hardness and oxidation: copper needs higher bond force and ultrasonic energy, a chip with a copper-compatible top layer (for example a copper metallisation or a bonded copper buffer) to avoid cratering the die, and sometimes an inert or forming-gas atmosphere to keep surfaces clean. Copper ribbon is used in next-generation SiC power modules, high-current discrete packages and battery connections.

Silver ribbon has the highest electrical and thermal conductivity of any metal and is softer than copper, so it bonds more easily. It is used in selected high-power and high-frequency applications where its performance justifies the cost. Designers should consider silver's tendency to tarnish in sulphur-containing atmospheres and to migrate under bias in humid conditions, and protect or encapsulate the assembly accordingly.

Ribbon is not a universal answer. Fine-pitch, high-I/O chips still need fine wire ball bonding or flip-chip. Ribbon must run in a straight line between pads, so routing is less flexible than with wire. It needs deep-access wedge bonding heads and ribbon-specific tools and cutters; a ball bonder cannot simply be converted. Wide ribbon also gives shorter spool lengths, which means more spool changes on high-throughput lines. Where these limits are acceptable, one ribbon can replace several parallel wires, reducing bond count and cycle time.

FAQ

Bonding ribbon: frequently asked questions

What is bonding ribbon?

Bonding ribbon is a thin, flat strip of aluminium, gold, copper or silver that is wedge-bonded to connect semiconductor chips to substrates, leads or terminals. It replaces several round wires with one wide, low-profile connection.

Is bonding ribbon the same as PV ribbon or a grounding bonding strap?

No. Photovoltaic ribbon is solder-coated copper used to connect solar cells, and grounding straps are heavy conductors for electrical earthing. Semiconductor bonding ribbon is a precision, high-purity strip made for ultrasonic wedge bonders.

Why is gold ribbon preferred above about 10 GHz?

At these frequencies current flows in a surface layer under 1 µm deep. A flat gold ribbon has more surface than a round wire, giving lower resistance and inductance, and gold does not oxidise, so contacts stay stable.

Does ribbon bonding need heat?

Aluminium ribbon is bonded at room temperature with ultrasound alone. Gold ribbon is normally bonded thermosonically on a heated stage. Copper ribbon needs higher force and energy and sometimes a protective gas.

Can one ribbon replace several bonding wires?

Often yes. A 2.0 × 0.2 mm aluminium ribbon has a cross-section of 0.4 mm², about the same as two 500 µm round wires, with a lower loop and fewer bonds.

What tolerances are achievable on aluminium and copper ribbon?

Typical tolerances are about ±0.05 – 0.10 mm on width and ±0.01 – 0.03 mm on thickness, depending on size. Tighter values can be agreed for specific programmes.
