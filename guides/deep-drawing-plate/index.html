Aluminium Sheet and Circles for Deep Drawing

Deep drawing pulls a flat aluminium blank into a die with a punch to make a seamless cup, can, pot, housing or panel. Whether the part draws cleanly or splits depends as much on the sheet as on the tool: alloy, temper, grain size, anisotropy (earing) and surface all matter. This guide explains which aluminium grades and tempers draw best, the material properties to specify, starting-point tooling rules and how to troubleshoot common defects. Fundamentals

What happens to aluminium during deep drawing

In deep drawing the flange of the blank is pulled radially inwards over the die radius while being squeezed circumferentially. The cup wall carries the drawing force, so the part fails if the force needed to draw the flange exceeds what the wall near the punch radius can carry.

That balance explains what a good drawing sheet needs. It must be ductile enough for the flange to compress and flow, strain-harden gradually so the wall stays strong, and resist thinning through its thickness. These qualities are captured by elongation, the strain-hardening exponent (n-value) and the plastic strain ratio (r-value). Aluminium alloys have lower r-values than drawing-quality steel, which is why they draw slightly less deeply in a single operation and why tool design and lubrication matter more.

Aluminium is drawn because it gives light, corrosion-resistant, thermally conductive parts with an attractive surface that can be anodised, polished or coated. Cookware, lighting reflectors, cans and closures, aerosol and battery housings, appliance parts, automotive inner panels and pressure-vessel heads are all made this way.

Heat-treatable alloys such as 2024, 6061 and 6016 are formed in the O or T4 temper and aged after forming; 6016-T4 is common for automotive outer panels. High-strength aerospace alloys are generally stretch-formed or machined rather than deep drawn.

As a general rule, the softer and purer the alloy, the deeper it draws, but the weaker and easier to dent the finished part. 3003 is the usual compromise for cookware and general deep-drawn parts; 1050 and 1060 are chosen for maximum draw depth and for reflectors that will be brightened or anodised; 5052 and 5182 when the part needs strength after forming.

Tempers

O, H2x or H1x: choosing the temper

O (annealed)

Lowest strength and highest elongation; the default for deep draws, multi-stage redraws and spinning. The finished part gains strength from work hardening during drawing.

H111 / H12

Slightly strain-hardened. Gives a little more strength in shallow and medium draws while keeping good formability.

H22 / H24

Strain-hardened then partially annealed. For the same strength, H2x has higher elongation than H1x, so it suits medium-depth draws where a firmer part is wanted, such as pot lids and trays.

H14 / H16 / H18

Harder tempers for shallow forming, embossing and blanking where draw depth is small and stiffness matters.

H19 can stock

Fully hard 3004/3104 for beverage cans, drawn and then wall-ironed; a specialised product with tightly controlled earing and surface.

Grain size

In O temper a fine, uniform grain is essential; coarse grain causes "orange peel" on drawn walls and can lead to splits. Specify a maximum grain size for visible parts.

Process guidance

Draw ratios and tooling starting points

The limiting drawing ratio (LDR) is the largest blank diameter that can be drawn into a cup of a given punch diameter in one operation. For common aluminium drawing alloys in O temper, single-draw ratios of roughly 1.8 – 2.0 are typical starting points, i.e. a first-draw diameter reduction of about 40 – 45 %. Redraws allow smaller reductions, typically 20 – 30 % and falling with each stage unless the part is annealed in between.

The blank diameter for a simple flat-bottomed cylindrical cup can be estimated from surface area: D ≈ √(d² + 4dh), where d is the cup diameter and h the height (ignoring the corner radius and trim allowance). For example, a 100 mm diameter, 60 mm deep cup needs a blank of about 184 mm, a ratio of 1.84, which is near the single-draw limit; a deeper cup would need a redraw.

Ordering

How to order deep-drawing aluminium

  1. Describe the partPart drawing or dimensions, number of draw stages, and whether the surface will be visible, polished, anodised or coated.
  2. Specify the materialAlloy, temper, thickness and tolerance, earing and grain-size limits, standard (ASTM B209, EN 485, JIS H4000).
  3. Choose the formCoil (width, ID/OD), cut sheet, or circles (diameter, tolerance, edge condition).
  4. Packing and documentsInterleaving or film for surface-critical parts, moisture-proof export packing, mill test certificate to EN 10204 3.1.
  5. Trial and approvalRun a press trial on samples before bulk orders, especially when changing alloy or supplier.

FAQ

Deep drawing aluminium (aluminum) sheet: FAQ

What is the best aluminium alloy for deep drawing?

For maximum draw depth, 1050, 1060 or 1100 in O temper. For a stronger part that still draws well, 3003-O or H22 is the most common choice. 5052-O and 5182-O are used where strength and corrosion resistance after forming matter.

What is earing in deep drawing?

Earing is the wavy, uneven rim of a drawn cup, caused by the sheet having different properties in different directions after rolling. It is measured by an earing test (for example EN 1669) and controlled by the rolling and annealing route; low-earing stock reduces trim scrap.

Why does my drawn aluminium part look like orange peel?

Orange peel is caused by coarse grains in annealed sheet, which deform unevenly and roughen the surface. Specify a fine grain size for O-temper material that will be visible, polished or anodised.

How deep can aluminum be drawn in one stage?

For common O-temper alloys a blank-to-punch diameter ratio of about 1.8 – 2.0 is a typical single-draw limit. Deeper parts are made in several redraw stages, sometimes with intermediate annealing.

Should I buy sheet or circles for deep drawing?

Circles save a blanking step and scrap handling, and they are usual for cookware and round parts. Coil or sheet suits high-speed lines with in-line blanking and non-round parts.

Can deep-drawn aluminium parts be anodised?

Yes. 1050, 1060 and 5005 anodise most evenly. Specify fine grain and a clean surface, because grain size and drawing marks show through the anodic film.
