Tanker plate guide

5454 Aluminium Plate for Asphalt and Heated Chemical Tankers

5454 is the aluminium-magnesium plate grade tank builders choose when the cargo is hot: asphalt, bitumen, heated emulsions and warm chemicals. Its magnesium content is held below 3 %, which keeps it resistant to the sensitisation that makes higher-magnesium 5083 and 5182 unsuitable above about 65 °C. This guide explains the grade, its properties and tempers, how to weld it and how to specify plate for a tanker build.

The problem it solves

Why hot cargo needs a different aluminium

Most aluminium road tankers are built from 5083 or 5182, both of which contain 4 – 5 % magnesium. That magnesium gives high strength without heat treatment, but it has a weakness: held for long periods between roughly 65 °C and 200 °C, the excess magnesium migrates to grain boundaries and forms a continuous film of the β-phase (Al3Mg2). The alloy is then "sensitised" and can suffer intergranular corrosion and stress-corrosion cracking.

Asphalt and bitumen are hauled hot, commonly at 140 – 180 °C, so that they stay pumpable. Heated emulsions, molasses, waxes, some resins and many chemicals also travel warm. A tank shell that sits at those temperatures for thousands of hours over its life must not sensitise, and that is the job 5454 was developed for.

5454 limits magnesium to 2.4 – 3.0 % and adds 0.5 – 1.0 % manganese for strength. With magnesium below about 3 %, there is too little excess magnesium to form a continuous β-phase network, so the alloy stays resistant to intergranular attack at elevated temperature. Strength at room temperature is lower than 5083, but it is still well above 3003 or 5052, and 5454 welds and forms just as easily.

In one sentence

  • Choose 5454 when the shell will run above about 65 °C for long periods; choose 5083 or 5182 for ambient-temperature cargo where their extra strength saves weight.

Which temper and thickness for each part of the tank

A tanker is not built from one plate specification. Shells need strength and roll well; heads and baffles are pressed or spun and need maximum ductility. The table summarises common practice; your design code and calculations set the final thicknesses.

Wide plate saves welding. With plate up to about 2,500 – 2,600 mm wide and long lengths, a typical barrel can be formed from fewer pieces, which means fewer longitudinal seams to weld, inspect and potentially leak.

Fabrication

Welding 5454: use the right filler for hot service

5454 is readily welded by MIG and TIG. The point that is often missed is the filler. The common 5xxx fillers, ER5356 and ER5183, contain about 5 % magnesium. Using them on a hot tank puts a high-magnesium weld bead into exactly the temperature range where it can sensitise, which defeats the reason for choosing 5454.

For service above about 65 °C, the recommended filler is ER5554, which matches the magnesium level of the base metal (about 2.7 %). It gives slightly lower weld strength than ER5356 but keeps the joint resistant to intergranular corrosion when hot. For ambient-temperature cargo in a 5454 tank, ER5356 is acceptable.

Preparation

Remove oil with solvent and the oxide film with a stainless wire brush just before welding. Keep plate dry; condensation is a source of hydrogen porosity.

Process

Pulsed MIG for long shell seams; TIG for nozzles, manholes and repairs. Back-gouge and seal-weld full-penetration butt joints.

Strength after welding

Because 5454 is not heat-treatable, welding an O or H111 plate costs almost no strength. Welding H32 plate softens the heat-affected zone back to near O-temper values, which design codes account for.

Forming

O and H111 bend comfortably to about 1 – 2 × thickness. Heads are spun or pressed in O temper.

Inspection

Radiography or ultrasonic testing of shell seams, leak testing and pressure testing as required by the tank code.

Finishing

Mill finish is normal. Insulated asphalt tanks are clad; bright or mirror sheet is sometimes used for the outer jacket to reflect sunlight.

Under ADR, shell thickness for an aluminium tank is derived from a reference mild-steel thickness using the alloy's guaranteed tensile strength and elongation, so a plate with a higher strength-times-elongation product can be thinner. That is why the certified Rm and A values on the mill certificate matter to the tank designer, not just the alloy name.

FAQ

5454 tanker plate: frequently asked questions

Why is 5454 used for asphalt tankers instead of 5083?

Asphalt is carried at around 140 – 180 °C. 5083 contains more than 3 % magnesium and can sensitise when held above about 65 °C, risking intergranular and stress-corrosion cracking. 5454 keeps magnesium below 3 %, so it stays stable at those temperatures.

What filler wire should be used to weld 5454 aluminum tankers?

For hot service use ER5554, which matches the base metal's magnesium level. ER5356 or ER5183 contain about 5 % magnesium and are better kept to tanks that operate at ambient temperature.

Is 5454-H111 or H32 better for a tank shell?

H111 is the usual choice because it rolls and welds easily and its strength barely changes in the weld zone. H32 is stronger and can allow a thinner shell, but it is less formable and the heat-affected zone softens towards O-temper strength.

What thickness of 5454 plate is used for tanker shells?

Shells are commonly 5 – 8 mm, heads equal to or thicker than the shell and baffles slightly thinner. The exact figures come from the tank design code calculation, which uses the certified tensile strength and elongation.

Can 5454 plate be used for ambient fuel tankers too?

Yes. It performs well, but a 5083 or 5182 tank can be lighter for the same capacity because of their higher strength. Builders often choose 5454 when a tank may carry heated cargo at some point in its life.

Which certificates come with 5454 plate?

Each lot ships with an EN 10204 3.1 mill test certificate showing chemistry, tensile strength, yield strength and elongation against ASTM B209, EN 485 or EN 14286. Third-party inspection can be arranged on request.
