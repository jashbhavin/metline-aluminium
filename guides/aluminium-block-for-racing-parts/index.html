Billet Aluminium Block for Racing Parts

Racing calipers, housings, bearing supports, main caps and brackets are usually CNC-machined from solid aluminium block, plate or bar, the material machinists call "billet". The usual choices are 6061 in stress-relieved tempers for most parts, 7075 where strength and stiffness per gram matter most, and 2024-T351 for high-clamp, fatigue-loaded parts. This guide explains how to pick the alloy and temper, why billet beats castings for these parts, and how to keep machined parts flat and leak-free.

Overview

Why racing parts are machined from billet aluminium

Race parts are made in small batches, changed often and loaded close to their limits. Machining them from solid wrought aluminium avoids pattern and die costs, allows design changes between events, and gives a material with predictable strength and no hidden porosity.

"Billet" in motorsport means wrought stock: rolled plate, extruded or rolled bar, or forged block that has been hot-worked and heat-treated. Hot working closes casting pores and breaks up the cast structure, so the metal is dense and uniform in every direction that matters. A good casting can be excellent too, but its quality depends on the foundry, the gating and how well the part was inspected. For a brake caliper or a hydraulic body, a single pore that breaks into a fluid passage after machining scraps the part or, worse, leaks under pressure.

Wrought tempers also offer controlled residual stress. Plate in T651 and bar in T6511 have been stretched after quenching to relieve stresses, so deep pockets and thin walls stay where the programme put them. That matters on five-axis parts with many datum faces and bearing bores that must line up.

Billet vs casting: when each makes sense

  • Billet: low volume, frequent revisions, thin walls, sealing faces, high fatigue or impact loads, short development time
  • Casting: high volume, complex near-net shapes, internal cores, milder loads, cost per part is the main driver
  • Forging: high volume and high loads, for example production suspension parts; machined after forging

6061 (T651 plate, T6511 bar)

The default. Good strength, the best corrosion resistance and anodising of the three, easy to machine and widely available in every size. Weldable if a bracket must be added later.

7075 (T651 or T7351)

About 80 % stronger than 6061 for little extra weight. Choose T7351 for thick blocks and parts in damp or salty conditions: it is slightly weaker than T651 but far more resistant to stress-corrosion cracking and has lower residual stress.

2024-T351

High strength with good fatigue and toughness, naturally aged and stretched. Common for caps and highly clamped parts. Needs anodising or coating for corrosion protection and is not weldable.

Tempers

Why T651, T6511, T7351 and T351 matter

Heat-treated aluminium is quenched rapidly from about 500 °C. The surface cools first, locking in residual stresses: compressive at the surface and tensile in the core. If you machine away one side of an unrelieved block, those stresses rebalance and the part bows. The "x51" tempers prevent this.

What each temper means

T651 (plate, rolled bar)
Solution treated, stretched about 1.5 – 3 %, then artificially aged. Low residual stress; the standard for machined plate and block
T6511 (extruded bar and profiles)
Solution treated, stretched, then given minor straightening and aged. The usual temper for 6061 extruded round, square and flat bar
T7351 (7075 plate, bar)
Stretched and over-aged for high stress-corrosion resistance; preferred for thick 7075 sections
T351 (2024 plate, bar)
Solution treated, stretched and naturally aged; stable and tough
T6 (no stretch)
Same strength as T651/T6511 but higher residual stress; avoid for thin-walled or deep-pocketed parts

Machining

Keeping racing parts flat and true during machining

  1. Choose the blank wiselyUse stress-relieved stock (T651, T6511, T7351, T351) and order blanks with modest stock allowance, typically a few millimetres per side, so you are not removing the outer layers unevenly.
  2. Rough symmetricallyRemove material in balanced stages from both sides, flip the part between roughing passes and leave even finishing stock.
  3. Release and re-clampAfter roughing, unclamp to let the part relax, then re-clamp lightly and finish critical faces and bores last.
  4. Mind the grain directionOrient highly stressed features along the rolling or extrusion direction; the short-transverse direction of thick plate has the lowest ductility and, in 7075-T651, the lowest stress-corrosion resistance.
  5. Control heatUse sharp, polished carbide tools with high positive rake, high spindle speeds and plenty of coolant. Aluminium expands about twice as much as steel, so measure critical bores at a stable temperature.

A common mistake is ordering the cheapest T6 or as-rolled block for a thin, pocketed part, then fighting distortion for weeks. The small premium for properly stress-relieved stock is usually repaid in the first batch.

Finishing

Anodising and coating billet racing parts

Type II (sulphuric) anodising

Decorative and protective, typically about 5 – 25 µm, in clear, black or colours. Ideal for 6061 brackets, housings and steering parts.

Type III (hard) anodising

Thicker, very hard layer (often 25 – 50 µm) for wear surfaces such as piston bores and slides. About half the thickness grows outward and half penetrates, so allow for growth on bores and threads.

PTFE-sealed hard anodising

Hard anodising impregnated with PTFE for lower friction and better release in brake and hydraulic components.

Masking critical features

Mask threads, bearing seats and sealing faces, or machine them with compensation for coating build-up, agreed with your anodiser before machining.

Alloy effect

6061 gives the most even colour; 7075 anodises well but slightly darker; 2024 can look mottled and is often hard-anodised or painted instead.

Fatigue note

Hard anodic films can reduce fatigue strength of highly stressed parts; designers sometimes limit coating thickness or avoid it on critical fatigue zones.

Ordering

How to order aluminium block for racing parts

  • Alloy and temper, for example 6061-T651 plate, 6061-T6511 bar, 7075-T7351 or 2024-T351, and the governing standard (ASTM B209, B211, B221, AMS)
  • Blank dimensions with machining allowance, or the finished part envelope so blanks can be sized for you
  • Quantity per size and whether repeat batches must come from the same lot
  • Inspection: mill test certificate to EN 10204 3.1; ultrasonic testing for thick blocks used in critical parts
  • Packing that protects saw-cut faces and marks each blank with alloy, temper and lot number

FAQ

Billet aluminium (aluminum) for racing parts: frequently asked questions

Is 7075 always better than 6061 for racing parts?

No. 7075 is much stronger, but 6061 resists corrosion better, anodises more evenly, costs less and is weldable. Most race parts are 6061; 7075 is reserved for parts where strength or stiffness per gram is the limit.

Why use T6511 or T651 instead of T6?

They are stretched after quenching to relieve residual stress, so thin walls and deep pockets do not warp as material is removed. T6 stock has the same strength but distorts more on complex parts.

When should I choose 7075-T7351 over 7075-T651?

For thick blocks and parts exposed to moisture, road salt or sustained stress. T7351 gives up some strength but has far better resistance to stress-corrosion cracking and lower residual stress.

Are billet calipers better than cast calipers?

Billet calipers machined from stress-relieved 6061 or 7075 have uniform, pore-free material and can be revised quickly, which suits racing. High-quality castings or forgings are cost-effective for high-volume road parts.

How much allowance should a blank have?

Typically a few millimetres per side for saw-cut blanks, more for long or thick parts. Enough to clean up saw marks and surface layers evenly, but not so much that heavy one-sided removal causes distortion.

Can you supply saw-cut blanks with certificates?

Yes. Metline supplies plate, block and bar saw-cut to blank sizes, marked with alloy, temper and lot, with mill test certificates to EN 10204 3.1.
