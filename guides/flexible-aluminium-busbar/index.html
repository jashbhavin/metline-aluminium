Flexible Aluminium Busbar

A flexible aluminium busbar is a high-current conductor built from many thin aluminium foils or fine wires, with solid contact ends and a flexible middle. It carries hundreds to thousands of amperes while absorbing vibration, thermal expansion and assembly misalignment that would crack a rigid bar. This guide covers the main constructions, conductor alloys, sizing, joints, insulation and installation, so you can specify the right design for EV packs, energy storage, inverters, transformers and switchgear.

Overview

Why use a flexible aluminium busbar?

Rigid busbars are efficient conductors but they transmit every movement straight into the terminals they connect. Battery modules swell and shrink as they charge, transformers hum, vehicles vibrate and long bus runs expand with temperature. A flexible section between two rigid points takes up that movement, so bolted joints and welded terminals stay unstressed.

Aluminium is well suited to flexible conductors. Its density is about 2.70 g/cm³ against roughly 8.9 g/cm³ for copper, so an aluminium conductor of equal resistance weighs about half as much as the copper one even though it is larger in section. In battery systems where cells have aluminium terminals, an aluminium connector also avoids an aluminium-copper interface and the galvanic and intermetallic problems that come with it.

The trade-off is conductivity: high-purity aluminium reaches about 61 – 62 % IACS, so the cross-section must be larger than a copper part for the same current. Good design also has to deal with the hard, insulating oxide film on aluminium at every contact face. Both are routine engineering problems with well-proven solutions, covered later in this guide.

What a flexible aluminium busbar solves

  • Vibration and shock that would fatigue a rigid bar or loosen bolts
  • Thermal expansion between equipment that heats and cools at different rates
  • Hole-position and height tolerances when modules or cabinets are assembled
  • Weight in EVs, aircraft ground power and portable systems
  • Dissimilar-metal joints where the equipment terminals are aluminium
Types

Main constructions of flexible aluminium busbar

Laminated foil busbar

Ten to fifty thin aluminium foils, typically 0.10 or 0.20 mm each, are stacked and their ends fused into solid contact pads by press (diffusion) welding, ultrasonic welding or laser welding. The free foils in between slide over each other and bend easily. This is the most common type for EV and battery-storage connections because it is compact and carries high current in a low height.

Braided busbar

Fine aluminium wires are braided into a flat or round strap and the ends are crimped or welded into terminals. Braids flex in every direction and tolerate continuous vibration better than laminates, but they occupy more volume for the same conductive area.

Rigid-flex busbar

A short laminated section is welded into a solid aluminium bar or terminal. The rigid part sets the routing and the bolting interface; the flexible part sits only where movement must be absorbed. Used in longer runs between transformers, switchgear and busduct.

Insulated flexible busbar

Any of the above with heat-shrink sleeving or a dipped PVC coating on the flexible zone, leaving only the contact pads bare. Needed where clearances are small or live parts may be touched.

Large-section expansion joints

Wide laminations (100 – 200 mm) of thicker strip, often around 1 mm per layer, used as expansion links in heavy busbar systems and rectifier connections carrying thousands of amperes.

Formed laminates

Laminates pre-bent into offset, S, U or V shapes so they fit between terminals at different heights and still allow movement in the planned direction.

Materials

Aluminium alloys for flexible busbars

Flexible conductors use the purest commercial aluminium grades because conductivity falls quickly with alloying additions. Foils and wires are supplied in the annealed (O) temper for maximum flexibility; terminals and rigid sections can use stronger tempers or a heat-treatable conductor alloy.

Design

How to size a flexible aluminium busbar

  1. Start from current and allowed temperature riseContinuous current, peak and short-circuit current, and the temperature rise the equipment allows (often 30 – 65 K above ambient) set the conductive cross-section. Enclosed, still-air installations need more area than ventilated ones.
  2. Convert from a copper design if neededAluminium resistivity is about 1.6 times that of copper. For equal resistance you need about 1.6 times the copper area; for equal temperature rise the factor is often closer to 1.3 because the larger aluminium part sheds heat over more surface. Confirm by calculation or temperature-rise test.
  3. Choose foil gauge for the movementThinner foils bend more easily and survive more cycles; thicker foils are cheaper to stack and weld and are stiffer. 0.10 mm suits continuous vibration, 0.20 – 0.30 mm suits occasional assembly or thermal movement.
  4. Set the free length and shapeThe flexible zone must be long enough to take the expected displacement without straining the welded ends. A pre-formed offset or loop increases movement capacity in a short space.
  5. Design the contact padsPad area and bolt pattern control contact resistance. Use enough bolts, flat clean surfaces and plating where the mating terminal is copper.
  6. Specify insulation and clearanceWorking voltage, creepage and clearance, flame-retardancy needs and the operating temperature range decide the insulation material and thickness.

Contacts

Making reliable contacts with aluminium

Most field failures of aluminium conductors start at the joint, not in the conductor. Aluminium forms a thin, hard oxide film within moments of exposure to air, and it creeps under sustained bolt pressure more than copper. Both are managed with established practice.

Welded foil ends

Press-welding or ultrasonic welding fuses all foils into a solid block, so current passes through metal rather than across many foil-to-foil contacts. Weld quality is verified by peel or cross-section tests and resistance measurement.

Surface preparation

Contact faces should be flat and freshly cleaned or plated just before assembly. A contact compound helps keep oxygen and moisture out of bare aluminium joints.

Plating

Tin plating gives a stable, low-resistance contact surface and is the usual choice when mating to copper. Nickel resists higher temperatures and abrasion; silver offers the lowest contact resistance.

Bolting

Use the torque the terminal and fastener allow, with spring (Belleville) washers or wide flat washers to maintain pressure as aluminium relaxes. Avoid over-tightening thin pads.

Aluminium to copper

Do not bolt bare aluminium directly to bare copper in damp conditions. Use tin plating on one or both faces, or an aluminium-copper bimetal transition piece.

Checking after commissioning

Measure temperature rise or joint resistance after start-up and after the first thermal cycles to catch any joint that has lost pressure.

Installation

Installation rules for flexible aluminium busbars

  • Install the flexible zone in its relaxed shape; never stretch or twist it to make up large hole-position errors
  • Respect the minimum bend radius and do not fold laminates flat or reverse-bend them repeatedly at one point
  • Support long rigid-flex assemblies so the flexible zone does not carry the weight of the rigid section
  • Keep insulation away from sharp edges and bolt heads, and keep the specified clearances to earthed metal
  • Tighten in stages to the specified torque and mark bolts so loosening is visible on inspection
  • In battery packs, plan for cell swelling over life, not just at assembly
  • Record the as-installed joint resistance or temperature for comparison at maintenance

A typical EV battery module connector, for example, uses about 15 – 20 layers of 0.2 mm 1060 or 1070 foil with welded pads at each end and a gentle offset in the middle to take up module tolerance and swelling. A transformer-to-switchgear link may instead use a wide rigid-flex assembly with thicker laminations and tin-plated bolted ends.

FAQ

Flexible aluminium (aluminum) busbar: frequently asked questions

Should I choose 0.1 mm or 0.2 mm foil?

Choose 0.1 mm where the connector sees continuous vibration or must bend in a tight space, because thinner foils have lower bending stress and longer fatigue life. 0.2 mm needs fewer layers for the same section, welds more easily and suits connections that only move during assembly or thermal cycling.

How many foil layers does a flexible aluminium busbar need?

Divide the required cross-section by the foil thickness times width. A 50 mm wide connector needing 200 mm² of aluminium uses 20 layers of 0.2 mm or 40 layers of 0.1 mm. Typical designs fall between 10 and 50 layers.

How much larger must aluminium be than copper?

About 1.6 times the area for the same resistance, and often around 1.3 times for the same temperature rise. Even at the larger size the aluminium part weighs roughly half as much.

Can a flexible aluminium busbar connect to copper terminals?

Yes, if the contact is plated (usually tin) or a bimetal transition piece is used, and the joint is kept dry and correctly torqued. Bare aluminium bolted to bare copper in humid conditions will corrode.

Which alloy is best for laminated foil connectors?

1070, 1060 and 1350 are the usual choices. All are over 99.5 % aluminium with conductivity around 61 – 62 % IACS and excellent formability in the annealed temper.

Do you supply the aluminum foil and strip for flexible busbars?

Yes. Metline supplies conductor-grade aluminium foil, strip, wire and busbar stock, cut, punched, formed and plated to drawing, with EN 10204 3.1 certificates, to customers worldwide.
