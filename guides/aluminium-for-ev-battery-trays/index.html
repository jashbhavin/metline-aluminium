Aluminium for EV Battery Trays and Battery Enclosures

Most electric-vehicle battery trays are built from 6xxx aluminium extrusions welded into a frame, closed with an extruded or sheet floor and an aluminium, steel or composite lid. Aluminium gives the stiffness, crash energy absorption and corrosion resistance the pack needs at a fraction of the mass of steel. This guide explains which alloys go where, how they are joined and sealed, and what the tray must survive. Metline supplies the extrusions, sheet and plate, cut and machined on request.

Overview

What a battery tray must do

The battery tray (or lower housing) carries the cells or modules, bolts the pack into the vehicle body and is usually part of the car’s structure. It has to stay stiff under the weight of several hundred kilograms of cells, protect them from side-pole impacts and from objects striking the underbody, keep water and dust out for the life of the car, carry the cooling system, and contain a cell fire long enough for occupants to leave.

Aluminium meets these demands with low density (2.70 g/cm³, about one third of steel), extrusions that put material exactly where it is needed, good energy absorption when hollow sections fold in a crash, natural corrosion resistance against road salt, and high thermal conductivity that helps spread heat to the cooling plates. Because the pack is one of the heaviest components in an EV, saving mass in the tray directly increases range or allows a smaller battery for the same range.

Most volume trays today use a hybrid design: a welded frame of extruded side rails and cross members, a floor made of welded multi-hollow extrusions or a sheet pan, and a top cover. High-pressure die castings are also used for some trays and for parts of others, and steel trays remain common where cost matters most.

Engineering requirements in short

  • Stiffness and strength to carry the cell mass and act as part of the body structure
  • Crash performance: side-pole and frontal load paths, plus underbody impact protection
  • Sealing to IP67 or similar for the life of the vehicle
  • Integration of liquid cooling, either in the floor or as separate plates
  • Thermal-runaway containment and resistance to an external fire
  • Corrosion resistance against road salt, stone chips and galvanic contact with steel fasteners

Alloy choice varies by vehicle maker; the table shows common practice, not a single standard.

6082 is the stronger choice for crash-critical rails and is standard in Europe; 6005A offers a better balance of strength and extrudability for large, thin-walled multi-hollow sections, so it dominates wide floor profiles; 6061 is common in North America and Asia, and 6063 is used where strength demands are lower and surface or thin-wall extrusion matters more.

Two property issues dominate tray design. First, welding softens 6xxx alloys: the heat-affected zone of a fusion weld in 6082-T6 can lose roughly a third to a half of its yield strength, so designers place welds in low-stress areas or use friction stir welding, which runs cooler. Second, crash behaviour depends on ductility, not just strength. Rails must fold progressively without cracking, so vehicle makers often specify crush tests on the extrusion and control quench rate and ageing to achieve it.

Fabrication

Joining, sealing and machining the tray

Friction stir welding (FSW)

The usual method for joining wide floor extrusions side by side. Solid-state, low distortion, very flat panels and leak-tight joints with less strength loss than fusion welding.

MIG and CMT welding

Used for frame corners, cross-member joints and brackets. Filler 4043 or 5356 depending on joint design; low-heat-input processes such as cold metal transfer limit distortion.

Laser welding

High speed and low heat input for thin sheet and lid flanges; requires good fit-up.

Mechanical joining and bonding

Self-piercing rivets, flow-drill screws and structural adhesive, often combined, especially for joining aluminium to steel or composite.

Sealing

Lid-to-tray seals use foam gaskets or formed-in-place sealant on a machined flange; flatness of the flange and leak testing of every pack are critical.

Machining

Frames are CNC machined after welding to give accurate mounting faces, sealing flanges and module seats; long-bed five-axis machines are typical.

Distortion is the constant enemy. A tray two metres long assembled from many welded extrusions can move several millimetres if weld sequence and fixturing are not controlled, and the sealing flange and module seats must then be machined flat. Specifying extrusions with tight straightness and twist tolerances reduces the work needed after welding.

Extrusions

Designing extrusions for trays

  • Multi-cavity sections: internal webs stiffen rails and floors and give crash load paths; keep wall thickness as uniform as possible for consistent extrusion
  • Wide integrated floors: fewer, wider profiles mean fewer welds, less distortion and fewer leak paths, but need larger presses and careful control of flatness
  • Integrated cooling: coolant channels can be extruded into the floor, avoiding separate cooling plates, though designs with separate brazed plates are easier to repair and modify
  • Weld preparation: design tongue-and-groove or backing features into the section to locate profiles for friction stir welding
  • Tolerances: agree straightness, twist, flatness and wall tolerances to EN 755-9 or EN 12020-2 (for 6060/6063 precision profiles) or tighter where the part will be machined
  • Temper sequence: profiles are often bent or formed in T4 and aged to T6 afterwards; define the delivery temper with the forming plan

Aluminium melts at around 600 – 650 °C, below the temperatures reached in a cell thermal runaway, so a bare aluminium lid can burn through over a failing cell. Designs counter this with mica, ceramic-fibre or intumescent layers under the lid, venting paths that direct hot gases away from the cabin, and steel or composite lids in some packs. The tray frame and floor are usually less exposed but are included in the fire resistance test.

Durability

Corrosion protection and surface treatment

Under the car, the tray sees salt spray, stone chipping and standing water. 6xxx and 5xxx alloys resist this well on their own, but trays usually receive a conversion coating and paint, cathodic e-coat or a stone-chip protective coating on the underside. Where steel fasteners or steel body parts touch the aluminium, isolating washers, coated fasteners and sealants prevent galvanic corrosion.

Coolant leaks are another concern. Glycol-water coolants are compatible with aluminium when inhibited correctly, but brazed or welded cooling channels must be leak-tested and the coolant chemistry maintained, since uninhibited or contaminated coolant can pit aluminium from the inside.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) for EV battery trays: FAQ

Which aluminium alloy is used for EV battery trays?

Frames and floors are mostly 6xxx extrusions: 6082-T6 for the strongest crash rails, 6005A-T6 for wide thin-walled floor profiles, and 6061-T6 and 6063-T6 in many designs. Sheet floors and lids use 5754 or 5182, and machined mounts use 6061 or 6082 plate and bar.

Why use aluminium instead of steel for battery enclosures?

Aluminium gives a substantially lighter tray for the same stiffness, absorbs crash energy well in extruded hollow sections, resists road-salt corrosion and conducts heat. Steel is cheaper and more fire-resistant, so some packs use steel lids or steel trays where cost or fire protection dominate.

How are aluminium battery trays welded?

Wide floor extrusions are usually friction stir welded, which gives flat, leak-tight joints with limited strength loss. Frame corners and brackets are MIG or CMT welded, lids are often laser welded or bolted, and mixed-material joints use rivets, flow-drill screws and adhesive.

Does welding weaken 6xxx alloys in a tray?

Yes. Fusion welding softens the heat-affected zone of T6 material significantly, so welds are placed in lower-stress areas and the design uses reduced HAZ strength. Friction stir welding reduces, but does not remove, this softening.

Can an aluminium battery tray contain a thermal runaway?

Not by itself, since aluminium melts at about 600 – 650 °C. Packs use mica, ceramic or intumescent barriers, venting paths and sometimes steel or composite lids to contain or direct the heat and gases during the time required by regulations.

Can Metline supply a complete battery tray?

No. Metline supplies the aluminium materials: custom extrusions, sheet, plate and bar, with cutting and machining on request and EN 10204 3.1 certificates. Welding, assembly and pack validation remain with the tray manufacturer.
