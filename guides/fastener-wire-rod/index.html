Aluminium Wire for Rivets, Screws and Bolts

Aluminium fastener wire is drawn wire and wire rod made for cold heading and machining into rivets, screws, bolts, nuts and studs. The right choice depends on how the fastener is formed and loaded: soft, ductile grades such as 1100, 2117 and 5056 for rivets, free-machining 2011 for turned screws, and heat-treatable 2024, 6061, 7050 and 7075 for structural bolts. This guide compares the alloys, tempers, standards and quality checks fastener makers need.

Overview

Why aluminium fastener wire is a separate product

A rivet or bolt starts life as a short slug cut from a coil and shaped in a header in a fraction of a second. The head may spread to twice the wire diameter in one or two blows. Ordinary drawn wire would split under that strain, so fastener wire is produced and tested specifically for cold heading: tight diameter tolerance, a clean surface free of seams and laps, controlled grain size and a temper that gives the right balance of softness and strength.

Aluminium fasteners exist for three main reasons. They avoid galvanic corrosion when joining aluminium structures, because the fastener and the parts are close in electrochemical potential. They save weight, which matters in aircraft where a single airframe can contain hundreds of thousands of rivets. And they are non-magnetic, conductive and easy to form, which suits electrical, electronic and appliance assemblies.

The same wire can be supplied in different forms depending on the customer's process: coils for automatic cold headers, straight lengths for screw machines, and heavier wire rod for drawing down to final size in the fastener plant.

What buyers usually specify

  • Alloy and temper, for example 2117-H13, 5056-O or 7075-O for heading then heat treatment
  • Diameter and tolerance, coil weight and inside diameter suited to the header
  • Surface condition and lubricant or coating for heading
  • Standard (ASTM B316, EN 1301, an AMS or customer specification) and test certificate

Rivet wire

Rivet wire: alloys, codes and driving condition

Solid rivets are formed twice: once when the head is cold-headed from wire, and again when the rivet is driven in the joint and the shank is upset to form the second head. The alloy and temper must survive both operations without cracking, then deliver the required shear strength in service.

AlloyCode letter (MS / NAS rivets)Head markingDriving condition
1100APlain headDriven as received
2117ADDimpleDriven as received (T4); becomes T3 after driving
2017DRaised dotSolution treated and driven promptly, or kept refrigerated
2024DDTwo raised dashes"Icebox" rivet: solution treated, then refrigerated and driven before it age-hardens
5056BRaised crossDriven as received
7050ERaised ringDriven in the aged T73 condition

The table follows the long-established conventions for aircraft solid rivets used in MS20470, MS20426 and related standards. 2117 became the standard general aircraft rivet precisely because it can be driven without heat treatment immediately before use. 2017 and 2024 rivets harden at room temperature after solution treatment, so they must be driven within minutes or stored at sub-zero temperatures to delay ageing, which is why they are called icebox rivets.

For industrial blind rivets, the body is commonly 5052, 5154 or 5056 for corrosion resistance and good flaring, or 1100 and 3xxx alloys for soft, easy setting. The mandrel may be aluminium (for all-aluminium joints) or a stronger metal supplied by the rivet maker.

Screws, bolts, nuts

Wire for screws, bolts, nuts and studs

Cold-headed screws and bolts

Heading stock in 6061, 6082, 2024 or 7075 is usually supplied annealed (O) or lightly drawn so it can be headed, then the parts are solution treated, quenched and aged to T6 (or T73 for 7075). Threads are rolled before or after heat treatment depending on the strength and fatigue requirement.

Machined screws

2011-T3 is the classic free-machining alloy: it forms small, broken chips on automatic lathes. Because it contains lead and bismuth, buyers with RoHS-sensitive products may specify lead-free variants such as 2011B, or 6xxx alloys that machine well.

Nuts

Nuts are cold-formed or machined from wire, rod or hexagon bar in 2024, 6061, 6082 and 7075. Use the same or a compatible alloy to the bolt so galling and galvanic effects are controlled.

Studs and pins

Straight lengths in 2024, 6061 and 7075 are cut, chamfered and thread-rolled. Straightness and diameter tolerance are as important as strength.

High-temperature fasteners

2219 and 2618 retain strength better than other aluminium alloys up to roughly 150 – 250 °C and are used for engine-area and aerospace fasteners.

Conductive fasteners

1xxx alloys and 6101 are used for terminal screws, bus connections and earthing clamps where current must pass through the joint.

Mechanical properties for finished aluminium bolts, screws and nuts are covered by ISO 8839 (EN 28839), which defines property classes for non-ferrous fasteners, and by aerospace specifications for flight hardware. The wire supplier delivers material that can reach those properties after the fastener maker's heat treatment.

Tempers

Choosing the wire temper for your process

TemperConditionWhen to use it
OFully annealed, softestSevere heading (large head-to-shank ratios), followed by heat treatment for heat-treatable alloys
H12 / H13 / H14Lightly to quarter-hard drawnRivets and parts that need some as-supplied strength but moderate heading strain; 2117-H13 is a classic rivet wire temper
H16 / H18Three-quarter to full hardStraight wire, pins and parts with little or no heading
T4Solution treated, naturally agedRivet wire in 2017, 2024 and 2117 for heading and driving
T3 / T8Solution treated, cold worked (and aged for T8)2011 screw-machine stock
T6 / T73Solution treated and artificially agedFinished-strength stock for machined fasteners, or as the final condition after heading

Matching the temper to the header is a joint decision between wire supplier and fastener maker. A wire that is too hard splits at the head periphery; one that is too soft may buckle in the die or fail to fill sharp head corners. Sending a sample part or the heading sequence with the enquiry is the quickest way to agree the right condition. For new parts, a short trial run on a few coils before full production confirms that head fill, cracking and finished strength are all within limits.

Quality

Quality requirements for cold-heading wire

  • Diameter and ovality: held to the tolerance of ASTM B316 or EN 1301-3, or tighter where the customer's dies require it, so slugs have consistent volume.
  • Surface quality: free of seams, laps, scratches and die marks that open into cracks when the head expands. Eddy-current testing in line is common for critical wire.
  • Upset (heading) test: a sample is compressed to a set fraction of its height and examined for cracks, confirming cold-heading performance.
  • Mechanical properties: tensile strength and elongation in the supplied temper, and after a test heat treatment for heat-treatable grades.
  • Shear test: on rivet wire, double-shear testing of heat-treated samples confirms that the finished rivet will meet its shear requirement.
  • Chemistry and grain size: verified per heat; coarse grains cause orange-peel heads and reduce fatigue life.
  • Coil quality: even layering, correct coil weight and inside diameter so the wire pays off smoothly into high-speed headers.

Each Metline delivery carries a mill test certificate to EN 10204 3.1 with heat number, chemistry and mechanical results; further tests such as upset or shear testing are agreed at order stage.

Production route

How aluminium fastener wire is produced

Understanding the route helps when discussing defects or tolerances with a supplier. The usual sequence is:

  1. Rod productionAlloy rod of about 9.5 – 25 mm is produced either by continuous casting and rolling or, for most 2xxx and 7xxx fastener alloys, by extruding homogenised billet. Extruded rod gives better control of chemistry, grain structure and surface for high-strength grades.
  2. DrawingThe rod is drawn through a series of carbide or diamond dies, reducing the diameter step by step. Each pass work-hardens the metal.
  3. Intermediate annealingBetween drawing stages the wire is annealed to restore ductility, so the final cold reduction can be set precisely to give the required H-temper or a soft O condition.
  4. Shaving or surface conditioningFor critical rivet and bolt wire, a thin surface layer may be removed by shaving to eliminate rod surface defects that would otherwise open up during heading.
  5. Final temper and heat treatmentWire is finished hard-drawn, annealed, or solution treated (for T4 rivet wire) as specified.
  6. Cleaning, coating and coilingDrawing lubricant is removed or replaced by a heading lubricant, the wire is wound into level-layered coils, and test samples are taken from each coil end.

Most heading defects trace back to one of three causes: surface seams carried over from the rod, inconsistent temper along the coil, or diameter variation that over- or under-fills the die. Good mills control all three; asking for in-line eddy-current testing and coil-end mechanical tests gives the buyer evidence that they have.

Design

Design and corrosion points for aluminium fasteners

Match the metals

Use aluminium fasteners in aluminium structures where possible. Where steel or stainless fasteners are unavoidable, isolate them with washers, sealants or coatings to limit galvanic attack on the aluminium.

Mind stress corrosion

7075-T6 can suffer stress-corrosion cracking under sustained tension in moist environments. The overaged T73 temper, or 7050-T73, is used for bolts and rivets that stay highly loaded.

Protect high-strength alloys

2xxx and 7xxx fasteners are usually anodised or given a conversion coating, and often installed wet with sealant in aircraft.

Magnesium assemblies

5056 rivets are traditionally used with magnesium parts because their potential is closer to magnesium than 2xxx rivets.

Temperature

Most aluminium fasteners lose strength above about 120 – 150 °C over long exposure; use 2219 or 2618 where higher temperatures are expected.

Thread engagement

Aluminium threads have lower shear capacity than steel, so allow longer engagement in tapped aluminium holes or use thread inserts.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) rivet and fastener wire: FAQ

Which aluminium alloy is best for rivets?

For general aircraft and structural solid rivets, 2117 is the standard because it can be driven as received. 2024 and 2017 give higher shear strength but must be driven soon after solution treatment. For marine, blind and magnesium-assembly rivets, 5056 and 5052 are common; for soft electrical rivets, 1100.

What is the difference between rivet wire and ordinary aluminium wire?

Rivet and cold-heading wire is produced to tighter diameter tolerance, a defect-free surface, controlled grain size and a temper chosen for heading. It is tested for heading performance, which ordinary wire is not.

Why are 2024 rivets called icebox rivets?

After solution treatment 2024 begins to age-harden at room temperature within minutes. Keeping the rivets refrigerated slows that ageing so they stay soft enough to drive; once driven they harden to full strength.

Can 7075 be used for bolts?

Yes. 7075 bolts give the highest strength of common aluminium fasteners. For bolts under sustained tension in moist environments, the T73 temper is preferred to reduce the risk of stress-corrosion cracking.

Which standard covers aluminium cold-heading wire?

ASTM B316 covers aluminium rivet and cold-heading wire and rod, and EN 1301 covers drawn aluminium wire in Europe. Aerospace fasteners add AMS, NAS or customer specifications.

Should I buy wire rod or finished drawn wire?

Buy finished drawn wire if you head directly from coil and have no drawing equipment. Fastener plants with their own drawing lines often buy wire rod of about 9.5 – 25 mm and draw it to the exact size their dies need, which gives flexibility across many part sizes and allows a final in-house anneal.

Do you supply wire in coils for automatic headers?

Yes. Fastener wire is supplied in coils or spools sized to the customer's pay-off equipment, as well as straight and cut lengths for screw machines.
