Grounding and bonding

Aluminium Ground Bus Bar: Earth Bar Material Guide

A ground (earth) bus bar is the common connection point that ties equipment, cabinet and structural bonding conductors to the earthing system. Aluminium ground bars in 1060, 1350 or 6101 carry fault current at about half the weight and a fraction of the metal cost of copper, provided the cross-section is increased and joints are made correctly. Metline supplies the aluminium bar cut to length, drilled to your hole pattern and plated where needed.

Function

What a ground bus bar does

Unlike a phase or neutral busbar, a ground bar carries no current in normal service. Its job is to provide a low-impedance path during a fault, so that protective devices trip quickly, and to hold every bonded metal part at the same potential so nobody can be exposed to a dangerous touch voltage.

In a distribution board or control cabinet, the ground bar collects the protective (PE) conductors of each outgoing circuit and connects them to the main earthing terminal. In a substation or plant room, larger earth bars link switchgear frames, cable armour, transformer tanks and the earth electrode system. In telecom and data rooms, bonding busbars collect the bonding conductors from racks, cable trays and surge protection.

Because a ground bar must survive the full prospective fault current for the clearing time of the protective device, it is sized by short-circuit withstand rather than continuous ampacity. That is the key difference when you design it in aluminium.

Material choice

Aluminium or copper for a ground bar?

Copper and tinned copper are the traditional choice, and many telecom specifications call for them. Aluminium is widely used for earth bars in power distribution, switchgear, industrial plants and large installations where weight and cost matter, and where the governing specification permits it.

Sizing

Sizing an aluminium ground bar

Protective conductors are sized with the adiabatic equation used in IEC 60364-5-54 and similar codes: S = I√t / k, where S is the cross-section in mm², I the fault current in amperes, t the disconnection time in seconds, and k a factor that depends on the conductor material and its permitted temperature rise. For aluminium, k is roughly two-thirds of the copper value (for example 95 against 143 for PVC-insulated separate conductors in the IEC table), so the aluminium section must be about 1.5 times larger for the same fault duty.

  1. Find the fault levelTake the prospective earth-fault current at the board and the clearing time of the upstream device.
  2. Apply the adiabatic checkCalculate the minimum section with the aluminium k factor for the insulation and installation conditions in your code.
  3. Allow for holesSubtract the section lost to drilled holes at the narrowest point; this is often what governs a heavily drilled bar.
  4. Check mechanical needsBars over about 600 mm long, or carrying many heavy cables, benefit from 6101-T6 or a thicker section to stay rigid.
  5. Confirm the lugsMake sure the bar width and hole spacing suit the lug sizes, with room for a socket around each nut.

Configurations

Typical ground bar sizes and hole layouts

Wide multi-hole bar

About 100 mm wide and 600 – 800 mm long with roughly 30 – 36 holes in two or three rows. Used as a main earth bar in plant rooms, communication rooms and large distribution boards.

Cabinet bar

About 50 – 75 mm wide and 400 – 600 mm long, 20 – 30 holes. Fits distribution and control cabinets with flexible wiring.

Narrow space-saving bar

About 25 – 40 mm wide and 300 – 500 mm long, 12 – 24 holes. For compact enclosures and mounting rails.

Short equipment bar

About 50 mm wide and 200 – 300 mm long, 8 – 16 holes. Short bonding points between equipment and the cabinet earth.

Terminal-style bar

Narrow bar with tapped holes and clamping screws for many small PE conductors in a row.

Long horizontal earth run

Straight lengths up to several metres along switchgear line-ups, usually 6101-T6 for stiffness, jointed with bolted splice plates.

Hole patterns usually follow the two-hole lug spacing of the conductors being connected (for example 5/8 in or 1 in centres for North American two-hole lugs, or metric spacings for DIN lugs). Send a sketch with hole diameter, pitch and edge distances and we cut and drill to it.

Connections

Making reliable joints on aluminium ground bars

Almost every problem with aluminium grounding comes from poor joints, not from the bar itself. Aluminium forms a hard insulating oxide within minutes of exposure, it creeps under sustained bolt load, and it corrodes galvanically when wet and in contact with copper. Each of these is solved by good practice:

  • Surface preparation: wire-brush or abrade contact areas and apply an oxide-inhibiting joint compound immediately, before bolting
  • Plating: tin-plated aluminium contact faces give stable, low resistance against copper lugs; see tin-plated aluminium bus bar
  • Bimetal transitions: where copper and bare aluminium meet outdoors or in damp rooms, use bimetallic washers or Al-Cu transition pieces
  • Spring washers: Belleville (conical) washers maintain clamping force as aluminium relaxes and through thermal cycles
  • Rated hardware: lugs and connectors marked for aluminium (AL or AL/CU) and torqued to the manufacturer value
  • Inspection: periodic torque checks or thermography on large earth bars in critical installations

Mill-finish aluminium is fine for dry indoor cabinets where joints are prepared properly. Tin plating is the usual upgrade for many-lug bars and humid locations. Anodising is not used on contact areas because the anodic film insulates.

FAQ

Aluminium ground bus bar: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a ground bus bar and a neutral bus bar?

A neutral bar carries return current from loads in normal operation. A ground bar carries current only during a fault and bonds metal parts to earth. They are connected together only at the point required by the earthing system, typically the main service or source.

Should a ground bus bar be copper or aluminum?

Both work. Copper is more compact and is often mandated in telecom specifications. Aluminium is lighter and cheaper for the same fault capacity but needs about 1.5 – 1.6 times the cross-section and properly prepared joints. Follow your project specification and local code.

Which aluminium alloy is best for an earth bar?

1350 or 1060 give the highest conductivity and are ideal for short, well-supported bars. 6101-T6 is slightly less conductive but much stiffer, which suits long or self-supporting bars. 6063 is used where the bar is part of an extruded profile.

Can you drill the holes to our layout?

Yes. We cut aluminium bar to length and drill or tap holes to your drawing, including hole diameter, pitch, row spacing and edge distances. Tin plating can be added after machining.

Can copper lugs be bolted to an aluminium ground bar?

Yes, if the joint is designed for it. Use tin-plated bar or bimetallic washers, AL/CU-rated lugs, joint compound and Belleville washers, and torque to the lug maker value. Keep bare copper-to-aluminium joints out of wet locations.

Do you supply insulators, brackets and lug kits?

No. Metline supplies the aluminium bar, cut, drilled and plated to order. Insulating supports, brackets, fasteners and lugs are sourced by the panel builder or installer to suit their enclosure system.
