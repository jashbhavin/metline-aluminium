Aluminium Condenser Header Pipe

A condenser header pipe (also called a manifold or collector tube) is the clad aluminium tube at each end of a microchannel or parallel-flow condenser that distributes refrigerant into the flat tubes and collects it again. It is normally a high-frequency welded tube made from brazing sheet: a 3003-type core with an Al-Si braze clad such as 4343 or 4045, sometimes with a zinc-bearing 7072 layer for corrosion protection. This guide covers the materials, shapes, sizes, tolerances and brazing points that buyers and heat exchanger engineers need to specify.

Function

What a header pipe does in a condenser

In a brazed aluminium condenser, flat microchannel tubes run in parallel between two header pipes. Hot refrigerant gas enters one header, is distributed across a group of tubes, condenses as it flows through them while air passes over the louvered fins, and is collected in the opposite header. Internal baffles divide the headers into passes, so the refrigerant may cross the core several times before it leaves as liquid.

The header therefore has to do several jobs at once. It must hold the system pressure, which in R134a, R1234yf and especially R410A or R32 systems can be high, with a large safety margin for burst testing. It must accept dozens of tube ends through accurately punched slots and form leak-tight brazed joints at every one of them. It must take baffles, end caps, connection blocks and sometimes an integrated receiver-drier. And it must survive years of road salt, coastal air or industrial pollution on the outside.

All of this is done in one controlled-atmosphere brazing (CAB) cycle at about 600 °C, where the Al-Si clad on the header melts and flows by capillary action into each joint. The header material is therefore a carefully engineered clad product, not a plain aluminium tube.

What the header pipe must deliver

  • Burst and fatigue strength after the brazing cycle has softened the core
  • Enough braze clad, in the right place, to fill every tube-to-header joint
  • Accurate slots and baffle grooves so tubes and partitions fit without gaps
  • A sound, flat weld seam that does not leak or interfere with slotting
  • External corrosion resistance, often helped by a sacrificial zinc layer
Material structure

Core and cladding alloys

Header pipe is usually formed and welded from clad strip. The layers are chosen as a system: the core gives strength, the braze clad provides filler metal, and the optional zinc layer protects the core by corroding first.

LayerTypical alloysRoleNotes
Core3003, 3003 with Cu/Zn modifications, 3005Strength and pressure resistance after brazingMn-bearing 3xxx alloys keep their strength through the braze cycle better than 1xxx; Cu raises the core potential so it is protected by the clad
Braze clad (outside)4343 (about 7.5 % Si), 4045 (about 10 % Si)Melts at roughly 577 – 600 °C to form tube-to-header jointsClad ratio typically about 5 – 15 % of thickness per side
Braze clad (inside, optional)4343, 4045Brazes internal baffles and partitionsDouble-sided clad used when baffles are brazed in place
Sacrificial layer (optional)7072 (about 1 % Zn) or Zn-added cladCorrodes preferentially, protecting the core from pittingZinc diffuses during brazing, creating a protective gradient

Typical structures are described by their layer order, for example 4343/3003 (single-side clad), 4343/3003/4343 (double-side clad) or 4343/3003/7072. Extruded headers in 3003 or 6063 are also used in some designs; these are not clad and receive filler from clad tubes, fins or added braze rings and paste.

Some sources quote 237 W/m·K for aluminium. That is the value for pure aluminium; 3003 core alloy is closer to 160 – 190 W/m·K depending on temper. For headers, conductivity is secondary to pressure strength and brazeability.

Product types

Round, D-shape, square and pre-punched headers

Round header

The most common form. Easy to weld and slot, with good pressure resistance for its weight. Used in automotive and residential HVAC condensers.

D-shape (semi-round)

A flat face for the tube slots and a round back for pressure. The flat face gives shorter, more uniform tube insertion and a slimmer core package.

Square or rectangular

Used where the header must sit flush with frames or where large connection blocks are attached.

Pre-punched (slotted)

Supplied cut to length with tube slots, baffle slots and end features already punched, ready for core assembly.

Reduced and flared ends

Necked, flared or expanded ends for connections and end caps, formed after cutting.

Two-piece and folded headers

Header and tank stamped from brazing sheet rather than welded tube, used in some large or high-pressure designs.

How it is made

How header pipes are made

  1. Clad stripBrazing sheet is roll-bonded with the core and clad layers, rolled to gauge and slit to a precise width for the tube circumference.
  2. FormingA series of roll stands forms the strip into a round tube with the clad on the required side.
  3. High-frequency weldingThe edges are heated by HF current and forged together; the outside bead is scarfed flush. Weld bead width and height are controlled so slots can be punched across or near the seam.
  4. Sizing and shapingSizing rolls bring the tube to final diameter; D-shape and square forms are rolled or drawn from the round tube.
  5. Cutting and end formingFlying saws or cut-off dies cut to length; ends are chamfered, flared or reduced.
  6. SlottingTube slots and baffle slots are punched with progressive or rotary tooling, then the pipe is cleaned and inspected.

Typical quality limits on a good header line include a flush internal weld bead (often within about 0.1 mm), straightness of about 0.5 mm per 500 mm, square cut ends, and a clean, oil-free surface that will not upset flux application or brazing.

Brazing quality

Brazing performance and leak prevention

Most field failures of condenser headers trace back to two sources: leaks at the weld seam, and poor tube-to-header joints. Both are controlled through material and process choices.

ProblemTypical causesPrevention
Weld seam leakBurrs or uneven slit edges, unstable HF power, frequency or squeeze pressure, inclusionsClean slit edges, stable weld parameters, eddy-current testing of the seam
Unfilled tube jointsToo little clad, wrong slot clearance, oxide or oil residue, uneven fluxCorrect clad ratio, slot tolerance matched to tube, clean surfaces and consistent flux load
Blocked microchannelsExcess filler flowing into short-inserted tubesCorrect tube insertion depth and controlled clad thickness
Core erosionToo long or too hot brazing cycle, high Si clad on thin wallFollow the brazing profile, match clad alloy to wall thickness
Collapse or saggingCore too soft after brazing for the header spanUse a stronger 3xxx core or thicker wall

After brazing, condensers are leak-tested (helium or pressure decay) and samples are burst-tested. Corrosion performance is usually assessed with cyclic acidified salt spray testing such as SWAAT (ASTM G85 Annex A3) or neutral salt spray (ASTM B117); specify the test and duration your OEM requires rather than relying on a generic hours figure.

Compared with copper headers in round-tube plate-fin coils, all-aluminium microchannel designs avoid galvanic couples, reduce refrigerant charge and weigh considerably less, because aluminium has roughly one-third the density of copper.

Specification

How to specify and order header pipe

  1. Profile and sizeRound, D-shape or square; OD or width × height, wall thickness and tolerances.
  2. Clad structureCore alloy, clad alloys and side, clad ratio, and whether a zinc layer is required.
  3. Length and featuresCut length or coil, end forms, slot and baffle drawings.
  4. Quality requirementsWeld bead limits, straightness, cleanliness, eddy-current testing and post-braze strength.
  5. Documents and termsEN 10204 3.1 certificate with chemistry, clad ratio and mechanical results; packing and delivery terms (EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP).

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) condenser header pipe: FAQ

What alloy is a condenser header pipe made of?

Usually a 3003 or modified 3xxx core clad with an Al-Si braze alloy (4343 or 4045) on the outside, and sometimes on the inside. A 7072 or zinc-added layer may be included to give sacrificial corrosion protection.

Why is the header pipe clad?

The clad layer melts during controlled-atmosphere brazing at about 600 °C and flows into the joints between the header slots and the microchannel tubes. Without it, filler metal would have to be added separately at every joint.

What is the difference between round and D-shaped headers?

Round headers are simpler and very pressure-efficient. D-shaped headers have a flat face for the tube slots, giving more uniform tube insertion and a more compact core, while the curved back still carries the pressure.

Can aluminium headers replace copper headers?

In microchannel condensers, yes; the whole core is aluminium, which removes galvanic couples and cuts weight and refrigerant charge. In traditional copper-tube coils the header is part of a copper system, so switching requires redesigning the coil.

What causes header pipe weld leaks?

Burrs or uneven slit edges, unstable high-frequency welding parameters and inclusions in the strip. Good edge preparation, stable welding and eddy-current inspection of the seam prevent most leaks.

Do you supply pre-punched header pipes?

Yes. Header pipe can be supplied cut to length with tube and baffle slots and formed ends to your drawing, or as plain tube for in-house slotting.
