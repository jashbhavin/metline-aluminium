Aerospace sandwich structures

Aircraft Honeycomb Core and Sandwich Panels

Aircraft honeycomb is a lightweight cellular core, most often 5052 or 5056 aluminium foil or aramid paper, bonded between thin skins to make sandwich panels that are very stiff for their weight. It is used in floors, interiors, control surfaces, fairings, engine nacelles and secondary structure. This guide explains the core materials, how core is specified, which facings suit which parts, and the tests and standards that govern aerospace use.

Overview

Why aircraft use honeycomb sandwich structures

A sandwich panel works like an I-beam. The two skins carry bending loads in tension and compression, and the core holds them apart and carries shear. Doubling the core thickness of a panel roughly increases its bending stiffness about seven-fold while adding only a few per cent to its weight, which is why honeycomb is everywhere in aircraft that must save every kilogram.

Honeycomb is the most efficient core geometry because its thin cell walls run perpendicular to the skins. They resist crushing and shear far better than foam of the same density, and the open cells keep weight very low: typical aerospace cores weigh 30 – 130 kg/m³, compared with 2,700 kg/m³ for solid aluminium. Aluminium honeycomb also absorbs energy predictably as it crushes, which is used in crash-energy absorbers and landing-gear components.

Sandwich panels have been used on aircraft since the 1940s. Today they are found in cabin floors, sidewalls and ceilings, galleys and lavatories, overhead bins, cargo liners, trailing-edge flaps and spoilers, rudders and elevators, wing-to-body fairings, radomes, helicopter rotor blades and engine nacelle acoustic liners.

Aluminium core gives the highest shear strength and stiffness per unit weight and conducts heat well, so it dominates structural panels. Aramid core is preferred inside the cabin because it does not corrode, is less prone to permanent denting, and is used in panels that must pass the strict fire, smoke and toxicity requirements. Many interior panels pair aramid core with glass or carbon facings, while floor panels in aircraft and helicopters often pair aluminium core with aluminium or composite skins.

Aluminium core for flight use is not the same as commercial core

  • Flight-grade core is made from 5052 or 5056 foil to AMS-C-7438 (formerly MIL-C-7438) with lot traceability
  • The foil receives a corrosion-resistant surface treatment so the bond line survives humidity and salt exposure
  • Each lot is tested for density, compressive strength and shear strength in both ribbon (L) and transverse (W) directions
  • Commercial 3003 core is excellent for building and rail interiors but is not a substitute on flight hardware
How core is specified

Reading an aluminium honeycomb core callout

Aerospace core is described by a short designation that gives cell size, alloy, foil thickness and density. For example, 1/8-5052-.001 4.5 means 1/8 inch (3.2 mm) cells, 5052 alloy foil 0.001 inch (25 µm) thick, giving a nominal density of 4.5 lb/ft³ (about 72 kg/m³).

As an indication of scale, one manufacturer publishes typical values for 1/8-inch, 3.1 lb/ft³ 5056 core of about 2.4 MPa stabilised compressive strength, 1.8 MPa shear in the L direction and 1.1 MPa in the W direction at room temperature. These are typical figures, not design minimums. Design values come from AMS-C-7438 or the qualified supplier specification for the exact core you buy, and core properties drop at elevated temperature.

Facing materials

Facing (skin) materials for aircraft panels

2024-T3 Alclad sheet

Damage-tolerant aluminium skin with a pure-aluminium cladding for corrosion protection. Common on bonded metal panels and floor skins.

7075-T6 sheet

Higher strength than 2024 for skins that see high bending or point loads, such as heavy-duty floor areas.

6061-T6 and 5052-H32 sheet

Lower cost, good corrosion resistance and formability; used for ground-support, interior and less critical panels.

Carbon fibre (CFRP)

Very stiff and light; widely used on modern interior panels, floors and control surfaces with aramid or aluminium core.

Glass fibre (FRP)

Low cost and electrically transparent; the standard interior skin with phenolic resin for fire performance.

Titanium and stainless steel

For hot zones near engines and exhausts, usually with metallic core brazed or bonded to the skins.

Metal facings are bonded to aluminium core with structural film adhesives, typically modified epoxies cured at 120 °C or 175 °C, after the skins are prepared by phosphoric acid anodising or a similar treatment that gives a durable bond. Mixing aluminium core with carbon skins needs a glass-fibre or adhesive isolation layer to prevent galvanic corrosion.

A typical bonded floor panel has two thin aluminium or carbon skins, a 10 mm core, and local high-density inserts or potted areas where seat tracks, fasteners and fittings pass through. Because inserts concentrate load, panel designers specify potting compound, insert type and edge closeouts as carefully as the core itself.

Aircraft programmes add their own material and process specifications on top of these, and panels for flight use are made only from qualified materials by approved processes. When ordering core for a qualified part, quote the airframe or customer specification as well as AMS-C-7438, and ask for the certificate of conformance with lot test results.

Design and processing

Design and processing points for honeycomb panels

  • Orient the ribbon: align the stronger L direction with the main shear load path
  • Check skin dimpling and wrinkling: thin skins over large cells buckle into the cells; choose a smaller cell for skins under about 0.5 mm
  • Seal the edges: moisture entering aluminium core through damaged edges or fasteners causes corrosion and freeze-thaw damage; use edge fillers and sealant
  • Use perforated core with care: it vents cure volatiles but also lets water travel through the panel if the skin is breached
  • Machine core correctly: use high-speed routers, freeze or wax-fill thin slices, and avoid crushing cell walls at chamfers
  • Plan inspections: tap testing, ultrasonic and thermographic inspection detect disbonds; design access accordingly
  • Isolate dissimilar metals: keep carbon fibre and steel fasteners away from bare aluminium core and skins

FAQ

Aircraft honeycomb core: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium is used for aircraft honeycomb core?

5052 is the standard aerospace core alloy. 5056 contains more magnesium and gives higher strength at the same density, so it is chosen for more highly loaded panels. 3003 core is a commercial grade for non-flight uses.

What does 1/8-5052-.001 4.5 mean?

It describes 1/8 inch cells made from 5052 foil 0.001 inch thick, giving a nominal core density of 4.5 lb/ft³ (about 72 kg/m³).

Is aluminum honeycomb or aramid honeycomb better for aircraft?

Aluminium core is stronger and stiffer for its weight and suits structural panels. Aramid core is tougher, does not corrode, resists permanent denting and is the usual choice for cabin interiors and radomes.

What specification covers aerospace aluminium honeycomb?

AMS-C-7438 (formerly MIL-C-7438) covers 5052 and 5056 aluminium core for sandwich construction. Aircraft programmes usually add their own material specifications.

What is the L and W direction in honeycomb?

L is the ribbon direction, along the continuous foil sheets; W is perpendicular to it. Core is stronger and stiffer in shear in the L direction, so it is aligned with the main load path.

Can Metline supply facing sheet as well as core?

Yes. We supply aluminium honeycomb core and aluminium facing sheet such as 2024, 7075, 6061 and 5052, with mill certificates, to panel makers worldwide.
