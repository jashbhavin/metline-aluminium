Aluminium Extruded Profiles for Bridges

Aluminium bridges are built mainly from 6xxx-series extrusions such as 6005A-T6, 6082-T6 and 6061-T6, with 7020-T6 used where higher strength is needed and 5083 plate for welded decks. This guide explains which alloy suits each bridge member, the minimum properties you can design to, the codes that govern the work and what to specify when you buy the profiles.

Overview

Why engineers choose aluminium for bridges

An aluminium bridge weighs roughly one third of an equivalent steel structure and does not need a paint system to survive outdoors. Those two facts drive almost every aluminium bridge project: the span can be prefabricated, lifted in one piece with a light crane and installed overnight, and the owner avoids decades of repainting.

Aluminium has been used in bridges since the 1930s. The Arvida Bridge over the Saguenay River in Quebec, opened in 1950 with a main arch of about 88 m, was built entirely of aluminium and is still in service after more than seventy years. Today the most common applications are footbridges and cycle bridges, lightweight deck replacements on older steel or movable bridges, military and emergency bridges, pontoon and marina gangways, and secondary parts such as parapets, railings and sign gantries.

Extrusion is what makes aluminium economic for bridges. A die can produce a hollow deck plank with integral stiffeners, interlocking edges and a non-slip top surface in one piece, so the designer places material exactly where it carries load. Steel cannot be shaped this way without extensive welding.

Where aluminium bridge profiles make most sense

  • Spans where self-weight dominates, such as movable, bascule and lift bridges
  • Sites with limited crane capacity or access, where the whole span must be lifted at once
  • Corrosive or coastal environments where steel would need frequent maintenance
  • Deck replacement on existing bridges, where a lighter deck raises the live-load rating
  • Temporary, military and disaster-relief bridges that must be carried and assembled by hand
Alloy selection

Which alloys are used for bridge extrusions?

Bridge profiles come almost entirely from the 6xxx (Al-Mg-Si) family, because these alloys extrude well into complex hollow shapes, reach useful strength after ageing and weld with standard MIG procedures. The choice between them is about section shape, strength and how the member is joined.

Alloy & temperTypical bridge useWhy it is chosen

Other 7xxx extrusion alloys such as 7003, 7005 and 7021 appear in some lightweight portable and military bridge designs. They offer more strength but are more sensitive to stress corrosion cracking and to the welding procedure, so they should only be used where the designer specifies the temper, the heat treatment and the corrosion testing explicitly.

Applications

Where extrusions are used in a bridge structure

Deck planks

Multi-void hollow extrusions, typically 200 – 400 mm wide, that interlock side by side and are welded by friction stir or MIG welding or simply clipped and bolted. The top face often carries an extruded ribbed or epoxy-grit anti-slip surface.

Main girders and chords

I-sections, box sections and large hollow tubes that form the primary load path of girder and truss bridges. Long lengths reduce the number of splices.

Truss diagonals and verticals

Round, square or rectangular hollow sections bolted or welded to the chords. Standard tube sizes keep the cost down.

Cross-girders and stringers

Channels, I-beams and hat sections that carry the deck between the main girders.

Parapets and railings

Posts, top rails, infill bars and balusters, often in 6063 or 6005A with an anodised or powder-coated finish.

Ancillary structures

Lighting columns, sign gantries, cable trays, inspection walkways and maintenance platforms fitted to steel and concrete bridges.

A typical modern footbridge combines two truss or box-girder sides in 6082-T6 with a deck of hollow 6005A-T6 planks spanning between cross-girders. Because the deck plank and the truss can both be extruded to the drawing, the fabricator mostly cuts, drills and joins standard lengths rather than building members up from plate.

Large road and rail bridges with long spans are still mainly steel and concrete. Aluminium competes best where its low weight brings a clear system saving, which is why decks and footbridges dominate.

Engineering

Design considerations for aluminium bridge profiles

Aluminium is not a drop-in substitute for steel. Its lower stiffness, sensitivity to welding heat and fatigue behaviour must be taken into account from the concept stage.

Stiffness and deflection

With about one third of the elastic modulus of steel, an aluminium girder deflects three times as much under the same load if the section is unchanged. Deeper sections, closed boxes and trusses are used to control deflection, which often governs the design more than strength.

Vibration and comfort

A light, flexible footbridge can have natural frequencies in the range excited by walking (about 1.6 – 2.4 Hz vertically). Designers check footfall response to EN 1990 Annex A2, ISO 10137 or national guidance and add depth or damping where needed.

Welding and the HAZ

Welding softens 6xxx alloys in a zone around the weld. Eurocode 9 and the Aluminum Design Manual apply reduced strength in this heat-affected zone, so welds are placed in low-stress areas or replaced by bolted, riveted or friction-stir-welded joints.

Fatigue

Fatigue strength of welded details is lower than in steel and controls many deck designs under traffic. EN 1999-1-3 gives detail categories; smooth extruded shapes and friction stir welds improve fatigue performance.

Thermal movement

Aluminium expands about twice as much as steel. Bearings and expansion joints must accommodate this, and mixed aluminium-steel structures need slotted connections.

Galvanic contact

Isolate aluminium from carbon steel, copper and wet concrete with gaskets, sleeves and coatings, and use stainless steel or coated fasteners.

Procurement

What to specify when buying bridge extrusions

  1. Alloy, temper and standardState, for example, "EN AW-6005A T6 to EN 755-2" or "6061-T6 to ASTM B221". Do not leave the temper open.
  2. Section drawing and tolerancesProvide a dimensioned drawing with critical dimensions marked and the tolerance standard (EN 755-9 or EN 12020-2, or ANSI H35.2). Note any tighter limits on twist and straightness for long deck planks.
  3. Length and quantityLong, cut-to-length profiles reduce splices. Confirm the maximum length you can transport and handle on site.
  4. Surface finishMill finish is common for structural members; anodising (typically 15 – 25 µm for outdoor use) or powder coating for visible parts and railings.
  5. Testing and documentsEN 10204 3.1 certificate with chemistry and tensile results per lot, plus any extra tests the execution class demands, such as hardness, dimensional reports or third-party inspection.
  6. Packing and deliveryInterleaved and protected profiles in wooden crates or bundles, with delivery terms (EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP) agreed for the destination.

Common mistakes

  • Designing to typical strength values rather than EN 755-2 or ASTM B221 minimums
  • Ignoring HAZ strength loss at welded connections
  • Checking strength but not deflection and footfall vibration
  • Bolting aluminium directly to galvanised or carbon steel without isolation
  • Leaving the twist tolerance open on long, wide deck planks

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) bridge extrusions: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium alloy is best for bridge decks?

6005A-T6 is the most common choice for hollow extruded deck planks because it extrudes thin multi-void sections well at good strength. 6082-T6 is used for heavier sections and 6061-T6 is common in North American practice. Welded plate decks use 5083.

How long does an aluminium bridge last?

Well-detailed aluminium bridges have lasted many decades with little maintenance; the Arvida Bridge in Canada opened in 1950 and is still carrying traffic. Design life depends mainly on fatigue detailing, drainage and avoiding galvanic contact with other metals.

Is an aluminium bridge strong enough for vehicles?

Yes. Aluminium decks and complete aluminium bridges carry road traffic, but the design is usually governed by deflection and fatigue rather than strength, so sections are deeper than in steel.

Can aluminium bridge profiles be welded?

Yes, by MIG, TIG or friction stir welding. Welding reduces strength in the heat-affected zone, which codes such as Eurocode 9 and the Aluminum Design Manual account for with reduced design values.

What certificates should come with bridge extrusions?

At minimum an EN 10204 3.1 mill test certificate showing chemistry and tensile results for each lot, traceable to the profiles delivered. The fabricator's execution class or project specification may require further tests or third-party inspection.

Does an aluminium bridge need painting?

Not for corrosion protection in most environments. Structural members are often left mill finish, while railings and visible parts are anodised or powder coated for appearance.
