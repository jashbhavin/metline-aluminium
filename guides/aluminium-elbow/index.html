Aluminium Elbows and Tube Bends

An aluminium elbow changes the direction of a pipe or tube run, most often by 45°, 90° or 180°. Industrial butt-weld elbows are made to ASTM B361 with dimensions from ASME B16.9, while mandrel-bent tube elbows serve automotive, HVAC and compressed-air systems. This guide explains the types, alloys, radii, sizes and inspection points so you can specify the right elbow first time.

Overview

What is an aluminium elbow?

An elbow is the fitting that turns a pipeline. Every change of direction in a process line, a compressed-air ring main, a cryogenic transfer line, a ship’s piping system or a turbocharger intake needs one. In aluminium systems the elbow must match the pipe in alloy, wall thickness and end preparation, or it becomes the weak point of the line.

Two families of product share the name. Pipe elbows are standardised fittings sized by nominal pipe size (NPS or DN) and schedule, with fixed centre-to-end dimensions, and are welded, threaded or flanged into industrial piping. Tube bends (often called mandrel-bent elbows) are formed from tube sized by actual outside diameter and wall thickness, with a radius chosen by the designer, and are common in vehicles, HVAC, food processing and architecture.

Aluminium is chosen for elbows where the whole system benefits from low weight (about one third of the weight of steel), resistance to atmospheric and many chemical environments, non-sparking behaviour, good toughness at cryogenic temperatures and high thermal conductivity. Metline supplies aluminium pipe, tube, bar and forgings for elbow production, and finished elbows and bends to drawing, to buyers worldwide.

Specify these five things for any elbow

  • Angle: 45°, 90°, 180° or a custom angle
  • Size: NPS/DN and schedule for pipe, or OD × wall for tube
  • Radius: long radius (1.5D), short radius (1D) or a stated centreline radius
  • Alloy and temper, matched to the pipe and to the service
  • End type: butt-weld bevel, plain end, socket, thread, flare or flange
Elbow types

Types of aluminium elbows

Long-radius (LR) elbow

Centreline radius 1.5 × NPS. The default choice for process piping because it gives lower pressure drop and gentler flow than a short radius elbow.

Short-radius (SR) elbow

Centreline radius 1.0 × NPS. Used where space is tight; higher pressure drop and, under pressure, higher stress at the inner wall, so check the pressure rating.

45° elbow

Turns the line by half a right angle. Two 45s with a short straight between them give a softer 90° turn for slurries or high-velocity flow.

180° return bend

Reverses the flow direction. Used in heat-exchanger coils, heaters and serpentine lines; available in long and short radius.

Reducing elbow

Changes direction and pipe size in one fitting, saving a separate reducer and a weld.

Mandrel-bent tube elbow

Bent from tube over an internal mandrel to keep the bore round. Any angle and radius from about 1D upward; used in intake, intercooler and exhaust ducting and in HVAC.

Segmented (mitred) elbow

Fabricated from cut and welded pipe segments. Economical for large-diameter, low-pressure ducts and pipes where a formed elbow is not practical.

Socket and threaded elbows

Small-bore fittings for quick assembly. Threaded aluminium joints suit low-pressure air and fluid lines; thin-wall pipe cannot be threaded without losing too much wall.

Flanged and push-fit elbows

Flanged elbows bolt into a line for easy removal. Push-fit elbows with internal seals are the norm in modular aluminium compressed-air systems.

Manufacturing

How aluminium elbows are made

The manufacturing route affects wall thickness distribution, grain flow, cost and the sizes that can be produced. ASTM B361 allows the fittings to be made from rod, bar, forgings, sheet, plate, seamless pipe or seamless tube, provided the alloy and temper match the order.

After forming, heat-treatable alloys such as 6061 and 6063 are usually solution heat-treated and aged back to T6, because hot forming leaves the metal in an uncontrolled condition. Non-heat-treatable alloys such as 3003 and 5083 are supplied annealed (O) or in the as-fabricated (H112) condition. Ends are then machined to the required bevel, normally 37.5° for butt welding.

Material selection

Choosing the alloy for an aluminium elbow

ASTM B361 designates fittings with a WP prefix followed by the alloy and temper, for example WP6061-T6 or WP5083-O. The alloy should normally be the same as, or weld-compatible with, the pipe it joins.

Typical properties above are Aluminum Association values; the order standard sets the guaranteed minimums. Remember that welding softens heat-treated alloys: the heat-affected zone of 6061-T6 drops to roughly 165 MPa tensile strength, so piping codes such as ASME B31.3 use reduced allowable stresses for welded 6061 unless the assembly is re-heat-treated. 5083 loses far less strength on welding, which is one reason it is preferred for marine and cryogenic lines.

Sizes

Aluminium butt-weld elbow dimensions (ASME B16.9)

Aluminium butt-weld elbows use the same centre-to-end dimensions as steel fittings, so they fit the same piping layouts. The pipe outside diameter follows ASME B36.10, and aluminium pipe wall thickness is normally ordered by schedule (5, 10, 40 or 80) to ASTM B241 or B241M. ASME B16.9 also requires that the wall thickness at any point of the fitting is at least 87.5 % of the nominal wall of the pipe it matches. Because forming stretches the outer wall (the extrados) and thickens the inner wall (the intrados), manufacturers start with heavier pipe or control the process to stay within that limit. Ask for wall-thickness measurements at the extrados on critical orders.

Inspection

Quality checks for aluminium elbows

A good elbow is round, has the right wall thickness all the way round the bend, and is free of wrinkles and cracks. The following checks are standard for industrial orders:

  • Ovality: the difference between maximum and minimum outside diameter at the bend, kept within the B16.9 out-of-round limit for pipe fittings or the drawing for tube bends
  • Wall thickness: measured ultrasonically at the extrados, intrados and flanks; minimum 87.5 % of nominal for B16.9 fittings
  • Angle and centre-to-end: checked with fixtures or a coordinate measuring arm against tolerance tables
  • End bevel and squareness: correct bevel angle and root face for welding; ends square to the axis
  • Surface: no wrinkles on the intrados, no cracks or tool marks on the extrados; dye-penetrant testing where required
  • Mechanical properties: tensile or hardness testing to confirm temper after heat treatment
  • Pressure test: hydrostatic or pneumatic testing of assemblies when the order or code requires it
  • Certificates: EN 10204 3.1 mill test certificate showing chemistry, mechanical properties and heat treatment

Common mistakes when buying aluminium elbows

  • Using a short-radius elbow in a high-velocity or erosive line where a long radius would last longer
  • Matching OD but not schedule, leaving a step at the weld that disturbs flow and concentrates stress
  • Ordering 7075 or 2024 elbows for a welded line; these alloys are not suitable for fusion welding
  • Forgetting that a welded 6061 line is designed to the as-welded strength, not T6
  • Mixing tube OD sizes (e.g. 2 in tube) with pipe NPS sizes (2 in NPS is 60.3 mm OD)

Aluminium is not suited to every service. Strong alkalis, mercury and some chlorinated solvents attack it, and its allowable stress falls above about 150 – 200 °C. For hot, high-pressure steam lines, steel or stainless elbows remain the right choice. Where aluminium piping meets steel equipment, use an insulating flange kit or a bimetallic transition joint to prevent galvanic corrosion.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) elbows: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a long-radius and a short-radius elbow?

A long-radius elbow has a centreline radius of 1.5 times the nominal pipe size; a short-radius elbow has 1.0 times. Long radius gives lower pressure drop and lower wall stress and is the default; short radius saves space where the layout is tight.

What is centreline radius (CLR) and why does it matter?

CLR is the distance from the centre of the bend arc to the centre line of the tube. A smaller CLR makes a more compact bend but thins the outer wall more, increases ovality and pressure loss, and needs a mandrel to avoid wrinkling. Choose the largest CLR the space allows.

Which standard covers aluminium butt-weld elbows?

ASTM B361 covers the material, manufacture and testing of factory-made wrought aluminium welding fittings, and refers to ASME B16.9 (and B16.11 for socket-weld fittings) for dimensions. Older documents cite ASME B16.28 for short-radius elbows; that standard was withdrawn and its content moved into B16.9.

Can aluminum elbows be welded to steel pipe?

Not directly. Aluminium and steel cannot be fusion welded together. Use a flanged joint with an insulating gasket kit, or a bimetallic transition joint made by explosion welding, then weld aluminium to aluminium on one side and steel to steel on the other.

Are seamless or welded elbows better?

Seamless elbows formed from seamless pipe avoid a longitudinal weld and are preferred for pressure and cyclic service. Welded elbows made from plate are economical in large diameters where seamless pipe is not available; their seams should be inspected as the piping code requires.

What finish can aluminium elbows have?

Mill finish is normal for process piping. Bends for architecture and vehicles can be polished, anodised or powder coated. Galvanising, which applies to steel, is not used on aluminium.
