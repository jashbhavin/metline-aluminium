Copper Clad Aluminium Bus Bar

A copper clad aluminium (CCA) bus bar is a rectangular aluminium conductor fully sheathed in a bonded copper layer, typically 20 – 30 % copper by volume. It bolts, plates and terminates like a copper bar while weighing well under half as much size for size. Metline supplies CCA busbar to ASTM B1005 for switchgear, busway, earthing and battery systems worldwide, with test certificates for bond, conductivity and dimensions.

Overview

What a CCA bus bar is

In a CCA bus bar the copper wraps the whole perimeter of the aluminium core, including the edges, and is bonded to it along its full length. This is different from a plated aluminium bar, where a few microns of tin or copper sit on the surface, and from a roll-bonded plate, where only the faces are clad. Because the sheath is continuous, every surface the installer touches, drills near or bolts against is copper.

The bar is usually produced by casting or cladding aluminium inside a copper shell and then rolling or drawing the composite to its final rectangular section. Annealing after working builds the metallurgical bond and sets the temper. The copper fraction is fixed by the starting geometry and stays the same through all later reductions, so a 25 % bar is 25 % copper at any size.

The aim is a busbar that can drop into a copper design with minimal change to terminals, hardware and assembly practice, while removing a large part of the copper weight and cost. It is an established alternative to copper in low-voltage switchgear, distribution boards, busway, earthing systems, data-centre power distribution and battery energy storage.

Benefits in practice

  • Copper-to-copper bolted joints with standard hardware, plating and torque procedures
  • Roughly 44 – 51 % of the weight of a copper bar of the same dimensions (20 – 30 % copper)
  • Easier handling and lighter supporting structures in large boards and busway runs
  • Material cost linked mostly to aluminium, reducing exposure to copper price swings

ASTM B1005 defines the bar by copper volume and temper class and sets requirements for tensile and bend properties, interface bonding, density, resistivity, dimensional tolerances, inspection and packaging. Order to the standard and the certificate will report each of these against its limits.

Two practical points come out of the numbers. Size for size, a CCA bar has about 1.4 – 1.45 times the resistance of copper, so for the same current and temperature rise the section must normally be enlarged, either by a wider bar or an extra bar in parallel. Even after that enlargement, a 25 % CCA bar weighs only about two thirds of the copper it replaces. Annealed bar conducts slightly better than hard bar of the same ratio.

Fabrication

Cutting, punching, bending and jointing CCA bus bar

Cutting

Shear or saw like copper. The cut face exposes aluminium, so keep cut ends away from damp zones or seal them with lacquer, heat-shrink or a plated cap.

Punching and drilling

Standard copper tooling works. Hole walls show aluminium but carry little current; the joint current passes through the copper faces under the washer.

Bending

Use annealed (A) class for flat-wise and edgewise bends. A generous inside radius, typically at least one to two times thickness flat-wise, protects the copper layer from thinning or cracking.

Bolted joints

Prepare and torque joints as for copper. Use disc-spring washers to maintain contact pressure, since the aluminium core expands more and creeps more than copper.

Plating

Tin, nickel and silver plate onto the copper surface by normal processes. Tinning is common for humid or polluted sites.

Heat

Avoid brazing, welding and long exposure to high temperature. Heat grows brittle intermetallics at the interface; mechanical or friction-welded terminations are preferred.

Insulation methods used on copper bar, such as epoxy powder coating and heat-shrink sleeving, apply to CCA unchanged. Because the bar is lighter, supports and insulators can sometimes be reduced, but short-circuit forces depend on current and spacing, not bar weight, so bracing must still be checked.

Quality

Inspection and testing

  • Copper ratio: measured on a polished cross-section; sheath thickness checked on faces and edges.
  • Bond integrity: bend, twist or peel tests to the order specification; no separation at the interface.
  • Resistivity: DC resistance of a measured length, corrected to 20 °C, against the class minimum.
  • Mechanical properties: tensile strength and elongation for the A or H class.
  • Dimensions and edges: thickness, width, edge radius, straightness and twist.
  • Surface: free of cracks, laps, blisters and exposed aluminium on faces and edges.
  • Documents: EN 10204 3.1 certificate, packing list and, on request, third-party inspection.

FAQ

Copper clad aluminium (aluminum) bus bar: FAQ

What standard covers copper clad aluminum bus bar?

ASTM B1005 covers copper-clad aluminium bar for electrical purposes. It defines 20, 25 and 30 % copper classes in annealed (A) and hard (H) conditions and sets requirements for bonding, resistivity, mechanical properties and tolerances.

How does CCA busbar compare with copper busbar?

Size for size, a 25 % CCA bar weighs about 48 % as much as copper and has about 1.4 times the DC resistance. For equal resistance the CCA section is enlarged by about 40 %, and it then weighs about two thirds of the copper bar.

Can CCA busbar be bent?

Yes. Order annealed (A) class for bending and use an inside radius of at least one to two times the thickness for flat-wise bends. Hard (H) class is intended for straight runs.

Do the drilled holes expose aluminium?

The hole wall shows aluminium, but the joint current flows through the copper faces clamped under the bolt and washer. Use disc-spring washers and, in damp locations, a tinned surface and sealed cut ends.

Can CCA busbar be welded or brazed?

It is not recommended. High temperatures grow brittle copper-aluminium intermetallics at the interface. Use bolted, clamped or friction-welded terminations.

Which copper ratio should I choose?

25 % is a common balance. Choose 30 % where joints are numerous or the bar runs hot, and 20 % where weight and cost are the priority and the section can be made larger.
