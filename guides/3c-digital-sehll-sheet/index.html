Electronics alloy selection

Aluminium Sheet and Strip for 3C Electronics Housings

"3C" covers computers, communication devices and consumer electronics, and aluminium is the standard housing material for many of them because it is light, stiff, conducts heat and takes a premium anodised finish. The right alloy depends on the part: bright-anodising 5252 and 5052 for stamped covers, 6xxx grades for CNC-machined shells and 7xxx grades where frames must be thin and strong. This guide maps alloys to parts.

Overview

What electronics makers need from aluminium

A device housing is judged first by how it looks and feels, so electronics-grade aluminium is bought as much for surface and anodising response as for strength.

Four requirements drive alloy choice. Manufacturing route: stamped and drawn parts need formable non-heat-treatable sheet, while unibody shells and frames are CNC-machined from plate or thick sheet. Strength and stiffness: thinner devices need stronger alloys so frames do not bend in a pocket. Finish: decorative anodising shows every inclusion, streak and grain variation, so low-iron, fine-grained material is essential. Thermal management: housings increasingly double as heat spreaders, which favours high-conductivity grades.

Surface quality

Getting a flawless anodised finish

  1. Specify anodising qualityOrder "anodise quality" or "decorative anodising" material, not general engineering sheet. It is cast and rolled with tighter control of inclusions and grain size.
  2. Control iron and siliconLow Fe and Si (as in 5252) reduce cloudy, grey anodic films and keep colours bright.
  3. Ask about grain and streaksCoarse grains and rolling streaks appear as bands after etching and anodising. Agree a sample-anodising test on each lot.
  4. Protect the surfaceLaser-cuttable PE film (transparent, blue or black and white) prevents scratches during stamping and CNC.
  5. Keep one source per programmeMixing material from different casts can change colour shade across a production run.

Avoid

Common mistakes when specifying housing material

  • Ordering standard 6061 plate for a cosmetic part and finding stripes or blotches after anodising
  • Choosing 7075 for appearance parts without testing the anodised colour, which can look yellowish
  • Specifying a hard temper for a deep-drawn cover, leading to cracking at corners
  • Ignoring flatness on thin strip, which shows as waviness on large back panels
  • Forgetting that the housing is a heat path: a low-conductivity alloy can raise chip temperature

FAQ

Aluminium for electronics housings: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium alloy is used for laptop housings?

Machined unibody shells are commonly 6061-T6 or 6063-T6; stamped lids and keyboard plates are often 5052-H32. Thin, high-end designs may use stronger 6013 or 7xxx alloys.

What is 5252 aluminium used for?

Bright-anodised stamped parts such as phone back covers and all-in-one panels. Its high-purity base gives a clearer, brighter anodic finish than 5052.

Why do phone frames use 7xxx alloys?

Thin frames must resist bending. 7xxx alloys such as 7075 and 7021 offer roughly twice the strength of 6061, allowing slimmer sections.

Does anodising change thermal performance?

The thin anodic layer has little effect on heat spreading through the metal and increases surface emissivity, which helps radiate heat.

What does "3C" mean?

Computers, communications and consumer electronics: phones, laptops, tablets, routers, cameras, TVs and wearables.

Can you supply film-protected, cut-to-size sheet?

Yes. Sheet and strip can be supplied slit or cut to size with PE protective film and export packing.
