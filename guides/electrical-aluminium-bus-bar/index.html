Electrical Aluminium Busbar: Selection and Sizing Guide

An electrical aluminium busbar is a solid flat, round or shaped conductor that collects and distributes current inside switchgear, panels, transformers, bus ducts and battery systems. Electrical-grade aluminium carries the same current as copper at roughly half the weight once the cross-section is enlarged by about 60 %, which is why 1350 and 6101 bars are now standard in power distribution. This guide explains how to choose the alloy and temper, size the bar, make reliable joints and order it correctly.

Overview

What an aluminium busbar does and why aluminium is used

A busbar replaces bundles of cable wherever large currents must be collected from several sources or split between several loads. Because it is rigid, it also acts as a structural member: it must resist the magnetic forces of a short circuit, hold its shape between insulators and keep bolted joints tight for decades.

Aluminium became a mainstream busbar material for three practical reasons. First, its conductivity per kilogram is about twice that of copper, so a bar sized for the same resistance weighs about half as much. Second, the larger cross-section gives a bigger surface area, which helps the bar shed heat and often allows aluminium to run at the same current with a similar or lower temperature rise. Third, aluminium is far cheaper per ampere and its price is less volatile, which matters on projects where busbar is a large share of the material bill.

The trade-offs are well understood: aluminium forms a hard surface oxide that must be managed at joints, it expands about a third more than copper with temperature, and pure aluminium is soft enough to creep under bolt pressure. Each of these is solved by grade selection and joint design, covered below. Busbar manufacturers for switchgear, bus duct, transformers, rectifiers, smelter potlines and energy storage all use aluminium routinely.

Quick selection rules

  • Highest conductivity, low mechanical load, easy bending: 1350 (EC grade) or 1060/1070
  • Bars that span between supports, carry bolt loads or must resist creep: 6101 in T6, T61, T63, T64 or T65
  • Profiles with complex shapes or a decorative finish where conductivity is secondary: 6063 or 6060
  • Structural parts that also conduct, such as earthing frames: 6061 or 6082

Within 6101, the temper decides the balance. T6 gives the highest strength with the lowest conductivity of the group (55 % IACS minimum). Over-aged tempers such as T61 and T64 raise conductivity towards 59 – 60 % IACS and make the bar easier to bend, at the cost of strength; T63 and T65 sit in between. For most switchgear bars T6 or T61 is the default, while T64 suits bars that are formed into tight offsets.

ASTM B317/B317M sets the minimum tensile, yield and conductivity values for each 6101 temper. Because published secondary sources disagree on some temper values, design to the edition of the standard named in your contract and to the certificate.

Two consequences follow. For the same DC resistance an aluminium bar needs about 1.64 times the copper cross-section (100 ÷ 61) in 1350, or about 1.82 times in 6101-T6. Multiply that by the density ratio (2.70 ÷ 8.89) and the aluminium bar weighs roughly 50 – 55 % of the copper bar. The higher expansion means long runs need expansion joints or flexible links, and bolted joints need spring washers so clamping force is maintained as the bar heats and cools.

Current rating

How to size an aluminium busbar for current

Ampacity is not a property of the bar alone. It is the current at which the bar reaches its permitted temperature in a given installation, so it depends on ambient temperature, the allowed temperature rise, whether the bar is painted or bare, how bars are spaced and oriented, whether they sit in a ventilated or closed enclosure, and AC effects. Published tables are therefore starting points, not guarantees.

  1. Fix the limitsTake the maximum ambient and the permitted temperature rise from the product standard. In assemblies to IEC 61439-1, the rise is limited by the materials and insulation the bar touches, and terminals for external cables are limited to 70 K rise.
  2. Pick a starting sizeUse a recognised rating table, such as DIN 43670 for aluminium busbars or the Aluminum Association and Copper Development Association conversion charts, for your arrangement and rise.
  3. Convert from copper if neededWhen replacing a copper bar, start with about 1.6× the copper area in 1350 or 1.8× in 6101-T6, then check the geometry: increasing width usually cools better than increasing thickness.
  4. Correct for AC and groupingFor AC, several thin laminations spaced apart beat one thick bar, because skin effect leaves the centre of thick bars underused. Apply derating for bars stacked close together or in closed ducts.
  5. Check short-circuit withstandVerify the bar and supports withstand the peak electromagnetic force and the thermal energy of the fault; this is where 6101 strength becomes important.
  6. Confirm by test or calculationType testing or verified calculation to the assembly standard is the final proof of rating.

Common sizing mistakes

  • Copying a copper bar size one-for-one into aluminium
  • Using a single very thick bar for high AC current instead of spaced laminations
  • Ignoring the higher resistance of 6101 compared with 1350 when converting a table
  • Rating the bar but not the joints, which usually run hottest
Connections

Making reliable joints and choosing a surface finish

Almost every aluminium busbar failure starts at a joint. Aluminium oxide forms within seconds of exposure and is a good insulator, so contact surfaces must be prepared, protected and kept under steady pressure.

Surface preparation

Clean contact areas and abrade them with a stainless-steel wire brush or abrasive pad, then immediately coat with an oxide-inhibiting joint compound before assembly.

Spring washers

Use conical spring (Belleville) washers and thick flat washers under the nut so clamp force survives thermal cycling and the slow creep of the aluminium.

Contact area and bolts

Overlap length is usually set so the joint area is a multiple of the bar width; follow the switchgear maker's bolt pattern and torque values rather than generic tables.

Tin plating

A thin tin layer gives a stable, low-resistance contact surface, resists oxidation in humid air and is the usual finish where aluminium meets copper terminals.

Other platings

Nickel and silver plating on aluminium are used for high-cycle contacts or high-temperature joints; they can be arranged for specific projects.

Insulation

Epoxy powder coating, heat-shrink sleeves and insulating films allow closer spacing and protect against accidental contact; check the coating temperature class.

Where aluminium bars must connect to copper, avoid bare metal-to-metal contact in damp conditions, because the two metals form a galvanic couple and the aluminium corrodes. Use tin-plated aluminium, a bimetallic copper-clad aluminium transition piece, or a bimetal connector. See our pages on tin-plated busbar and copper-clad aluminium busbar.

Processing

Cutting, drilling, bending and welding busbar

Busbar is normally delivered cut to length, and much of it is punched, drilled and bent to a drawing before plating or insulation. A few material-specific rules prevent rejected parts.

  • Bending: 1350 and over-aged 6101 tempers bend most easily; 6101-T6, 6061-T6 and 6082-T6 need larger radii and can crack on the outside of tight edgewise bends. Bend across the extrusion direction where possible and keep holes away from the bend zone.
  • Punching and drilling: deburr all holes; burrs reduce contact area and damage insulation. Slotted holes accommodate expansion on long runs.
  • Welding: TIG and MIG welding are used for permanent joints and fabricated assemblies. Heat lowers the strength of heat-treated tempers locally, and filler alloy affects joint conductivity, so welded joints are designed with this in mind.
  • Friction-stir and explosion bonding: used for aluminium-to-copper transitions and for joining heavy conductors with low added resistance.
  • Edge finish: radiused edges reduce local electric field strength and help coatings cover evenly.
  • Flexible links: laminated foil or braided connectors take up thermal movement and vibration between rigid bars and equipment terminals.
Where it is used

Applications of aluminium busbar

Switchgear and panels

Main and distribution bars in low- and medium-voltage switchboards, motor control centres and panel boards, usually 6101 or 1350 flat bar.

Bus duct and busway

Insulated aluminium bars inside sandwich or ventilated busway systems that distribute power through buildings and factories.

Transformers and reactors

Lead-outs, terminal bars and connections in distribution and dry-type transformers, often matched with aluminium foil or strip windings.

Battery and energy storage

Cell and module interconnects, string bars and rack bars where weight and cost per ampere matter; see battery busbar.

Electrolysis and smelting

Very large plate-cut conductors carrying tens of thousands of amperes in rectifier and electrolytic plants.

Earthing and bonding

Ground bars and equipotential bonding systems in substations, data centres and telecom sites; see ground busbar.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) busbar: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium alloy is best for busbar?

1350 (EC grade) has the highest conductivity, at least 61 % IACS, and is best where the bar is well supported and bent often. 6101 in T6 or T61 is the usual choice for switchgear and bus duct because it is much stronger and resists creep at bolted joints, with conductivity of roughly 55 – 59 % IACS.

How much bigger must an aluminium busbar be than copper?

For equal DC resistance, about 1.64 times the copper cross-section in 1350 and about 1.8 times in 6101-T6. Because aluminium is less than a third as dense, the larger bar still weighs only about half as much.

What is the current rating of a 50 × 5 mm aluminium busbar?

It depends on ambient temperature, permitted rise, enclosure and spacing, so no single figure is correct. Use a recognised table such as DIN 43670 for your installation, then verify by test or calculation to IEC 61439. Its DC resistance in 1350 is about 113 µΩ per metre at 20 °C.

Can aluminium busbar be connected directly to copper?

Not bare in damp or outdoor conditions, because galvanic corrosion attacks the aluminium. Use tin-plated aluminium contact areas, a copper-clad aluminium transition piece or a bimetal connector, plus joint compound and spring washers.

Why do aluminium busbar joints need spring washers?

Aluminium expands more than steel bolts and creeps slowly under pressure. Conical spring washers keep the clamp force steady through heating and cooling, so contact resistance stays low.

Do you supply drilled, bent or plated busbar?

Yes. Metline supplies busbar cut to length and processed to drawing, including punching, drilling, bending, tin plating and insulating coatings, with mill test certificates, for delivery worldwide.
