High-Power Shielded Aluminium Busbar for EV High-Voltage Systems

A shielded aluminium busbar is a rigid high-voltage conductor built in three layers: a solid aluminium core that carries the current, an insulation layer, and an outer aluminium shield that contains electromagnetic interference and protects the part mechanically. It replaces heavy shielded copper cable between battery, distribution box, inverter and fast-charging inlet. This guide explains the structure, material choices, design rules and tests, and the aluminium materials Metline supplies for it.

Overview

What is a shielded aluminium busbar and why use one?

Electric vehicles move hundreds of amperes at 400 V or 800 V between the battery pack, the junction or disconnect unit, the power distribution unit, the inverter and the charging inlet. Traditionally this is done with large shielded copper cables. A shielded aluminium busbar does the same job with a formed, solid aluminium conductor inside an integrated shield, so it holds its shape, packages tightly against the battery or body, and weighs much less.

The weight saving is straightforward physics. Aluminium conducts about 61 % as well as annealed copper by volume, so for the same resistance it needs roughly 1.6 times the cross-section. But it is only 30 % as dense (2.70 versus 8.9 g/cm³), so the aluminium conductor ends up at about half the mass of the copper one. Over several metres of high-voltage routing, the difference is measurable in kilograms.

A rigid busbar also removes the uncertainty of cable routing. It is bent to a fixed 3D path, clipped at defined points and terminated with machined or welded lugs, so creepage, clearance and bend radii are designed once and repeated on every vehicle.

Functions of the three layers

  • Solid aluminium conductor: carries continuous and peak current with a controlled temperature rise
  • Insulation layer: isolates the conductor from the grounded shield and withstands test voltage
  • Aluminium shield: contains electromagnetic emissions from switching currents and provides abrasion and crush protection
  • Together: a self-supporting, formable high-voltage link that replaces cable, braid and protective conduit

The choice between 1060 and 6101 is the most important conductor decision. 1060 gives the lowest resistance and bends easily, but it is soft, so terminals need careful design to avoid creep and loosening under clamping force. 6101 in a T6x temper accepts bolted joints and vibration better and keeps a formed shape, at a small conductivity penalty. The 6101-T64 and T65 tempers are a common compromise because they keep conductivity high while adding useful strength.

Conductivity values are standard minimums from ASTM B317 (6101) and typical values for 1060; the mill certificate reports the measured figure for each lot.

Geometry

Round or flat shielded busbar?

Flat / rectangular

Low profile, so it fits along battery trays, under floors and between modules where height is limited. A wide, thin conductor also has more surface area for heat dissipation and lower AC resistance from skin effect. Bending is easiest about the thin axis; edgewise bends need larger radii.

Round

Bends equally well in every direction, so it suits complex 3D paths with frequent changes of direction. A round shield is also simple to make continuous and to terminate with standard sealing and shielding rings.

How to decide

Start from the routing envelope. If height is the constraint and the path is mostly planar, choose flat. If the path twists through the chassis, choose round. Then size the cross-section from current, temperature rise and short-circuit duty.

Whichever shape you choose, keep the conductor solid rather than stranded where the route allows. A solid aluminium core has no interstitial air gaps, so it conducts heat outwards to the insulation and shield more evenly than a stranded cable of the same area, and it avoids the crimping problems that stranded aluminium can suffer at terminals. Stranded or laminated flexible sections are then used only where movement must be absorbed, for example between a battery pack and a body-mounted component, and joined to the rigid busbar by welding.

Architecture

Single-core or integrated dual-core?

AspectSingle-coreIntegrated dual-core
Conductor layoutPositive and negative routed as separate busbarsPositive and negative inside one shielded body
RoutingTwo paths, more flexibleOne path, more compact
SpaceNeeds room for two runs and their clearancesBetter for tight packaging
EMCEach conductor shielded individuallyClose pairing reduces the loop area, which helps emissions
Parts countTwo sets of clips, seals and terminationsFewer clips and seals; less insulation material
Best forPlatform variants with differing layoutsHigh-volume platforms with fixed packaging

Dual-core designs need careful attention to insulation between conductors, because the full system voltage now sits across a thin internal barrier, and to heat, because two current-carrying conductors share one shield. Single-core designs are easier to service and to adapt between vehicle variants.

Design

Sizing and design considerations

  • Current and temperature rise: size the conductor so that the continuous current gives an acceptable temperature rise at the highest ambient in the pack or underbody, and check peak current during fast charging or acceleration over its actual duration.
  • Short-circuit withstand: the conductor must survive the fault current until the fuse or pyro-disconnect opens, without softening the insulation.
  • Bend radius: specify minimum inside bend radii as a multiple of conductor diameter or thickness; too tight a bend cracks the shield, thins the insulation or work-hardens soft conductors.
  • Shield continuity: the shield must be bonded to the housing at both ends with low-impedance connections. A gap in the shield at a termination undoes its benefit.
  • Terminations: aluminium oxide and galvanic contact with copper must be managed: use tin- or nickel-plated contact areas, bimetallic transitions or friction or ultrasonic welding, and design bolted joints for aluminium creep.
  • Temperature class: automotive high-voltage parts are commonly specified across roughly −40 °C to 125 °C or 150 °C; the insulation, not the aluminium, usually sets the upper limit.
  • Vibration and fixing: rigid busbars need clips at defined spacings so that resonance and fretting at terminals do not occur.

Exact figures, such as a minimum shielding effectiveness in dB across a frequency band, a maximum transfer impedance in mΩ/m or a withstand voltage, come from the vehicle maker's specification or from standards such as ISO 19642 and LV 216 for high-voltage conductors. Write them on the drawing; they decide shield wall thickness and insulation thickness more than any other factor.

Applications

Where shielded aluminium busbars are used

Battery pack to distribution

Main positive and negative links between modules, the battery junction box (BJB) and the battery disconnect unit (BDU).

Power distribution unit

Short, rigid links from the PDU to the inverter, DC-DC converter and auxiliary loads.

Fast-charging path

High-current runs from the charging inlet to the battery, where peak current and temperature rise are highest.

Inverter and e-axle

Connections to the traction inverter where switching noise makes shielding essential.

Energy storage and buses

Stationary storage racks, electric buses and trucks, where long high-current runs make the weight saving larger.

Rail and marine electrification

Traction and hotel-power links in vehicles that need compact, robust high-voltage routing.

FAQ

Shielded aluminium (aluminum) busbar: frequently asked questions

Why use an aluminium conductor instead of copper cable?

For the same electrical resistance an aluminium conductor needs about 1.6 times the cross-section of copper but weighs roughly half as much. As a rigid busbar it also holds a fixed route, packages tightly and reduces clips, conduit and assembly variation.

Should the conductor be 1060 or 6101?

1060 gives slightly higher conductivity and bends easily but is soft at bolted joints. 6101 in T6x tempers is stronger, resists creep and vibration better and keeps its formed shape, with conductivity minimums of 55 – 59.5 % IACS depending on temper.

Can a shielded aluminium busbar carry 800 A?

Peak current capability depends on cross-section, duration, cooling and allowed temperature rise, not on the material alone. Busbars for fast charging are routinely sized for several hundred amperes; confirm by calculation and a temperature-rise test at your duty cycle.

Why is the shield made of aluminium?

An aluminium alloy such as 3003 conducts well enough to give effective shielding, forms easily around the insulation, resists corrosion and keeps the whole assembly light. It also protects the insulation against abrasion and crushing.

Can shielded busbars be bent after assembly?

Yes, within defined minimum bend radii. Too tight a bend can crack the shield or thin the insulation, so bends are designed with the conductor temper, insulation thickness and shield wall in mind and validated with a withstand test afterwards.
