Aerospace materials guide

Aluminium Alloys for Aerospace Components

Aerospace components such as frames, spars, landing-gear fittings, window frames, intake panels and propeller hubs are machined from aluminium forgings, thick plate and extrusions in a small family of alloys: 7050, 7075, 2024, 2014, 2618, 2219 and 6061. Each is chosen for a particular balance of strength, fracture toughness, stress-corrosion resistance and temperature capability, and bought to SAE AMS specifications. This guide maps components to alloys, tempers, product forms and standards for engineers and buyers.

Overview

Why aluminium still dominates many aircraft parts

Composites now make up much of the skin of the newest wide-body airliners, but aluminium remains the main material for most of the world's fleet and for a large share of internal structure, fittings and systems on every aircraft. It is light, well understood, easy to machine and inspect, repairable and comparatively inexpensive.

Aerospace components fall into a few groups. Primary structure (frames, spars, ribs, bulkheads, wing and fuselage fittings) carries flight loads and must resist fatigue and crack growth. Landing-gear and engine-mount fittings see high static loads and need stress-corrosion resistance. Engine-adjacent parts such as propeller hubs, intake components and compressor housings need strength at elevated temperature. Secondary structure, ducts and ground equipment need weldability or formability more than peak strength.

Higher-temperature engine parts, such as compressor discs and turbine components, are made from titanium and nickel alloys rather than aluminium. Landing-gear cylinders are typically high-strength steel. This guide focuses on the aluminium parts, which Metline supplies as certified raw material: forgings, plate, bar and extrusions for machining by component makers.

What aerospace aluminium must prove

  • Strength and fracture toughness in the direction the part is loaded
  • Resistance to stress-corrosion cracking and exfoliation, especially in the short-transverse direction
  • Freedom from internal defects, verified by ultrasonic inspection
  • Full traceability from melt to finished product, with certification to the AMS specification

Product forms

Forgings, plate and extrusions compared

Die forgings

Metal is forged in closed dies so the grain flow follows the part shape. This gives the best fatigue and stress-corrosion performance for complex fittings, but needs dedicated tooling, so it suits repeat production.

Hand (open-die) forgings

Forged between flat or simple dies into blocks, bars and rough shapes, then machined. T7452 and T7352 hand forgings are compression stress-relieved to keep machined parts stable.

Rolled rings

Seamless rings for engine casings, adapters and tank joints, forged and rolled to diameter.

Thick plate

Stretched T7451 or T651 plate up to about 150 – 200 mm is machined into frames and ribs; "buy-to-fly" ratios can exceed 10:1.

Extrusions

Seat tracks, stringers, floor beams and spar caps are extruded to near-final shape in T6511, T73511 or T76511 tempers.

Sheet and clad sheet

Alclad 2024-T3 and 7075-T6 sheet for skins, with a thin pure-aluminium layer for corrosion protection.

Quality

Inspection and certification of aerospace aluminium

  • Chemistry: melt analysis per heat, checked against the AMS or Aluminum Association limits
  • Mechanical tests: tensile tests in longitudinal, long-transverse and, for thick sections, short-transverse directions
  • Temper verification: electrical conductivity and hardness checks, especially for overaged 7xxx tempers
  • Stress-corrosion and exfoliation testing where the specification requires it
  • Ultrasonic inspection to the class stated on the order, typically class A or AA for thick plate and forgings
  • Dimensional and surface inspection, and grain-flow or macro-etch checks on forgings
  • Certification: EN 10204 3.1 or AMS-compliant certificates traceable to heat and lot

Traceability is as important as the test results. Every plate, bar or forging should be marked so it can be traced back to its heat, lot and certificate throughout machining.

Selection

Practical selection advice and common mistakes

MistakeBetter practice
Specifying 7075-T6 for a thick fitting loaded through its thicknessUse 7050-T74/T7452 or 7075-T73, which resist stress-corrosion cracking in the short-transverse direction
Machining thick plate in unstretched tempersUse T7451 or T651 stretched plate, or T7452 compression-stressed forgings, to limit distortion
Ordering by alloy onlyState alloy, temper, AMS specification, dimensions, ultrasonic class and any customer specifications
Ignoring grain directionPlace the highest loads along the longitudinal grain; ask for test results in the critical direction
Choosing 7xxx for parts above about 120 °CUse 2618-T61 or 2219 where sustained elevated temperature is expected

  2. Choose alloy and temperBalance strength, toughness and stress-corrosion needs using the table above.
  3. Choose the formDie forging for repeat complex parts, hand forging or plate for prototypes and large machined parts, extrusion for long constant sections.
  4. Specify certificationAMS specification, ultrasonic class, test directions and documentation.
  5. Quote and shipDelivery on EXW, FOB, CIF or DAP terms, with export packing that protects machined or sensitive surfaces.

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) for aerospace components: frequently asked questions

What is the most common aluminium alloy in aircraft structure?

7050 and 7075 dominate high-strength structure and fittings, while 2024 remains the standard for fatigue-critical skins and tension structure. 6061 is used for secondary structure and ground equipment.

Why is 7050 preferred over 7075 for thick forgings?

7050 keeps high strength and toughness through thick sections and, in T74/T7451/T7452 tempers, resists stress-corrosion cracking much better than 7075-T6. That makes it the usual choice for frames, bulkheads and large fittings.

Which aluminium alloy is used for propeller hubs?

2618-T61 is commonly used because it keeps its strength at elevated temperatures better than 7xxx alloys. It is supplied as die or hand forgings to AMS 4132.

What is the difference between a die forging and a hand forging?

A die forging is shaped in closed dies to near the final geometry, with grain flow following the part. A hand forging is formed between open dies into a simple shape and then machined. Die forgings suit repeat production; hand forgings suit prototypes, large parts and low volumes.

What ultrasonic class do aerospace forgings need?

It depends on the part and the customer specification. Class A or AA to AMS-STD-2154 is common for thick plate and critical forgings; the class must be stated on the order.
