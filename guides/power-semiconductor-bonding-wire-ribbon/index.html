Heavy Bonding Wire & Ribbon for Power Semiconductor Packaging

In IGBT, power MOSFET and SiC modules, the topside of each die is connected by heavy wire (round, roughly 100–500 µm) or bonding ribbon (flat, roughly 0.5–2 mm wide), wedge bonded ultrasonically. This guide explains how to choose between wire and ribbon, why aluminium remains the reference material, what changes if a design moves to copper, and how to qualify a new or second-source aluminium interconnect supplied by Metline.

Overview

The topside interconnect in a power device

A power semiconductor die is soldered or sintered to a substrate on its underside. Its topside, a thin aluminium-based metallisation over the source or emitter, must also be connected to the substrate tracks, terminals or neighbouring dies. In most power modules that connection is made by heavy bonding wire or bonding ribbon.

The current path runs from the power die, through its topside metallisation, into the wire or ribbon, and onwards to the substrate copper, a lead frame or a module terminal. The interconnect therefore has three jobs. It must carry tens to hundreds of amperes with low resistance and modest self-heating; it must be bonded without damaging the thin metallisation and the active cells beneath it; and it must survive tens of thousands to millions of thermal and power cycles in service.

Heavy-wire and ribbon bonding uses ultrasonic wedge bonding: a tool presses the conductor onto the pad and vibrates it at ultrasonic frequency, typically at room temperature, so that oxide films break up and the metals form a solid-state weld. This is a different technology from the fine-wire ball bonding (gold, copper, silver or AlSi1 wire of 15–50 µm) used in ICs and LEDs, and from clip and copper-pillar interconnects used in some advanced packages.

What drives interconnect choice

  • Current per die and the number of parallel conductors that fit on the pad
  • Topside metallisation thickness and what bonding force it can tolerate
  • Power-cycling life required by the application (industrial, traction, renewable)
  • Existing bonder, wedge tool and qualified process window
  • Whether the project is a second source, a cost change or a new platform
Geometry

Heavy wire vs bonding ribbon

The first decision is geometry. Heavy wire is round and specified by diameter; ribbon is rectangular and specified by width × thickness. A ribbon of the same cross-section as several wires sits flatter, needs fewer bonds and leaves a lower loop, which helps with package height and gel encapsulation.

In practice, layout decides more often than area. A die with a long, narrow source pad may suit two or three ribbons better than eight wires, while a die surrounded by gate pads, sensors and other wires is usually easier to connect with round wire. If you are considering a change from wire to ribbon, check the pad geometry, the clearance to neighbouring bonds and the path to the substrate before comparing material prices.

Aluminium route

Aluminium heavy wire and ribbon

Aluminium is the established material for power-device topside bonding. It bonds readily to aluminium die metallisation at low force, it is soft enough not to crack the die, and decades of field data exist for it. Metline supplies aluminium-based heavy wire and ribbon in the following families:

High-purity aluminium

99.99 % or 99.999 % aluminium, very soft and easy to bond. The classic choice for heavy wire on IGBTs and diodes.

Doped aluminium

High-purity aluminium with small controlled additions (for example nickel) that improve corrosion resistance and stabilise the grain structure during processing and service.

Alloyed, reliability-enhanced wire

Aluminium alloys designed to resist recrystallisation and grain growth in power cycling, extending life at higher junction temperatures.

Aluminium ribbon

High-purity or doped aluminium rolled to a flat rectangular section with controlled edges, for ribbon bonders.

Copper-clad aluminium

Aluminium core with a copper sheath, explored as an intermediate step that keeps aluminium-like bonding with improved conductivity and cycling behaviour.

Spooling and packing

Wire and ribbon wound on spools matched to the bonder, with clean, controlled winding and protective packing.

Each wire is characterised by its breaking load, elongation, diameter or section tolerance and surface condition. These properties set the bonding window. Two wires of the same nominal diameter but different hardness or elongation will not bond identically, which is why the supplier datasheet must be compared line by line during a second-source evaluation.

Material comparison

Aluminium vs copper: a system change, not a swap

Copper is increasingly used on new high-performance modules, especially SiC, because of its lower resistivity and higher fatigue resistance. Metline supplies the aluminium route only; the comparison below is to help you decide whether an aluminium design can be kept, or whether a copper-based redesign is justified.

PropertyAluminiumCopper
Electrical resistivity (20 °C)About 2.7 µΩ·cmAbout 1.7 µΩ·cm
Thermal conductivityAbout 235 W/m·KAbout 400 W/m·K
Thermal expansionAbout 23 ppm/KAbout 17 ppm/K
Hardness as bondedLowMuch higher; needs more force and energy
Typical die metallisationStandard aluminium-based padUsually needs a thicker or copper-based topside layer, or a buffer
Process maturityVery mature, widely qualifiedNewer; equipment and tool changes needed
CorrosionGood with suitable encapsulationNeeds protection against oxidation

Moving from aluminium to copper is therefore not a same-size conductor substitution. It changes bonding force, ultrasonic power, tool wear, die metallisation and often the substrate finish. For an existing, qualified aluminium product, the lower-risk route is usually to keep aluminium and, if more life is needed, evaluate a reliability-enhanced aluminium wire or ribbon first. SiC does not automatically require copper, and ribbon is not automatically better than wire; the right answer depends on the package.

Reliability

Initial bond quality is not long-term reliability

A bond that passes pull and shear tests on day one can still fail in the field. Each power cycle heats the die and wire, and because aluminium expands about nine times more than silicon, the bond interface and the wire heel are strained on every cycle. Over thousands of cycles this causes:

  • Heel cracking: fatigue cracks at the thinned section where the wire leaves the bond
  • Bond lift-off: cracks that grow along the interface between wire and metallisation until the bond detaches
  • Metallisation reconstruction: the thin aluminium pad roughens and degrades, increasing resistance
  • Wire recrystallisation: grain growth in pure aluminium at high temperature, which speeds crack growth
Validation levelQuestion answeredTypical method
Initial bond qualityIs the bond geometry and interface correct?Visual, cross-section, bond deformation
Mechanical bond testDoes the bond meet initial strength requirements?Wire pull (e.g. MIL-STD-883 method 2011), bond shear (e.g. JESD22-B116)
Package-level reliabilityDoes the interconnect survive the mission profile?Power cycling and thermal cycling, e.g. to JESD22-A122 or AQG 324 for automotive modules

Only package-level testing under the project’s own conditions can confirm a material change. The base properties of the wire are an input to that test, not a substitute for it.

Qualification

From engineering sample to approved source

  1. Specification reviewCompare the current material datasheet (alloy, diameter or section, breaking load, elongation, spool) with candidate products.
  2. Engineering samplesBond samples on your production bonder and tool, with your devices and substrates.
  3. Process and mechanical validationEstablish the process window and confirm pull, shear and cross-section results.
  4. Reliability validationRun the power-cycling or thermal-cycling tests your product requires.
  5. Multi-lot evaluationRepeat on several material lots to confirm lot-to-lot consistency.
  6. Customer qualificationRelease under your change-control process, with the agreed certificate of conformance and lot data for each delivery.

FAQ

Heavy aluminium (aluminum) bonding wire and ribbon: FAQ

Can you match our current heavy wire or ribbon specification?

Usually, yes. Send the datasheet or the alloy, diameter or width × thickness, breaking load, elongation and spool format. We propose the closest aluminium candidates and supply samples for bonding trials.

Do bonding parameters change with a new second source?

They can. Even with the same alloy and size, small differences in hardness, surface and winding shift the optimum force, ultrasonic power and time. Re-verify the process window on your bonder before release.

Can production start after sample bonding and pull/shear tests pass?

Not on that basis alone. Pull and shear confirm initial bond quality; long-term reliability needs power-cycling or thermal-cycling tests and a multi-lot check, as your qualification plan requires.

What changes when moving from aluminium to copper?

Bond force and energy rise, tooling wears faster, and the die usually needs a thicker or copper-based topside metallisation or buffer. It is a system redesign rather than a material swap. Metline supplies the aluminium route.

Is ribbon always better than heavy wire?

No. Ribbon reduces the number of bonds and loop height and can improve temperature-cycling life, but needs a ribbon-capable bonder and a layout with suitable pads. Many products remain best served by multi-wire layouts.

How is lot-to-lot risk controlled with a new supplier?

By evaluating several lots during qualification, agreeing a datasheet with tolerance limits, and receiving lot test data (breaking load, elongation, dimensions) with every delivery.
