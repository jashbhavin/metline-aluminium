Cleanroom materials guide

Clean Room Aluminium Profiles

Clean room aluminium profiles are the extruded sections that frame and seal modular cleanroom walls and ceilings: radiused coving, H and U panel joiners, ceiling grid, door and window frames, and floor skirting. Made in 6063 or 6061 and anodised or powder coated, they give flush, crevice-free junctions that are easy to clean and compatible with sandwich panels. This guide explains each profile group, material and finish choices, cleanroom standards and how to specify extrusions for a project.

Overview

What clean room profiles do

A modular cleanroom is built from insulated sandwich panels for walls and ceilings. The panels themselves are flat and smooth; it is at the joints, corners and edges that dirt, microbes and particles collect. Aluminium profiles are the parts that close those joints.

Every junction in a cleanroom has to meet three requirements. It must be flush or radiused, so there are no ledges or crevices for particles to settle and no sharp corners that a cleaning mop cannot reach. It must be sealed, so that pressure cascades between rooms are maintained and air leaks through the envelope are minimal. And it must survive repeated cleaning with detergents, alcohols and sporicidal disinfectants without corroding, chalking or shedding particles.

Extruded aluminium meets these requirements well. Complex shapes with clip-in gasket grooves, radiused faces and hidden fixing channels can be made in one extrusion, cut accurately to length and finished with a hard, non-porous anodic or powder coating. The profiles are light enough to handle in occupied buildings and are fixed mechanically, so most installation is dry and low-dust.

Profiles are usually supplied as part of a panel system designed by a cleanroom contractor, but many contractors and panel manufacturers buy the extrusions separately so they can control cost, finish and delivery. This guide is written for those buyers: it explains what each profile has to do so that the extrusion can be specified correctly.

In practice many cleanrooms combine materials: aluminium for the wall and ceiling framing, coving and door frames; stainless steel for sinks, pass-through boxes and areas washed with chlorine-based agents; and uPVC only for low-specification trims.

All profile groups must be dimensioned around the panel system: panel thickness, slot width at the panel edge and gasket series. The most common mistake on cleanroom projects is ordering profiles before the panel supplier has confirmed the edge detail.

Fixed and demountable walls need different joiners. In a fixed wall, a flush H-joiner is bonded or sealed into the panel edges and is not meant to come apart, which gives the cleanest face. In a demountable partition, an I-joiner or split post is screwed together so panels can be removed to bring in large equipment or to reconfigure the room later. Laboratories and contract manufacturers often choose demountable joiners in at least one wall of each room for this reason.

Door and window frames deserve particular attention. A cleanroom door frame must sit flush with the wall on both faces, carry the door hinges and closer without distortion, and seal against the leaf on all four sides. Vision panels are normally double glazed and flush on both sides, with the aluminium frame holding the glass in a sealed, glazing-bead-free detail so there is no ledge for dust.

Pre-cut and pre-drilled profiles are worth specifying on cleanroom projects in existing buildings. Every site cut releases aluminium swarf and dust, and every cut end exposes bare metal that should be sealed. Cutting and drilling under factory conditions keeps installation cleaner and faster.

Section design also affects cost. Coving and joiner profiles are usually light, with walls around 1.0 – 1.5 mm, while ceiling beams and door frames are heavier and may need 2 – 3 mm walls or hollow chambers. Asking for the weight per metre of each profile on the quotation makes it easy to compare offers on a like-for-like basis and to spot sections that have been lightened.

Finishes

Finishes and resistance to cleaning agents

Anodised (clear or bronze)

A hard, integral oxide layer, commonly 15 – 25 µm and sealed. Does not peel or chip, is non-porous after sealing and resists alcohols and most neutral detergents. The usual default for cleanroom framing.

Powder coated

Usually white or light grey to match panels, about 60 – 80 µm. Gives a uniform colour across profiles and panels and good resistance to many disinfectants; chipped areas must be repaired.

Antimicrobial coatings

Powder or anodic systems with antimicrobial additives are available for hospital and food areas; they supplement, not replace, cleaning.

ESD / static-dissipative

For semiconductor and electronics rooms, profiles and panels can be specified with static-dissipative coatings and earthing details.

Colour control

Specify a colour standard and ΔE tolerance so profiles from different batches match panels and each other.

Chemical caution

Aluminium finishes are vulnerable to strong alkaline cleaners and long contact with concentrated chlorine. Check the cleaning regime and test coated samples with the actual agents.

Ceilings and seals

Ceiling grid, filter housings and air-tight sealing

The ceiling is the most heavily engineered part of a cleanroom envelope. It carries HEPA or ULPA filters, fan filter units, lighting, sprinklers and often the weight of maintenance staff working from above. It also has to be the most airtight surface, because the filtered supply air enters through it and any leak around a filter bypasses the filtration.

Two broad ceiling types exist. A non-walkable ceiling uses a suspended aluminium T-grid, hung on threaded rods from the building structure, with ceiling panels and filter modules laid in or clipped into the grid. A walkable ceiling uses self-supporting sandwich panels or deep aluminium beams that span between walls and can carry a person, which lets technicians reach services without entering the clean space.

Ceiling featureProfile requirementWhy it matters
Gel-seal filter gridChannel profile filled with a non-drying silicone gel; filter knife-edge sits in the gelVery low leakage around terminal filters; common in ISO 5 and better
Gasket-seal gridGrid flange with continuous gasket under the filter frameSimpler and cheaper; suitable for ISO 6 – 8 rooms
Fan filter unit (FFU) gridHeavier T-bar or box sections sized for FFU weight and vibrationLarge FFU ceilings in semiconductor fabs carry significant distributed load
Walkable panel supportWall-head angle or beam profile carrying the panel endsMust resist panel loads plus maintenance point loads without visible deflection
Light and sprinkler cut-outsFrames or trims around penetrationsEach penetration needs its own seal to keep the ceiling airtight

Grid spans and hanger spacing should come from a structural calculation that includes filter and FFU weights, any walkable or maintenance loads and a deflection limit appropriate for the seals. Light architectural T-bar is not a substitute for a cleanroom grid: excessive deflection opens gasket joints and breaks gel seals.

At wall-to-wall and wall-to-ceiling joints, sealing is achieved with extruded EPDM or silicone gaskets clipped into grooves in the aluminium profiles, followed where required by a bead of low-outgassing, cleanroom-grade silicone. The profile design must leave a uniform, accessible sealant joint; joints that are too narrow or too deep cannot be tooled smooth and become dirt traps themselves.

Quality

Inspection, packing and site delivery

Cleanroom profiles are visible, finished components, so surface quality matters as much as dimensions. Scratches, dents or coating damage during transport are difficult to repair on site without creating dust, and a damaged anodic film can only be touched up, not restored.

Recommended checks before dispatch

Dimensions
Critical dimensions such as slot width, gasket groove and radius checked against drawing
Straightness and twist
Within EN 12020-2 limits, so long profiles align without forcing
Coating

Cut lengths and holes

Mill test certificate EN 10204 3.1 for alloy and temper; coating report

For packing, specify protective film on visible faces, interleaving between profiles, and bundles labelled by room, elevation or drawing reference. Export shipments should be in sealed, seaworthy cases with desiccant to prevent condensation staining on long sea voyages.

ISO 14644-1 classifies cleanrooms by airborne particle concentration, and ISO 14644-4 covers design, construction and start-up. For sterile medicine manufacturing in Europe, Annex 1 of the EU GMP Guide adds requirements such as smooth, impervious, unbroken surfaces and the avoidance of recesses that are difficult to clean, which is exactly what coving and flush joiners provide.

The required particle class drives much of the detailing. An ISO 8 packaging room can accept gasket-sealed ceilings and simple square-corner trims in non-critical areas, provided joints are flush and airtight. An ISO 5 filling zone or a Grade A/B aseptic suite calls for continuous radiused coving, gel-sealed filters, flush-glazed vision panels and finishes validated against sporicidal cleaning. Matching the profile system to the actual classification avoids both under-specification and paying for features the room does not need.

Practical guidance

Design and installation checklist

  1. Fix the panel system firstConfirm panel thickness, edge slot and gasket series, then select or design profiles to suit.
  2. Standardise the radiusUse one coving radius throughout so corner pieces, door frames and floor coving line up.
  3. Plan the ceiling loadsList filter units, light fittings and whether the ceiling must be walkable; size the grid from these loads.
  4. Coordinate finishesMatch profile colour to panels and choose finishes tested against the planned cleaning agents.
  5. Order pre-cut where possibleReduce site cutting and dust; label profiles by room and elevation for faster installation.
  6. Seal and verifySeal joints with cleanroom-grade silicone or gaskets; leak-test and verify classification at commissioning.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing coving radii from different suppliers, leaving steps at corners
  • Exposed screw heads and open profile ends that trap dirt
  • Using light trim sections as ceiling grid where filter units or maintenance loads are carried
  • Choosing finishes without checking resistance to the site’s sporicidal agents

FAQ

Clean room aluminium (aluminum) profiles: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium alloy is used for cleanroom profiles?

6063 in T5 or T6 temper for coving, joiners, trims and frames, because it extrudes complex shapes with a smooth, anodisable surface. Load-bearing and walkable ceiling grids use 6061-T6 or heavy 6063-T6 sections.

Is anodised or powder-coated aluminium better for a cleanroom?

Anodising is harder and cannot peel, so it is the usual default. Powder coating gives an exact colour match to white panels. Either works if tested against the cleaning agents used on site.

What coving radius is used in cleanrooms?

There is no single mandated value, but around 50 mm is widely used because it is easy to clean and matches common floor coving. The key is using one radius consistently across the project.

Can aluminium profiles be used in GMP Grade A/B areas?

Yes, as part of a sealed, flush panel system with radiused corners and finishes resistant to the disinfectants used. Wet areas cleaned with chlorine may need stainless steel instead.

Can you match profiles from an existing cleanroom system?

Yes. Send drawings or samples and we can supply extrusions to the same section, either from existing dies where available or new dies.

How are clean room profiles fixed to the panels?

Most are mechanically fixed through concealed channels or clip into the panel edge, then sealed with gaskets or cleanroom-grade silicone. Exposed screw heads are avoided on the clean side because they trap dirt and are hard to disinfect.

Does Metline build or install cleanrooms?

No. Metline supplies the aluminium profiles, sheet and related materials to cleanroom contractors and panel manufacturers, who design, build and validate the rooms.
