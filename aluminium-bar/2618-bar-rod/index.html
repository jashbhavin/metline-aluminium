2618 / 2618A Aluminium Bar and Rod

2618 and 2618A are aluminium-copper-magnesium-nickel-iron alloys designed to keep their strength at 150 – 250 °C, where most aluminium alloys soften. As bar, rod and forging stock they are used for forged pistons, compressor wheels, engine parts and airframe components that run hot. Metline supplies 2618 and 2618A round, square, flat and hex bar in the T6 and T8 family of tempers, certified to ASTM, AMS or EN, to customers worldwide.

Overview

What is 2618 aluminium?

2618 traces back to the British Hiduminium RR alloys developed for aero engines. In the 1960s it became famous as the main airframe alloy of Concorde (in its French form AU2GN, today 2618A), chosen because the skin of a Mach 2 aircraft runs at well over 100 °C for hours at a time.

The key is the combination of about 1 % iron and 1 % nickel. Together they form hard, thermally stable Al9FeNi intermetallic particles that do not dissolve or coarsen at operating temperature. Copper and magnesium provide precipitation hardening in the usual way, while the iron-nickel phase pins the structure and resists creep. The result is an alloy that is slightly less strong than 2014 or 2024 at room temperature but holds its strength far better after long exposure at 150 – 250 °C.

Today the largest single use of 2618 bar is as forging stock for high-performance pistons in motorsport, aviation and heavy-duty engines. It is also machined directly into turbocharger and compressor wheels, impellers, engine brackets and fittings near heat sources. Because it is a forging alloy at heart, bar is supplied in both as-fabricated (F) condition for forging and fully heat-treated tempers for direct machining.

Why engineers specify 2618 / 2618A

  • Best retention of strength and creep resistance among common forging alloys at 150 – 250 °C
  • High toughness and resistance to thermal fatigue, making it tolerant of detonation and shock loads in pistons
  • Excellent hot workability for closed-die and open-die forging
  • Long service history in aerospace and engine components

Tempers

2618 tempers explained

F (as fabricated)

Extruded or rolled bar supplied for forging. The forger performs solution treatment and ageing after shaping the part. The most common condition for piston forging stock.

O (annealed)

Softened for machining or forming operations before full heat treatment.

T6 / T61

Solution treated, quenched and artificially aged. T61 uses a quench and ageing practice developed for forgings; it is the standard condition in AMS 4132.

T651 / T6511

T6 with stress relief by stretching, for rolled (T651) or extruded (T6511) bar that will be machined.

T851 / T852

Cold worked or stretched before artificial ageing, giving higher yield strength. T852 applies to forgings stress-relieved by compression.

Choosing a temper

Order F for forging stock, T6511 or T851 for machining directly from bar, and T61 when your drawing references forging specifications.

2618 is not used in T3 or T4 naturally aged conditions for service; its benefits come from artificial ageing.

Working with 2618

Forging, heat treatment, machining and joining

Forging

Excellent hot workability. Forge in the region of 410 – 450 °C, keeping dies hot to avoid surface cracking. Grain flow should follow the part profile, especially in piston crowns and pin bosses.

Heat treatment

Solution treat at about 525 – 535 °C, quench (often in hot or boiling water for forgings to limit distortion), then age at around 190 – 200 °C. Follow AMS 2770 or the forging specification.

Machining

Machines well in aged tempers, with short chips and a good finish. Hard Al-Fe-Ni particles are abrasive, so carbide or PCD tools last much longer than HSS.

Welding

Not recommended for fusion welding; the alloy is prone to hot cracking. Use mechanical joining. Friction welding is possible in some designs.

Corrosion

Fair. Parts are usually anodised or coated for corrosive environments; pistons operate in oil and need no special protection.

Surface treatments

Hard anodising of ring grooves and skirt coatings are common on pistons to improve wear resistance.

Piston alloy choice

2618 versus 4032 for forged pistons

Forged piston makers mainly choose between 2618 and 4032. The decision comes down to the trade-off between toughness and thermal expansion.

Factor2618 / 2618A4032
Silicon contentVery low (≈0.2 %)High (≈12 %)
Thermal expansionHigher (≈22 – 23 µm/m·°C)Lower (≈19 – 20 µm/m·°C)
Piston-to-bore clearanceLarger; some cold-start noiseTighter; quieter running
Toughness and ductilityHigher; tolerant of detonation and boostLower; less forgiving of abuse
Strength at high temperatureExcellentGood
Wear resistanceGood (often coated)Very good (silicon particles)
Typical useRacing, turbo, nitrous and heavy-duty enginesStreet performance and endurance pistons

In short, 2618 is the choice when the engine is pushed hard and reliability under extreme loads matters more than noise; 4032 suits engines that need tight clearances and long, quiet service.

Applications

Where 2618 bar and forgings are used

  • Engines: forged pistons for motorsport, aviation, marine and heavy-duty diesel engines
  • Turbomachinery: compressor wheels, impellers and blower rotors
  • Aerospace: airframe skins and structure for supersonic aircraft, engine mounts and hot-section fittings
  • Automotive: connecting rods, cylinder head and valvetrain parts in performance engines
  • Defence: parts that must retain strength after heat exposure
  • Industrial: fittings and machine parts operating continuously at 150 – 230 °C

FAQ

2618 aluminium (aluminum) bar: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between 2618 and 2618A?

2618 is the Aluminum Association / ASTM registration; 2618A is the European EN 573-3 version (AlCu2Mg1,5Ni) with slightly wider limits on copper, nickel, iron and titanium plus a manganese limit. They behave almost identically and are frequently dual-certified.

Why is 2618 used for racing pistons?

It keeps strength at high temperature, resists creep and is tough enough to survive detonation and high boost. Its higher thermal expansion means larger cold clearances, which racing engines accept.

What temperature can 2618 aluminium handle?

It is designed for sustained service around 150 – 230 °C, with better strength retention in that range than 2014, 2024 or 7075. Above about 250 °C all aluminium alloys lose strength quickly.

Can 2618 be welded?

Fusion welding is not recommended because of hot-cracking sensitivity. Design for mechanical joints; for a weldable heat-resistant alloy, consider 2219.

Should I order 2618 bar in F or T6 temper?

Order F (as fabricated) if the bar will be forged, because the part is heat treated after forging. Order T6511, T651 or T851 if you will machine parts directly from bar.

What is the density of 2618?

About 2.76 g/cm³. A 100 mm round bar weighs roughly 21.7 kg per metre.
