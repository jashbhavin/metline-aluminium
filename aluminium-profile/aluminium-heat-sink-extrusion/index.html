Aluminium Heat Sink Extrusions

An extruded aluminium heat sink is a finned profile pushed through a die in one piece, then cut to length, so the base and fins form a continuous path for heat to flow from a component into the surrounding air. Metline supplies heat sink extrusions in 6063, 6060, 6061 and 1xxx alloys, as cut lengths or as fully machined and anodised parts, for power electronics, LED lighting, telecoms and industrial equipment worldwide.

Overview

Why heat sinks are extruded from aluminium

Every electronic device turns part of its power into heat. A heat sink spreads that heat out of a small, hot component and offers a large finned surface to the air, keeping the component below its maximum junction temperature. Extrusion is the most economical way to make that finned shape in volume.

Aluminium wins over copper for most heat sinks on balance rather than on conductivity alone. Copper conducts about twice as well (around 390 W/m·K against roughly 200 W/m·K for 6063), but it is more than three times as dense and considerably more expensive, and it cannot be extruded into thin, tall fins. For a given weight and cost, an aluminium extrusion usually removes more heat; copper is reserved for small, very high flux spots, often as an insert or base plate under an aluminium fin stack.

The extrusion process also suits thermal design. One die produces a profile of constant cross-section in long lengths, so a single tool can serve heat sinks of 30 mm or 300 mm simply by cutting to different lengths. Cooling performance can be tuned later by changing the cut length, adding a fan, or machining pockets and mounting holes without new tooling.

What an extruded heat sink gives you

  • One-piece base and fins with no thermal joint between them
  • Low tooling cost compared with die casting or bonded-fin assemblies
  • Any length from one die, so one profile can cover a product family
  • Easy machining, tapping and anodising for a finished, ready-to-fit part
Profile types

Types of extruded heat sink

Straight-fin (plate-fin)

Parallel fins rising from a flat base. The default design for power supplies, motor drives and board-level cooling, and the most efficient when air flows along the fins.

Slant or angled fin

Fins set at an angle to fit an enclosure, to direct airflow, or to give more fin length within a low height.

Radial / round (LED)

Fins radiating from a central core, used in downlights, spotlights and street lights. Works with air from any direction and suits natural convection when mounted vertically.

Pin-fin (cross-cut)

A straight-fin extrusion cross-cut by sawing or milling into an array of pins, so air can enter from any direction. Good in unpredictable airflow and in natural convection with horizontal mounting.

Enclosure heat sink

A profile that forms the housing of the electronics as well as the cooler, such as amplifier cases, inverter housings and LED drivers.

Large and wide profiles

Wide-base sections for IGBT modules, welders and inverters, often combined with fans or bonded-fin extensions where fins must be very tall.

Alloys and tempers

Alloys for heat sink extrusions

Thermal conductivity falls as alloying content rises, but so does extrudability for thin fins. 6063-T5 is the standard compromise: high conductivity, excellent extrudability, good anodising and enough strength for mounting.

Thermal design

How to size an extruded heat sink

Heat sink selection starts with a thermal budget. The total allowed thermal resistance from the component junction to the air is the temperature rise you can accept divided by the power dissipated. The heat sink must take whatever is left after the component package and the thermal interface material have used their share.

  1. Set the limitsMaximum junction or case temperature from the component datasheet, maximum ambient air temperature, and the power to be removed in watts.
  2. Work out the budgetRequired total resistance = (Tjunction − Tambient) ÷ power. Example: (110 − 50) °C ÷ 40 W = 1.5 °C/W.
  3. Subtract the lossesDeduct the junction-to-case resistance from the datasheet and the interface material resistance (pad, grease or phase-change). The rest is the heat sink target, for example 1.0 °C/W.
  4. Choose a profilePick a section whose published resistance per length meets the target in your airflow condition, natural or forced. Resistance does not halve when length doubles; extra length gives diminishing returns.
  5. VerifyConfirm with thermal simulation or a prototype test, especially in enclosures where airflow is uncertain.
Design factorNatural convectionForced air (fan)
Fin spacing (gap)Wider, roughly 6 – 12 mm, so warm air can rise freelyNarrower, roughly 2 – 5 mm, for more surface area
Fin orientationVertical fins, aligned with rising airAligned with the fan airflow
Surface finishAnodising matters: raises emissivity from about 0.05 – 0.1 to around 0.8Radiation is minor; finish chosen for protection and appearance
Base thicknessThicker bases spread heat from small sources across more finsSame; also leaves material for tapped mounting holes

Extrusion sets practical limits on fin geometry. The ratio of fin height to the gap between fins is the key one: conventional dies comfortably produce ratios of around 6:1 to 10:1, and specialised dies can go higher. Fins are normally 1 – 2 mm thick, with a slight taper towards the tip. Beyond these limits, bonded-fin, skived or brazed constructions are used instead.

Processing

Machining and finishing

Precision cutting

Saw cutting to length with deburred ends; tight length tolerances for automated assembly.

CNC machining

Mounting holes, tapped threads, pockets for components, fin removal for clearance, and flycutting of the base for flat contact.

Cross-cutting

Sawing or milling across the fins to make pin-fin heat sinks from a straight-fin profile.

Anodising

Clear or black anodising to MIL-A-8625 Type II, typically 10 – 25 µm. Improves corrosion resistance and emissivity; the film is an electrical insulator, so mask contact areas if grounding is needed.

Conversion coating

Trivalent (RoHS-compliant) chromate conversion to MIL-DTL-5541 for a conductive, corrosion-resistant surface or a primer base.

Assembly

Pressed-in studs, threaded inserts, clips, thermal pads and fan fixings fitted before delivery on request.

Flatness of the mounting surface is often the deciding quality point: a poorly flat base leaves air gaps that raise interface resistance more than any alloy choice. For power modules, specify a machined base with a flatness tolerance and surface roughness on the drawing.

Applications

Where extruded heat sinks are used

  • Power electronics: inverters, UPS systems, motor drives, welders and battery chargers using IGBTs and MOSFETs
  • LED lighting: street lights, high-bay and flood lights, downlights and linear fittings
  • Renewable energy: solar inverters and power-conversion cabinets
  • Electric vehicles: on-board chargers, DC/DC converters and charging stations
  • Telecoms and IT: base-station radios, network equipment and rack power supplies
  • Audio and consumer: amplifiers, enclosures and appliance controllers
  • Industrial control: PLC modules, servo amplifiers and cabinet electronics

FAQ

Aluminium (aluminum) heat sink extrusions: frequently asked questions

Which aluminium alloy is best for heat sinks?

6063-T5 for most extruded heat sinks. It conducts about 200 – 209 W/m·K, extrudes thin fins accurately and anodises well. Choose 6061-T6 only when the heat sink also carries structural loads, and 1050A when maximum conductivity matters more than strength.

Does black anodising improve heat sink performance?

In natural convection, yes: anodising raises surface emissivity from about 0.05 – 0.1 to around 0.8, which increases heat lost by radiation. The colour itself matters little at these temperatures. In fan-cooled designs the gain is small.

How long should my heat sink be?

Long enough to meet the thermal resistance target, but extra length gives diminishing returns because the far end of the profile runs cooler. Profile data sheets and a thermal check identify the useful length.

Can you supply heat sinks cut and machined?

Yes. Profiles can be cut to length, CNC machined, tapped, flycut flat, anodised or conversion-coated, and supplied ready to assemble.

Is copper better than aluminium for heat sinks?

Copper conducts about twice as well but is over three times heavier and much more expensive. Aluminium extrusions give better performance per kilogram and per unit cost for most applications.

Can a new heat sink profile be made?

Yes. Send the envelope, component layout, power and airflow. The section is reviewed for fin ratio and extrudability before the die is made, and samples can be supplied for testing.

Need a heat sink profile for your product?

Send the envelope, power, airflow and quantities, and we will propose a section, machining and finish with a delivered price.
